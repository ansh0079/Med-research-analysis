## Synopsis (gist)

Diabetes insipidus is a rare endocrine disease in which the kidney is unable to concentrate urine, either due to the lack of antidiuretic hormone or an inability to respond to antidiuretic hormone, leading to excess excretion of hypotonic urine. Central diabetes insipidus is characterized by deficient antidiuretic hormone secretion from the posterior pituitary, whereas nephrogenic diabetes insipidus is characterized by deficient V2 or aquaporin-2 response in the kidneys in the setting of appropriate antidiuretic hormone levels. The gold standard diagnostic test is an indirect water deprivation test, often followed by desmopressin to differentiate central from nephrogenic disease: an increase in urine osmolality greater than 50% is consistent with complete central diabetes insipidus, and no or minimal increase is consistent with complete nephrogenic diabetes insipidus. A mainstay of treatment, whether central or nephrogenic, is adequate access to water. Antidiuretic hormone replacement with desmopressin is the mainstay of treatment for central diabetes insipidus, while antidiuretic hormone replacement is not effective for patients with nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, although patients with partial nephrogenic diabetes insipidus may respond to supraphysiologic doses of desmopressin. Minimizing osmotic load is recommended in patients with nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, and acquired nephrogenic diabetes insipidus should be treated by reversing the underlying cause, if possible.
