## Synopsis (gist)

Morbilliform eruptions are a clinical reaction pattern of erythematous macules and papules that coalesce into patches covering most of the skin surface, and they are among the most common cutaneous findings in the inpatient setting. In hospital, most causes are benign low-risk drug exanthems, but the pattern may also indicate Stevens-Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis, drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms/drug-induced hypersensitivity syndrome, acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis, or graft-versus-host disease. The authors suggest differentiating high-risk, immediately visible features at the initial evaluation, including facial involvement, palm or sole involvement, mucosal involvement, and morphologic clues such as subtle duskiness, pustules, or vesicles or bullae. That risk stratification can be used to determine the need for hospitalization and additional laboratory or skin biopsy testing. Once a high-risk pattern is identified and severe cutaneous adverse reactions have been considered, evaluation of viral causes should be considered. It is not recommended for patients with SJS/TEN in this setting to undergo rechallenge.
