## Synopsis (gist)

Juvenile temporal arteritis is a rare non-granulomatous vasculitis affecting the superficial temporal arteries, mostly in individuals under 45 years old, and it is often misdiagnosed because of its benign nature and the absence of systemic symptoms. Key distinguishing factors from giant cell arteritis include a male predominance, onset in early adulthood, and an absence or minimal presence of symptoms such as polymyalgia, visual disturbances, malaise or fever. It typically presents as painless or painful temporal nodules, rarely accompanied by other nonspecific symptoms, making histopathological examination crucial for accurate diagnosis. Histopathological examination showing a lymphoeosinophilic infiltrate and the absence of giant cells is essential to differentiate juvenile temporal arteritis from other vasculitic and non-vasculitic disorders, and fibrinoid necrosis should prompt an alternative diagnosis. Patients frequently exhibit normal blood tests and may experience spontaneous remission, so clinical evaluation should avoid unnecessary interventions. Surgical procedures and corticosteroids are the primary treatment modalities, with occasional use of methotrexate based on anecdotal evidence.
