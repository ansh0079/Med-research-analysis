Objective To develop a practical, expert consensus for the management of acute asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) exacerbations in Saudi Arabia’s Emergency Departments (EDs), addressing gaps in triage, diagnostic certainty, and aerosol delivery. Methods A modified expert consensus process with Delphi elements was employed. Eight multidisciplinary experts in pulmonary medicine, emergency medicine, and respiratory therapy, three of whom were affiliated with the Saudi Society of Emergency Medicine (SASEM), two of whom are authors of the Saudi initiative for Asthma (SINA) Guidelines, and three of whom are authors of the Saudi COPD guidelines, reviewed evidence-based presentations and formulated structured discussion questions before the meeting. The advisory board session (Riyadh, November 19, 2025) served as the formal consensus round, addressing ten predefined clinical questions across two moderated debate sessions, with formal voting on two key device-selection statements. Results The panel adopted a dual-phase severity classification: a binary “Non-Severe vs. Severe” model for rapid ED triage, followed by the four-tier SINA classification for clinical management. Pharmacological algorithms were standardized, prioritizing early corticosteroid therapy and optimizing bronchodilator dosing to mitigate toxicity. The panel reached a unanimous consensus that aerosol delivery device selection significantly influences treatment success; A-VMN was recommended at escalating strengths across severity levels (Class IIb–I) and as a preferred option for severe and ventilated patients, based on superior lung deposition, infection control advantages, and improved discharge rates. Conclusion This modified expert consensus aims to provide an up-to-date, organized, and action-oriented protocol for Saudi EDs. It advocates a stratified triage system and evidence-based selection of aerosol-delivery devices to improve patient outcomes, enhance safety, and optimize resource utilization. In Saudi Arabia, the prevalence of self-reported clinical asthma diagnosis is estimated at 4.05%, while weighted pooled prevalence rates have been reported as high as 14.3% [ 1 , 2 ]. Asthma control remains a critical issue; the prevalence of uncontrolled asthma among adults ranges from 23.4% to 68.1%, and the SNAPSHOT program found that 44.2% of patients had uncontrolled asthma, which was associated with lower quality of life [ 3 , 4 ]. Additionally, 60.6% of asthma patients are overprescribed short-acting beta-agonists (SABAs; ≥3 canisters per year), a practice linked to poor clinical outcomes [ 5 ]. The burden of COPD is rising rapidly. Between 1990 and 2019, the total number of diagnosed COPD cases in the Kingdom increased by approximately 329.82%, from 101,104 to 434,560 individuals [ 5 ]. COPD prevalence in the population aged 40 years and older is 4.2%, rising to 14.2% among current or former smokers, and management complexity is increased by comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease, depression, and osteoporosis [ 6 ]. Both conditions carry substantial mortality and morbidity risk if not appropriately addressed in the ED; COPD epidemiology in the Gulf region remains limited, and the disease is frequently underdiagnosed. Distinguishing asthma from COPD guides appropriate intervention, critical for effective management [ 2 , 6 – 8 ]. Despite available guidelines (the Global Initiative for Asthma [GINA], Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease [GOLD], and Saudi Initiative for Asthma [SINA]), ED management remains variable, often complicated by diagnostic uncertainty and the unavailability of objective measures such as spirometry during acute distress [ 9 – 11 ]. Standardized assessment measures such as the Pediatric Respiratory Assessment Measure (PRAM) for severity grading and the Asthma Control Test (ACT) for evaluating asthma control are recommended to support clinical assessment [ 2 , 12 , 13 ]. The current four-tier severity classification is often impractical for rapid decision-making in high-volume EDs, and clinical stability is frequently hindered by suboptimal therapeutic delivery, a problem compounded by the limitations of traditional jet nebulizers, including low lung deposition efficiency, risk of infection transmission, and potential hyperoxia in COPD patients [ 14 – 17 ]. In-hospital mortality for COPD exacerbations can reach 12% to 25%, with a 35% readmission rate within three months; approximately 70% of readmissions are attributed to decompensation of comorbidities rather than respiratory failure alone, and gaps in care persist, including absent counselling for smoking cessation and limited pulmonary rehabilitation availability across the Kingdom [ 6 , 18 ]. This consensus aims to provide a unified, practical protocol for emergency physicians and pulmonologists in Saudi Arabia, standardizing pharmaceutical treatments and the role of advanced aerosol delivery systems to improve patient safety and clinical efficiency. Methods Panel selection The consensus panel comprised eight subject-matter experts selected for their clinical expertise and leadership at major Saudi healthcare institutions: four senior consultants and professors in emergency medicine, three leading consultants in pulmonary medicine from tertiary centers, and a respiratory therapy expert from Inaya Medical Colleges, Saudi Arabia. Institutions represented include Prince Sultan Military Medical City, King Faisal Specialist Hospital, King Saud University, Security Forces Hospital, and King Abdulaziz University Hospital. Two of the eight panelists are authors of the SINA Guidelines, and three are authors of the Saudi COPD guidelines. Moreover, three of them hold active membership and leadership positions within SASEM, ensuring that recommendations reflect national emergency medicine practice standards. Moreover, the expert panel included clinicians practicing in both major referral centers and smaller hospitals across different regions. This diversity of practice settings ensured that the recommendations were informed by a broad range of clinical experiences and were applicable across the Saudi healthcare system. Search strategy and evidence review Before the consensus meeting, a targeted literature search was conducted using PubMed and local databases to identify relevant clinical guidelines (SINA, GINA, GOLD), randomized controlled trials, and meta-analyses published between 2015 and 2025, focusing on “acute asthma,” “COPD exacerbation,” “aerosol delivery,” and “emergency department management.” To ensure thoroughness and transparency, final recommendations were graded along two independent dimensions following the standard framework used by international cardiovascular and respiratory societies (American College of Cardiology [ACC], American Heart Association [AHA], and European Society of Cardiology [ESC]). The Class of Recommendation, reflecting the strength of consensus among the panel, was assigned during the voting round as a panel-specific classification for this consensus process. These classes are not intended to represent formal guideline recommendations but rather reflect the level of agreement reached by the panel. The Class of Recommendation was defined as follows: Class I (evidence and/or general agreement that a given treatment or procedure is beneficial, useful, and effective; suggested wording “is recommended/is indicated”); Class II (conflicting evidence and/or a divergence of opinion about the usefulness or efficacy of the given treatment or procedure), further subdivided into Class IIa (weight of evidence/opinion is in favour of usefulness/efficacy; suggested wording “should be considered”) and Class IIb (usefulness/efficacy is less well established by evidence/opinion; suggested wording “may be considered”); and Class III (evidence or general agreement that the given treatment or procedure is not useful or effective, and in some cases may be harmful; suggested wording “is not recommended”). The Level of Evidence, reflecting the quality of the supporting literature identified during the pre-meeting evidence review, was assigned by the panel based on the body of evidence underpinning each recommendation and was defined as follows: Level A (data derived from multiple randomized clinical trials or meta-analyses), Level B (data derived from a single randomized clinical trial or non-randomized studies), and Level C (consensus of opinion of the experts and/or small studies, retrospective studies, or registries). Each recommendation in this consensus is therefore reported with both its Class (I, IIa, IIb, or III) and its Level of Evidence (A, B, or C). Consensus process A modified expert consensus process with Delphi elements was employed to ensure structured expert input. All panel members participated in the preparatory input phase: they reviewed evidence-based presentations covering the burden of asthma and COPD in Saudi Arabia, relevant SINA and GOLD guidelines, and published evidence on aerosol delivery technologies, and were directly involved in formulating the ten structured discussion questions guiding the meeting. The advisory board session (Riyadh, November 19, 2025) constituted the formal consensus round, employing moderated structured debate across two sessions: (1) Current Evidence and Practice Gaps and (2) The Clinical Value of Vibrating Mesh Nebulization. Consensus was primarily achieved through open expert discussion. Two specific statements related to device selection principles were subjected to formal panel voting, both achieving unanimous consensus: (1) “The choice of aerosol delivery device can significantly influence treatment success and the achievement of management goals,” and (2) “Evidence-based device selection is a fundamental clinical practice principle.” Where initial disagreement existed, the moderator facilitated discussion until a practical clinical compromise was reached. Results and recommendations Throughout the process, the expert panel addressed ten pre-defined key questions and established clinical directives to standardize acute respiratory care across Saudi Arabia’s EDs. Table 1 summarizes the primary consensus recommendations. Severity classification For asthma, the expert panel aligned with SINA, using a four-tier severity classification (Mild, Moderate, Severe, and Life-Threatening/Near-Fatal). However, the panel recognized the operational challenges of high-volume EDs and adopted a simplified binary model (Non-Severe vs. Severe) as a practical rapid triage tool to reduce cognitive load and prioritize physiological stabilization. While the binary model facilitates initial sorting, the four-tier stratification guides definitive clinical management: Mild: increased shortness of breath with stable vital signs. Moderate: increasing symptoms; peak expiratory flow (PEF) 50-75% predicted, without severe features. Severe: RR ≥ 25/min, HR ≥ 120/min, or inability to complete sentences in one breath. Life-Threatening/Near Fatal: SpO2 < 92%, confusion, silent chest, weak respiratory effort, or requiring mechanical ventilation. For COPD, exacerbation severity is graded separately according to GOLD criteria, which categorize exacerbations operationally by treatment intensity: mild (managed with short-acting bronchodilators alone), moderate (requiring short-acting bronchodilators plus antibiotics and/or oral corticosteroids), and severe (requiring ED visit or hospitalization), with associated respiratory failure further stratified as no respiratory failure, acute non–life-threatening respiratory failure, or acute life-threatening respiratory failure [ 6 , 9 ]. The Anthonisen criteria complement this scheme by characterizing exacerbations through three cardinal symptoms: increased dyspnea, increased sputum volume, and increased sputum purulence, and remain useful for therapeutic decision-making [ 19 ]. For binary triage at ED entry, applied to both asthma and COPD presentations, non-severe encompasses patients who can speak in sentences, are alert, RR < 25/min, HR < 120 bpm, and SpO2 ≥ 92%; Severe encompasses all others. Management protocols The panel endorsed a structured management algorithm stratifying care by severity tier (Fig. 1 ). The algorithm applies to both acute asthma and COPD exacerbations, with severity graded by the SINA four-tier scheme for asthma and the GOLD severity scheme for COPD as detailed in the Severity Classification section above. For mild cases, the focus is on symptom relief and optimizing bronchodilator delivery. Moderate cases require appropriate escalation of pharmacological therapy, while severe and life-threatening presentations mandate immediate intervention. Detailed, escalating recommendations for integrating aerosol delivery devices across these severity tiers are outlined in the Device Selection section (Fig. 1 ). Fig. 1 Adult acute asthma/COPD ED management algorithm with A-VMN recommendations The panel reaffirmed adherence to existing Ministry of Health (MoH) protocols for escalation of care (Table 2 ). For asthma, intensive care unit (ICU) admission is mandated for patients with a poor response (forced expiratory volume in 1 second [FEV1] or peak expiratory flow rate [PEFR] <50%) or who require nebulization more frequently than hourly [ 20 ]. The MoH Pocket Manual in Emergency identifies a normal partial pressure of carbon dioxide in acute asthma as a sign of worsening fatigue and a critical trigger for escalation [ 21 ]. For COPD, non-invasive ventilation (NIV) is the preferred method of ventilatory support when indicated; invasive mechanical ventilation is reserved for patients with NIV failure or intolerance [ 20 ]. Pharmacological management Short-acting beta-agonists (Salbutamol) combined with short-acting muscarinic antagonists (SAMAs; Ipratropium) remain the mainstay of initial therapy for both acute asthma and COPD exacerbations. The panel advises optimizing SABA dosing to avoid side effects. Although approximately 15% β-receptor stimulation is sufficient to achieve maximal bronchodilation, higher doses primarily increase the risk of tachycardia, elevated lactate, and hypokalemia [ 23 – 25 ]. The panel favored 2.5 mg every 20 minutes back-to-back over single 5 mg doses, or continuous nebulization (typically 10 mg over one hour) in severe cases [ 7 , 10 , 11 ]. Excessive cumulative SABA dosing should therefore be avoided, with adverse effects rather than a fixed milligram threshold guiding dose limitation. Regarding corticosteroids, early administration within the first hour improves outcomes in both asthma and COPD exacerbations [ 7 , 10 , 11 ]. For non-severe to moderate presentations, oral Prednisolone (40–50 mg) is preferred [ 7 , 10 ]. For severe and life-threatening exacerbations, intravenous (IV) formulations are recommended: IV Hydrocortisone (100–200 mg) or IV Methylprednisolone (80–125 mg). IV is also used in patients unable to swallow [ 7 , 10 ]. Across formulations, the recommended dose is approximately 0.5 mg/kg; doses exceeding 125 mg of Methylprednisolone are not supported by evidence of additional benefit; randomized trials have not demonstrated added benefit from higher corticosteroid doses with longer hospital and ICU stay and more frequent hyperglycemia [ 26 – 30 ]. Nebulized corticosteroids are not routinely recommended as an alternative to systemic corticosteroids for adults in the acute ED setting, as supporting evidence outside of paediatrics is limited [ 10 , 11 ]. For asthma patients, intravenous Magnesium Sulfate (2 g) is recommended as adjunctive therapy for severe acute cases failing initial bronchodilation [ 7 , 10 ]. For asthma patients with life-threatening presentations with impending respiratory failure, intramuscular (IM) Epinephrine or low-dose IV Ketamine may be considered [ 7 ]. For patients presenting with an acute exacerbation of COPD (AECOPD), treatment should be guided not only by exacerbation severity but also by clinical features suggesting bacterial infection. Increased sputum purulence is particularly useful for identifying patients who may benefit from antibiotic therapy. Antibiotics should be considered when patients have at least two cardinal symptoms, provided that one is increased sputum purulence, or when there is a prior positive sputum culture during a previous exacerbation. Antibiotics are also indicated in patients requiring invasive or non-invasive mechanical ventilation. In patients with frequent exacerbations, severe airflow obstruction, or those requiring mechanical ventilation, sputum cultures should be considered because of the increased likelihood of resistant or Gram-negative pathogens. When antibiotics are indicated, treatment should generally be limited to approximately 5 days, with selection guided by local antimicrobial resistance patterns and individual patient factors. These criteria provide a clinically practical approach to antibiotic stewardship while ensuring treatment of patients at higher risk of bacterial infection. Oxygen therapy in AECOPD should be controlled and titrated rather than administered indiscriminately. The panel recommends a target oxygen saturation (SpO₂) of 88–92% for patients with COPD exacerbations, with adjustment according to arterial blood gas results and clinical status. Excessive oxygen administration may worsen hypercapnia and respiratory acidosis in susceptible patients. Oxygen-driven nebulization should therefore be used cautiously in patients at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure, and the oxygen source used for aerosol delivery should not compromise the achievement of controlled oxygen targets [ 9 , 31 , 32 ]. Aerosol delivery device selection The panel unanimously agreed that the choice of aerosol delivery device affects treatment success. However, device selection should also consider availability, staff familiarity, cost, infection-control policies, oxygen delivery strategy, and patient- and interface-related factors. A-VMN is recommended as an effective alternative to traditional jet nebulizers, particularly for moderate to severe exacerbations, based on its ability to maximize lung deposition (3- to 6-fold higher)[ 33 , 34 ], measurably reduce ED length of stay and admission rates [ 35 , 36 ], provide infection control advantages for ventilated patients [ 14 , 37 ], and eliminate the need for oxygen/air driven jet nebulizers. Rapid improvements in airflow with VMN compared to jet nebulizer were demonstrated, supporting the potential clinical benefits of VMNs [ 38 ]. Due to this enhanced lung deposition, 2.5 mg of nebulized salbutamol delivered via A-VMN may provide bronchodilation comparable to that achieved with 5 mg via a standard jet nebulizer [ 33 – 35 ]. This deposition advantage allows the panel’s preferred SABA regimen: 2.5 mg every 20 minutes back-to-back, or continuous nebulization (typically 10 mg per hour) in severe cases [ 7 , 10 , 11 ], to be delivered at lower cumulative doses when administered via A-VMN, reducing the risk of tachycardia, elevated lactate, and hypokalemia [ 23 – 25 ]. Thus, A-VMN may be preferred, where available, particularly in clinical scenarios in which maintaining circuit integrity, minimizing infection risk, ensuring independence from an external oxygen source, or facilitating compatibility with NIV or mechanical ventilation is important. The panel endorsed the following escalating A-VMN recommendations by severity tier: Mild: Optimize bronchodilator delivery; A-VMN should be considered (Class IIb, Level of Evidence C). Moderate: Escalate to preferred A-VMN use (Class IIa, Level of Evidence B). Severe, Life-Threatening, and Ventilated (NIV or high-flow nasal cannula [HFNC]): Immediate resuscitation with A-VMN is recommended based on panel consensus (Class I, Level of Evidence B) to maximize lung deposition, reduce admission rates, and facilitate concurrent respiratory support without circuit disconnection. Although the panel favors the use of A-VMN, current guidelines continue to mention both nebulizers and metered-dose inhalers (MDIs) with or without a spacer (for COPD) or pressurized metered-dose inhalers (pMDIs) with a spacer (for asthma) as appropriate options for medication delivery during only mild/moderate exacerbations [ 39 , 40 ]. However, nebulizers (all types) may offer a simpler delivery approach for patients, in whom the use of inhaler devices may be limited by the severity of their condition. Respiratory support integration For patients requiring NIV or invasive mechanical ventilation, A-VMN should be placed within the inspiratory limb of the circuit, distal to the exhalation port (between the port and the patient mask) for NIV, and on the inspiratory limb of the ventilator circuit for invasive mechanical ventilation. Positioned this way, A-VMN delivers aerosol without requiring circuit disconnection, preserving positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) and alveolar recruitment, and improving infection control by preventing aerosol leakage at the patient interface [ 33 , 37 , 41 ]. For patients on HFNC, A-VMN should be placed at the humidifier inlet (dry side). Although high flow rates of 30–50 L/min could theoretically reduce aerosol deposition, available evidence shows that A-VMN still achieves adequate drug delivery (approximately 2.5 mg per dose) at HFNC flow rates encountered in routine ED use, with the closed delivery system preserving the deposition advantages observed in spontaneously breathing patients [ 35 , 42 ]. Discharge and follow-up For adults and adolescents discharged after an acute asthma exacerbation, an ICS-containing regimen is mandatory. Anti-inflammatory reliever therapy, where available, is a preferred option that aligns with current asthma guidelines (GINA) and local availability, but SABA-only treatment at discharge should be avoided. A 5- to 7-day course of oral corticosteroids could be considered. Follow-up referrals should be stratified by severity: moderate exacerbations to the primary care provider; severe exacerbations to a pulmonologist within 2 weeks to confirm diagnosis and adjust long-term management. For COPD patients, discharge planning should be considered an integral component of AECOPD management rather than a separate post-acute process. Before discharge, maintenance inhaled therapy should be reviewed and optimized according to the patient’s symptoms, exacerbation history, and treatment needs, with inhaler technique and adherence assessed and reinforced. Smoking status should be assessed and evidence-based smoking-cessation support initiated or reinforced for current smokers. Vaccination status should be reviewed and indicated respiratory vaccinations offered according to current national and international recommendations. Patients who have experienced significant functional limitation, particularly following hospitalization, should be assessed for referral to pulmonary rehabilitation where appropriate. The need for inhaled corticosteroid (ICS)-containing maintenance therapy should also be reassessed after the acute episode as part of longer-term COPD management. Blood eosinophil count can assist in identifying patients who are more likely to derive benefit from ICS, particularly when there is a history of recurrent exacerbations, while the potential risks of ICS therapy should also be considered. This assessment should be individualized rather than based on the eosinophil count alone. Where relevant, eosinophil-guided reassessment should therefore be incorporated into post-exacerbation treatment review rather than routinely initiating or continuing ICS solely because of an acute exacerbation. Early follow-up should be arranged after discharge to reassess symptoms, inhaler technique, adherence, smoking cessation progress, oxygen requirements where applicable, comorbidities, and the need for pulmonary rehabilitation. A structured discharge approach incorporating pharmacological optimization, non-pharmacological interventions, patient education, and timely follow-up may help reduce treatment failure and recurrent healthcare utilization. Discussion This consensus standardizes the acute management of asthma and COPD in Saudi Arabia, bridging high-level international guidelines (GINA, GOLD) and the practical realities of the ED by addressing specific operational challenges of the Saudi healthcare system. The simplification of severity classification can be considered the primary achievement of this work. While SINA and international guidelines provide comprehensive four-tier systems, these are often impractical in high-volume settings where diagnostic uncertainty is common. The binary ‘Non-Severe vs. Severe’ model serves specifically as a rapid triage tool to reduce cognitive load, ensuring high-risk patients are identified immediately; once stability is achieved, the standard four-tier SINA classification guides ongoing management. This two-step approach reflects evidence that complex multi-variable scoring systems are prone to reclassification errors. The adoption of a binary system in an emergency setting clearly attempts to solve the noted problems of complexity, uncertainty of classification, and the disconnect between the complexity of guidelines and operational triage. The potential role of A-VMN as a standard-of-care approach is driven by efficacy, operational efficiency, and safety. Regarding efficacy, in vivo scintigraphy shows lung deposition of approximately 34% of the nominal dose with an A-VMN combined with a valved holding chamber, compared with approximately 5% using a conventional jet nebulizer in spontaneously breathing subjects (an approximately six-fold difference), and more than three-fold greater lung deposition with an A-VMN than with a jet nebulizer during non-invasive ventilation in subjects with COPD; in vitro data confirm a high fine-particle fraction consistent with deep lung delivery [ 33 , 34 , 43 ]. Regarding operational efficiency, clinical studies reviewed by the panel indicate that A-VMN is associated with a shorter median ED length of stay (4.2 vs 4.8 hours), higher discharge rates (56.1% vs 43.0%), lower admission rates (28.1% vs 41.4%), and substantially reduced total albuterol dose requirements [ 35 , 44 ]. Together, these findings indicate that A-VMN improves ED efficiency and patient flow [ 45 ]. Regarding safety, A-VMN eliminates the risk of hyperoxia-induced hypercapnia in COPD patients as it requires no driving gas; oxygen-driven nebulizers can cause significant increases in arterial carbon dioxide, partly attributable to the Haldane effect in which hemoglobin loses affinity for carbon dioxide upon binding high levels of oxygen [ 31 , 32 ]. Furthermore, fugitive aerosol emission during nebulization is a recognized occupational exposure for healthcare workers and bystanders and both nebulizer type and patient interface influence the quantity released; when used within a closed circuit or with a valved interface, A-VMN reduces these emissions compared with open jet nebulizer confirguration, a relevant infection control considerartion in the post-COVID-19 era [ 37 , 46 – 48 ]. While A-VMNs offer several well-documented advantages, the optimal aerosol delivery device should be selected on an individual basis, considering clinical factors such as the underlying disease, comorbidities, ventilation strategy, medication formulation, and overall cost-effectiveness [ 49 , 50 ]. Moreover, the role of MDIs in the management of exacerbations should not be overlooked, as GINA and GOLD guidelines still consider MDIs with or without a spacer as an alternative to nebulizers in mild/moderate cases. Nevertheless, nebulizers may be preferred in patients with severe exacerbations, in whom the effective use of inhaler devices may be limited by the severity of their clinical condition, making nebulized drug delivery a more practical approach [ 39 , 40 ]. The consensus challenges the “more is better” approach to acute pharmacology. By recommending optimized bronchodilator dosing, the protocol aims to reduce dose-related toxicities such as tachycardia and lactic acidosis. High cumulative doses of beta-agonists (both SABAs, including albuterol, and LABAs, including formoterol) can cause Type B lactic acidosis, paradoxically worsening dyspnea and tachycardia, which may be misinterpreted as a severe exacerbation, leading to further harmful increases in beta-agonist dosage [ 23 – 25 ]. Similarly, the strong stance against mega-dose corticosteroids (>125 mg) aligns local practice with observational evidence associating higher doses with longer hospital and ICU stay and more frequent hyperglycaemia, in the absence of randomized trials directly comparing corticosteroids doses [ 26 – 29 ]. From an implementation and economic perspective, although A-VMN units carry a higher initial acquisition cost, available pharmacoeconomic analyses suggest potential offsets over time. Medication waste is significantly reduced (residual volumes often < 10% vs > 50% for jet nebulizers), per-treatment delivery is up to 1.5 times more cost-effective, and shorter treatment times reduce personnel workload and improve ED throughput [ 36 , 51 , 52 ]. A systematic review indicated roughly 2 days shorter hospital stays in ventilated COPD patients in favour of A-VMN. However, Direct economic evidence remains limited: a rapid response review of VMNs in acute care identified no cost-effectiveness evaluations and no clinical evidence specific to COPD, and comparative cost data in ventilated populations are still lacking. Formal prospective economic evaluation of A-VMN in ventilated COPD patients is therefore a priority for future research [ 36 , 53 ]. Availability of A-VMN units may vary across peripheral and primary healthcare centers compared to the tertiary centers represented by the panel, and transitioning to A-VMN technology requires specific staff training in proper device placement. Integrating A-VMN with HFNC and NIV enables continuous therapy without de-recruitment, a significant advantage for managing severe respiratory failure. The establishment of key performance indicators (KPIs), specifically Door-to-Nebulization time, Length of Stay, and Re-visit Rates, integrated into electronic health record (EHR) dashboards and reviewed in monthly multidisciplinary audits, provides a foundation for continuous quality improvement and accountability. The consensus also reinforces critical safety thresholds in local protocols. The decision to intubate is guided by the MoH Pocket Manual in Critical Care, which mandates invasive ventilation for patients with worsening mental status, persistent hypoxia, or requiring nebulization more frequently than every hour despite maximal therapy [ 20 ]. Several limitations apply. Large-scale randomized controlled trials conducted solely within the Saudi ED population are limited. The binary classification ‘Severe’ vs. ‘Non-Severe’ requires validation in prospective local studies to confirm its impact on patient outcomes. Conclusion This multidisciplinary consensus offers a practical and evidence-informed approach to acute respiratory management in Saudi Arabia. By simplifying triage with a binary entry model, standardizing pharmacological algorithms, and providing structured, evidence-graded guidance on aerosol delivery device selection, this framework supports frontline emergency physicians in delivering consistent, high-quality care for acute asthma and COPD exacerbations. Proposed next steps include sharing the binary triage algorithm with ED triage nurses, reviewing institutional protocols to ensure that appropriate respiratory support modalities (HFNC, NIV) and aerosol delivery options are available in the ED and ICU, and establishing structured tracking of five priority KPIs: Door-to-Nebulization time, Length of Stay, 28-day readmission rate, the proportion of patients discharged on ICS containing inhalers, and OPD follow-up rate.
