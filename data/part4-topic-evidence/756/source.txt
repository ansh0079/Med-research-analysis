Abstract Fibromyalgia (FM) is a chronic, multifactorial pain syndrome involving widespread musculoskeletal pain, fatigue, sleep disturbances, and cognitive dysfunction, significantly reducing the quality of life of patients. FM is classified under MG30.01, representing chronic widespread pain, within the broader classification of chronic primary pain according to the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision. FM has a complex, multifactorial pathophysiology involving peripheral and central mechanisms that contribute to its marked clinical heterogeneity. Currently, FM is diagnosed primarily through clinical assessment using widely accepted criteria, such as the American College of Rheumatology criteria, most recently revised in 2016, which evolved from the widely used 2010/2011 diagnostic framework. A thorough differential diagnosis is required to distinguish FM from a wide range of rheumatic, neurological, endocrine, psychiatric, and other medical disorders. FM management requires a multidisciplinary approach. Non-pharmacological interventions, including patient education, exercise (eg, aerobic and resistance training), and psychological therapies, such as cognitive behavioral therapy, are recommended as first-line treatments. Pharmacological therapies, including pregabalin, duloxetine, milnacipran, and cyclobenzaprine, are Food and Drug Administration-approved and provide clinically meaningful benefits to patients with FM. Although tramadol may be considered, the use of other opioids is generally not recommended because of their limited efficacy and potential risks. Despite the availability of current diagnostic criteria and treatment options, achieving a consistent diagnosis and optimal outcomes in patients remains challenging. Therefore, this narrative review aims to provide a concise, updated, and clinically relevant overview of the current evidence regarding the pathogenesis, diagnosis, and multidisciplinary management of FM to improve diagnostic accuracy and support individualized patient management. Furthermore, future directions should prioritize improving diagnostic precision and developing mechanism-based interventions targeting the underlying pathophysiology of FM. In the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision, FM is classified under MG30.01, representing chronic widespread pain, within the broader classification of chronic primary pain. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis across 38 countries estimated the pooled prevalence of FM in the general population at 1.40% (95% confidence interval, 0.49–2.47%), although the studies showed substantial heterogeneity. 1 The prevalence of FM was higher in several at-risk populations, particularly among individuals with autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, chronic pain, rheumatologic, and post-infectious conditions. 1 FM is predominantly diagnosed in women aged 20–55 years. Nonetheless, recent population-based studies indicate a female-to-male ratio of approximately 1.5–2:1, which is lower than previously reported. 2 , 3 Given its chronic and multifaceted symptom burden, FM significantly impairs quality of life, functional capacity, and work productivity, posing a substantial burden on patients and healthcare systems. 4–6 FM is a multifactorial disorder resulting from the complex interplay between central and peripheral mechanisms. Although its pathophysiology remains incompletely understood, current evidence suggests that central sensitization, immune and inflammatory abnormalities, dysautonomia (autonomic dysfunction), hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation, gut–brain axis dysfunction, genetic susceptibility, and psychological factors may contribute to its development and maintenance. According to the International Association for the Study of Pain, central sensitization is the heightened responsiveness of nociceptive neurons within the central nervous system (CNS) to normal or subthreshold sensory input and is a central mechanism that amplifies pain and drives FM. 7 In FM, central sensitization involves impaired descending inhibitory pathways, particularly serotonergic–noradrenergic dysfunction, alongside enhanced excitatory neurotransmission, neuroinflammation, and glial activation. 8–10 These changes, along with neuroplastic changes in pain-related brain regions, including the insula and anterior cingulate cortex, as revealed by neuroimaging studies, contribute to amplified pain processing and widespread pain perception. 8 , 9 In addition to cortical and subcortical abnormalities, patients with FM exhibit enhanced nociceptive processing at the spinal cord level. 10 Furthermore, evidence suggests that both neuroinflammation and systemic inflammation are involved in FM. Andrés-Rodríguez et al conducted a meta-analysis and concluded that FM is driven by an imbalance involving elevated pro-inflammatory and immune-regulatory cytokines, despite the small-to-moderate effect sizes reported. 11 Subsequently, in 2021, a meta-analysis by O’Mahony et al demonstrated that patients with FM exhibited significantly elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (tumor necrosis factor-alpha, interleukin-6, and interleukin-8, anti-inflammatory cytokine interleukin-10), and several chemokine markers, particularly eotaxin, compared with healthy controls. 12 Although elevated levels of C-reactive protein (CRP) have been observed in some patients with FM, this finding may be confounded by factors such as increased body mass index, physical inactivity, comorbidities, mood disorders, and sleep disturbances. 13 , 14 Autoimmune disorders may also contribute to the pathophysiology of FM. Hence, anti–G protein-coupled receptor autoantibodies targeting autonomic nervous system receptors have been identified in some patients with FM and may be associated with symptom severity. 15 In 2021, Goebel et al reported that passive transfer of immunoglobulin G from patients with FM, but not healthy controls, induced sensory hypersensitivity in healthy mice by sensitizing nociceptive neurons in the dorsal root ganglion, supporting an autoimmune contribution to FM pathogenesis. 16 Currently, dysautonomia is increasingly recognized in FM, which is characterized by elevated resting heart rate, reduced heart rate variability, sympathetic hyperactivity, and impaired autonomic responses to stress. 17–20 This disorder has been linked to anxiety and depressive symptoms and may interact with sleep disturbances in a vicious cycle that exacerbates pain and other symptoms in FM. 18 , 20 HPA axis dysregulation in FM has also been reported, characterized by altered cortisol levels and impaired physiological responses to stress, suggesting disruption of neuroendocrine stress-response mechanisms. 21 , 22 Recently, gut–brain axis dysregulation in FM has gained attention. Recent evidence suggests that gut microbiota dysbiosis, an increased prevalence of small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, and increased intestinal permeability (“leaky gut”) may facilitate the translocation of bacterial products and dietary antigens into the systemic circulation, thereby promoting immune activation and inflammation. 23 , 24 Additionally, alterations in microbial metabolites, neurotransmitter production, and gut–brain communication pathways, including vagal signaling, may influence neuroimmune, autonomic, and neuroendocrine signals, contributing to central sensitization and pain amplification. 23 , 25 , 26 Conversely, autonomic dysfunction and HPA axis dysregulation can further impair intestinal barrier integrity and alter microbial composition, creating a bidirectional feedback loop in FM. 23 , 25 , 26 Clinical reports indicate the presence of non-celiac gluten sensitivity characteristics in a subset of patients with FM. 27 Additionally, small fiber neuropathy is common in FM, suggesting that peripheral nerve dysfunction may contribute to FM pathophysiology. 28 , 29 Mitochondrial dysfunction, genetic susceptibility, and psychological factors have also been implicated in the development and persistence of FM. Currently, despite extensive research, reliable biomarkers for FM remain unavailable, leaving diagnosis dependent on clinical criteria. 13 Treatment of FM typically includes a combination of pharmacological and non-pharmacological approaches. While recent reviews have primarily focused on the mechanistic overlap between FM and other chronic pain conditions, this narrative review provides an updated clinical synthesis of diagnostic considerations, differential diagnoses, and multidisciplinary management strategies aimed at guiding practical patient care. Diagnosis FM has several diagnostic criteria, with the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria being the most commonly used in clinical practice. The first ACR criteria for diagnosing FM, introduced in 1990, required the presence of widespread pain and tenderness in at least 11 of 18 specified tender points. 30 The 1990 ACR criteria were originally developed as classification criteria for research purposes, not for clinical diagnosis. 31 , 32 In 2010, the ACR revised the FM diagnostic criteria, replacing tender point-based assessment with a symptom-based approach using the Widespread Pain Index and Symptom Severity Scale. 33 In 2011, the criteria were simplified by replacing physician-based evaluation of symptom extent with three patient-reported symptoms. 34 The most recent 2016 revision further refined these criteria to include a generalized pain requirement, thereby reducing the risk of misclassification. In addition to the ACR diagnostic criteria, other diagnostic standards have been developed, including those by the ACTTION (Analgesic, Anesthetic, and Addiction Clinical Trial Translations, Innovations, Opportunities, and Networks)–American Pain Society Pain Taxonomy (AAPT) group and a modified version of the Fibromyalgia Assessment Status (FAS) criteria. 35 , 36 In 2020, Salaffi et al reported a comparative study of the ACR 2011, ACR 2016, AAPT, and modified FAS criteria, indicating substantial agreement among the different FM diagnostic criteria. 35 The AAPT criteria had the lowest diagnostic accuracy for correct classification, whereas the modified FAS 2019 criteria showed a performance comparable to that of the ACR criteria. In 2021, Kang et al compared the AAPT and modified FAS criteria with the ACR 1990, 2010, 2011, and 2016 criteria in patients with FM in Korea. 37 The study showed that although the AAPT and modified FAS criteria simplified patient identification, they demonstrated lower diagnostic accuracy than most ACR-based criteria. The ACR 2016 criteria exhibited the best overall diagnostic performance among the evaluated tools. In 2023, Shapoval et al compared the ACR 1990, ACR 2011, ACR 2016, and AAPT 2019 criteria for diagnosing FM in patients with ankylosing spondylitis, and reported that the ACR 2011 and 2016 criteria are the most suitable for clinical use in this population. 38 Overall, the ACR diagnostic criteria are widely recognized for their clinical utility in FM diagnosis. Table 1 summarizes the ACR 2010, 2011, and 2016 criteria. From a clinical perspective, a comprehensive patient history and thorough physical examination are essential for an accurate FM diagnosis. Häuser et al proposed a systematic approach to facilitate its differential diagnosis from other conditions. 39 Evaluation of suspected FM should include a comprehensive assessment of pain and associated symptoms, including fatigue, non-restorative sleep, and other systemic manifestations. A detailed medical, psychosocial, and family history should be obtained, followed by a comprehensive physical examination. FM is characterized by pain that varies in location and intensity, often neuropathic-like with burning sensations, and worsens with changes in weather, stress, and inadequate sleep. On physical examination, the musculoskeletal examination is typically normal, except for diffuse soft tissue tenderness and dysesthesia. Limited laboratory testing may be performed when FM is suspected, including complete blood count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, CRP, creatine kinase, thyroid-stimulating hormone, and parathyroid hormone. 39 FM can be mimicked by various disorders, including inflammatory rheumatic diseases, non-inflammatory musculoskeletal disorders, non-rheumatic medical conditions (eg, endocrinal, infectious, and gastrointestinal diseases), neurological disorders, mental illness, cancer, and medication-induced pain syndromes. 39 , 40 Among these, conditions such as hypothyroidism, vitamin D deficiency, myopathies or myositis, Lyme disease, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, and hepatitis C may present with symptoms similar to FM, such as diffuse pain and fatigue. Indicators of alternative diagnoses include a family history of thyroid disease, weight gain, or abnormal thyroid function tests in cases of hypothyroidism; limited sun exposure or disturbed phosphate metabolism in vitamin D deficiency; proximal muscle weakness, difficulty climbing stairs or rising from a seated position, manifestations of connective tissue disease, or elevated creatine kinase levels in myopathies and myositis; and suggestive clinical features prompting targeted testing for Lyme disease, HIV infection, or hepatitis C. 39 Notably, following the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, long COVID, a syndrome characterized by symptoms persisting for more than 3 months after severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection, exhibits numerous clinical similarities with FM. 41 , 42 Mariette et al suggested that the term “ long COVID ” may be misleading because it implies persistent viral infection or immune dysregulation; consequently, they proposed the alternative term “FM-like post-COVID syndrome”. 42 Furthermore, COVID-19 vaccination may be associated with a decreased risk of developing new-onset FM among individuals who have survived SARS-CoV-2 infection. 43 Electromagnetic hypersensitivity, known as idiopathic environmental intolerance attributed to electromagnetic fields, has been proposed to share symptoms with FM, including widespread pain, fatigue, sleep disturbances, and cognitive dysfunction. 44 , 45 Therefore, FM diagnosis relies primarily on the clinical presentation of the patient, as well as the clinician’s judgement and expertise. It requires a comprehensive assessment rather than relying solely on the diagnostic criteria. A wide range of medical conditions may also coexist with FM, including autoimmune inflammatory diseases, sickle cell anemia, and advanced degenerative joint disease (traditionally known as secondary FM). 10 In addition, patients with FM frequently present with comorbid conditions such as irritable bowel syndrome, temporomandibular disorders, chronic headaches or migraine, and mood disturbances such as depression. 46 According to the ACR 2016 criteria, FM can be diagnosed even with coexisting conditions if the characteristic symptom profile is met, rather than being a diagnosis of exclusion. Treatment Managing FM is challenging due to its complex, multifactorial pathophysiology as well as its heterogeneous clinical presentation. In addition to chronic widespread pain, patients with FM frequently experience fatigue, sleep disturbances, cognitive dysfunction, mood disorders, and impaired physical functioning. Consequently, current management strategies in FM emphasize a multidisciplinary approach combining non-pharmacological treatments, including patient education, exercise, and psychological therapies, with pharmacological treatments, such as antidepressants and anticonvulsants, while recognizing non-pharmacological strategies as first-line management. 47 Effective FM care often requires a multidisciplinary healthcare team, involving knowledgeable healthcare professionals, nurses, physiotherapists, psychologists, sleep specialists, and social workers. 48 Non-Pharmacological Management Education and Self-Management FM is a chronic pain condition that significantly affects the quality of life of patients. Therefore, patient education is essential for effective FM management in multidisciplinary treatment programs. Zhang et al, in a 2025 patient-oriented guideline, proposed a structured framework consisting of seven main components addressed by 13 key questions, covering disease awareness, etiology and risk factors, diagnosis, treatment, lifestyle, prognosis, and social support. 49 Comprehensive, accessible, guideline-based educational resources help patients understand the disease and related issues, thereby providing a foundation for effective self-management. Despite limited scientific evidence that education reduces FM symptoms, it remains the first step in patient self-management. 50 , 51 Self-management interventions are typically multicomponent programs designed to assist patients in actively managing FM symptoms and their daily impact. Cedraschi et al (2004) conducted a randomized controlled trial (RCT) which demonstrated that a 6-week self-management program combining pool exercise and patient education improved the quality of life and treatment satisfaction in patients with FM. 52 Bourgault et al in 2015 subsequently demonstrated that a multicomponent, interdisciplinary, group-based self-management intervention enhanced patient control of FM symptoms. 53 This highlights the value of incorporating self-management strategies into multidisciplinary FM care. Exercise-Based Treatments Exercise is a type of physical activity involving planned, structured, and repetitive movements aimed at improving or maintaining physical fitness. 54 In patients with FM, exercise may alleviate pain through various mechanisms, including enhancing descending inhibitory pathways, modulating neurotransmitters, increasing pain thresholds, regulating the HPA axis, and improving emotional symptoms, particularly anxiety and depression. 55 , 56 According to the American College of Sports Medicine, exercise can generally be classified into four main types: aerobic (cardiorespiratory), resistance, flexibility, and neuromotor exercise training. 55 Guidelines from the APS (2005), Association of the Scientific Medical Societies in Germany (2008), Canadian Pain Society (CPS, 2012), and European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR, 2016) indicate that aerobic exercise has the strongest evidence among exercise-based interventions for managing FM. 47 , 48 , 57 , 58 Studies and clinical guidelines vary in their recommended intensity and frequency of aerobic exercise. According to the APS, patients should perform aerobic exercise at moderate intensity (approximately 60–75% of the age-adjusted maximum heart rate) two to three times per week. A meta-analysis by Sosa-Reina et al in 2017 reported that 30–60 min of aerobic exercise at an intensity of 50–80% of the maximum heart rate, two to three times weekly for approximately 4–6 months, effectively reduces pain and symptom severity in FM. 59 Subsequently, a meta-analysis by Albuquerque et al in 2022 demonstrated that exercise interventions lasting 13–14 weeks, with session durations of 30–60 min, effectively reduces pain in FM. 60 Recently, a 2026 meta-analysis by Wang et al recommended that aerobic exercise for FM should be performed at 60–90 min per session, twice weekly for at least eight weeks, keeping the total weekly exercise duration below 180 min. 56 In addition to aerobic exercise, resistance training is strongly recommended by the EULAR guidelines, typically performed with ≥ eight repetitions per exercise, two to three times weekly. 47 A meta-analysis by Sosa-Reina et al reported that performing 1–3 sets of 8–11 muscle-strengthening exercises, with each set comprising 8–10 repetitions performed at approximately 3.1 kg or 45% of the one-repetition maximum, is effective in reducing pain and symptom severity in FM. 59 In 2020, the World Health Organization recommended that adults perform 150–300 min of moderate-intensity physical activity, 75–150 min of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent combination of both weekly. 61 Therefore, aerobic and resistance exercises are recommended for patients with FM. However, as patients with FM often experience pain and fatigue, exercise frequency, session duration, total weekly volume, and intensity should be tailored and regularly reviewed throughout treatment to optimize tolerance and effectiveness. Taken together, a reasonable starting point for most patients with FM may be 30–60 min of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise performed two to three times per week, with gradual progression according to individual tolerance, and a total weekly exercise volume that generally does not exceed 180 min. Additionally, meta-analyses report the therapeutic benefits of combined or pool-based exercise programs. 56 , 59 , 60 , 62 Mind–body exercise interventions, including yoga, Tai chi, Qi Gong, and Pilates, are proposed as beneficial approaches for managing FM. 47 , 51 , 63 , 64 Psychological Interventions Patients with FM often exhibit psychological factors, such as vulnerable personality traits, negative life events, catastrophizing, passive coping strategies, and increased anxiety and depression, which contribute to emotional distress and may impair descending pain modulation mechanisms. 65 Therefore, psychological interventions are essential for managing FM. Several clinical guidelines strongly recommend cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for managing FM. 30 , 48 , 57 Neuroimaging evidence suggests that CBT may cause structural and functional changes in cortical brain regions, which reduce pain catastrophizing, improve sleep and arousal regulation, modulate HPA axis activity, and potentially reverse central sensitization. 66 , 67 Current guidelines and consensus statements recommend incorporating CBT into multimodal treatment for patients with FM. 47 , 51 , 57 , 68 , 69 Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) is a psychological intervention that promotes increased psychological flexibility, helping patients accept distressing thoughts and sensations while engaging in value-aligned behaviors. 70 An RCT by Simister et al, involving 67 patients with FM, reported that the integration of online ACT with standard care results in a significant improvement in the impact of FM compared to standard care alone. 71 Increased pain acceptance significantly mediated these improvements. Additionally, the combined treatment significantly improved depression, pain, and kinesiophobia. Catella et al also reported results favoring self-guided digital ACT over digital symptom tracking. 72 Moreover, ACT is cost-effective. 73 Therefore, ACT represents a promising adjunctive treatment for patients with FM. Current guidelines also recommend mind–body therapies, although the supporting evidence remains limited. 47 , 51 Other Treatments Several physical and complementary therapies have been investigated for the treatment of FM. Acupuncture, a traditional Chinese medicine technique, is recommended by some clinical guidelines as an adjunctive therapy for FM; 30 , 48 , 51 , 74 it may exert its effects by modulating pain-processing pathways, enhancing endogenous pain inhibitory mechanisms, and regulating inflammation and autonomic nervous system function, thereby alleviating pain in patients with FM. 51 , 75 , 76 However, a 2024 umbrella review of 11 meta-analyses by Araya-Quintanilla et al reported that acupuncture modestly improves pain and fatigue in patients with FM, but the overall evidence was limited, and over half of the observed benefits could not be directly attributed to the intervention. 77 Hydrotherapy, another physical therapy modality, is supported by high-level evidence in the EULAR 2016 (grade IA, weak recommendation) and Italian Society of Rheumatology 2021 (SIR, level 1C) guidelines; however, the 2025 Chinese guideline recommends it based on low-certainty evidence. 47 , 51 , 74 Several other physical and complementary therapies have also demonstrated potential benefits in patients with FM, although current evidence remains insufficient to support their routine recommendation in clinical guidelines. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) may alleviate FM symptoms by improving tissue oxygenation, reducing inflammation, modulating abnormal brain metabolism and glial function, and improving mitochondrial function. 78 In a 2023 meta-analysis, Chen et al concluded that HBOT may improve pain, fatigue, sleep, tender points, and overall function in patients with FM, with generally reversible adverse effects and potentially better tolerability at pressures below 2.0 atmospheres absolute. 78 In another meta-analysis performed by Yeh et al in 2019, 79 low-level laser therapy, which may reduce pain through photobiomodulation by enhancing mitochondrial function, increasing adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, and attenuating oxidative stress and inflammation, was found to be a potentially beneficial and well-tolerated treatment for FM. Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) has also been shown to improve pain, disability, and quality of life in patients with FM, according to a subsequent meta-analysis conducted in 2024 by García-López et al A recent cluster RCT involving 459 patients with FM further supported TENS as a safe and beneficial approach for pain management. 80 Additionally, in a meta-analysis by Li et al, massage therapy lasting at least 5 weeks was associated with significant improvements in pain, anxiety, and depression in patients with FM. 81 Virtual reality-based therapy and exergaming have also emerged as potential therapeutic options for patients with FM. 51 Noninvasive neuromodulation techniques, such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), have also been investigated for FM treatment. 82 rTMS may exert its therapeutic effects by modulating cortical excitability, restoring pain-processing networks, and regulating neurotrophic and glial activity. 82 , 83 Several meta-analyses have highlighted the effective role of rTMS in reducing pain and improving the quality of life in patients with FM; however, its effects on depression appear to be limited. 84–86 In addition to rTMS, tDCS has evolved as a promising noninvasive neuromodulation strategy for the treatment of FM. Although the exact mechanisms remain unclear, tDCS may alleviate FM symptoms by modulating cortical excitability, regulating neurotransmitter levels, and altering pain-related brain connectivity. 87 Evidence from several meta-analyses further supports its beneficial effects on pain management in FM. 88 , 89 Both rTMS and tDCS have shown potential for the management of FM. Nonetheless, heterogeneity across studies warrants further research to establish standardized stimulation protocols for rTMS and tDCS in FM treatment. Currently, several guidelines, including EULAR 2016 and the SIR 2021, do not recommend these modalities. 47 , 74 The 2025 Chinese guideline includes these interventions but with only a low level of recommendation. 51 Dietary interventions are increasingly being explored as adjunctive approaches to FM management. Their potential benefits may arise from reduced systemic inflammation, modulation of oxidative stress and energy metabolism, and alterations in gut microbiota composition. 90 A 2019 systematic review by Silva et al evaluated several dietary approaches, including gluten-free, raw vegetarian, low fermentable oligo-, di-, and monosaccharides and polyols (FODMAPs), hypocaloric, and monosodium glutamate- and aspartame-free diets. 91 Hypocaloric, raw vegetarian, and low-FODMAP diets may improve pain, functional status, quality of life, sleep, psychological symptoms, and inflammatory markers in patients with FM. Subsequently, a 2023 systematic review by Maddox et al reported that plant-based diets significantly improve pain outcomes in patients with FM, although small sample sizes and short intervention periods limited the strength of evidence supporting any specific diet. 92 Recently, a 2025 systematic review by Miller et al reported that adopting a Mediterranean diet in patients with early FM symptoms may confer low-to-moderate benefits, particularly before pharmacological treatment is initiated. 93 Although dietary interventions are not currently recommended in several clinical guidelines for FM, they may still be considered as adjunctive options for patient management. Several dietary supplements have been investigated as potential therapeutic options for FM; however, the current evidence remains limited. 94 , 95 Vitamin D has attracted particular interest in FM treatment because of its potential roles in pain modulation, anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory processes, and neurotransmitter regulation. 95 Wepner et al conducted an RCT, which demonstrated that 20 weeks of cholecalciferol supplementation in patients with FM and low vitamin D levels resulted in a significant reduction in pain compared with placebo. 96 Magnesium has also been proposed as a potential adjunctive therapy for FM through several mechanisms, including the prevention of central sensitization via voltage-dependent blockade of N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors and its essential role in ATP production. 94 In another RCT conducted by Macian et al, supplementation with 100 mg of magnesium daily for 1 month was associated with reduced pain severity and improved stress-related symptoms in patients with FM compared with placebo. 97 In addition, deficiencies of iron, zinc, and selenium have been found in patients with FM, suggesting a potential role for micronutrient supplementation in FM management. 98–100 Additionally, vitamin B12 has emerged as a potential supplement, with proposed mechanisms including anti-inflammatory effects, modulation of pain pathways, and promotion of nerve regeneration. 94 , 95 Vitamin B12 may also influence FM symptoms through its involvement in homocysteine metabolism. 101 Coenzyme Q10 has attracted interest as a potential adjunctive therapy for FM because of its antioxidant and mitochondrial-supporting properties, although current clinical evidence remains limited and inconclusive. 95 , 102 Acetyl-L-carnitine has also been explored for FM treatment owing to its antioxidant properties, role in supporting mitochondrial function, and ability to modulate pain- and mood-related neurotransmitter signaling. 95 In a retrospective study involving 137 patients with FM, acetyl-L-carnitine significantly improved pain severity and quality of life with a favorable safety profile, while no significant benefit on sleep quality was observed. 103 Omega-3 fatty acids may benefit FM through their anti-inflammatory properties. In an RCT involving 120 patients with FM, high-dose omega-3 significantly reduced pain, potentially by increasing serum magnesium and calcium levels. 104 Interestingly, melatonin may also alleviate FM symptoms through its effects on sleep, pain, and oxidative stress. 105 Moreover, as the understanding of the gut–brain axis in FM continues to evolve, probiotics have been investigated for FM treatment. Several RCTs have reported the beneficial effects of probiotic supplementation in the management of FM. 106 , 107 Other nutritional supplements, including S-adenosyl methionine and curcumin, have also shown potential as adjunctive therapies for FM. Current evidence indicates that vitamin D and magnesium have demonstrated promising results in RCTs, whereas evidence supporting other supplements remains preliminary and limited. Despite the growing interest in these supplements, further studies are required to clarify their clinical efficacy and therapeutic roles in the treatment of FM. Pharmacological Management Pharmacological therapy is important in FM management, particularly for patients with persistent symptoms despite non-pharmacological interventions. Overall, guidelines primarily recommend antidepressants and anticonvulsants for FM, which generally have the highest recommendation levels among pharmacological treatments. 47 , 48 , 74 Pharmacological treatment for patients with FM should balance the benefits and risks based on the principle of individualized therapy. Antidepressants Antidepressants include several classes: tricyclic antidepressants, serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (NRIs). Amitriptyline, a tricyclic antidepressant, is widely used in FM management despite the lack of FDA approval for this indication. The EULAR 2016, CPS 2012, and SIR 2021 guidelines suggest that low-dose amitriptyline may be considered for FM treatment. 47 , 74 A 2022 network meta-analysis by Farag et al showed that off-label amitriptyline improves sleep, fatigue, and overall quality of life more than FDA-approved treatments but provides only slight pain relief and no significant benefit for depression compared to placebo in treating patients with FM. 112 In contrast, a 2019 Cochrane systematic review by Moore et al showed that amitriptyline increases the likelihood of achieving at least 50% pain relief compared to that of placebo. However, it shows no consistent benefits for fatigue, sleep disturbance, quality of life, or tender point counts, and adverse events occur more frequently than with placebo. 113 Duloxetine and milnacipran (SNRIs) were approved by the FDA in 2008 and 2009, respectively, for treating FM. The EULAR 2016 and SIR 2021 guidelines recommend both agents as pharmacological options for FM management. A 2013 Cochrane systematic review by Häuser et al reported that duloxetine and milnacipran produced only small reductions in pain compared to placebo and did not substantially improve fatigue, quality of life, or sleep disturbances. 114 Moreover, treatment discontinuation due to adverse events was significantly more frequent with SNRIs than with placebo, although the incidence of serious adverse events did not differ significantly between duloxetine/milnacipran and placebo. A 2022 network meta-analysis by Farag et al showed that duloxetine 120 mg improves all efficacy outcomes, with the greatest effects on pain and depression. Milnacipran showed more modest effects: 100 mg slightly improves all outcomes except quality of life, while 200 mg modestly reduces pain, depression, and fatigue without significantly affecting sleep or quality of life. 112 Several other antidepressants have been investigated for FM treatment, including venlafaxine (SNRI), paroxetine (SSRI), and esreboxetine (NRI). A 2015 systematic review by VanderWeide et al reported that venlafaxine generally improves pain-related symptoms of FM; however, the evidence was limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous dosing regimens, and methodological concerns, suggesting only modest therapeutic benefit. 115 In a 2007 RCT involving 116 patients with FM, Patkar et al showed that paroxetine is generally well tolerated and enhances the overall effect on FM compared to that of placebo. However, it increases adverse events, including drowsiness, dry mouth, blurred vision, genital disorders, and anxiety. 116 A large multicenter RCT by Arnold et al in 2010 reported that esreboxetine significantly improves pain, global status, fatigue, and quality of life in patients with FM compared to placebo. However, it increases adverse events, particularly constipation, insomnia, dry mouth, and headache. 117 Anticonvulsants Pregabalin binds to the α2δ subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels, reducing the release of excitatory neurotransmitters and exerting analgesic, anxiolytic, and anticonvulsant effects. It was approved by the FDA in 2007 for FM treatment and is widely recommended in clinical guidelines with a high level of evidence. 47 , 74 A 2016 Cochrane systematic review by Ferreira-Dos-Santos et al reported that during 12–26 weeks of follow-up, only a minority of patients with moderate-to-severe FM pain experienced meaningful pain reduction with pregabalin (300–600 mg/day), while adverse effects were generally well tolerated. 118 A 2022 Bayesian network meta-analysis by Migliorini et al showed that pregabalin 450 mg/day maximizes the overall effect on FM, 600 mg/day provides the most favorable effects on sleep, and 300 mg/day was associated with the lowest rate of adverse events, suggesting that dosing should be tailored according to patient characteristics and predominant symptoms. 119 A 2022 network meta-analysis by Farag et al reported that pregabalin (150, 450, and 600 mg) moderately improves sleep, with 600 mg also enhancing quality of life. 112 However, its effects on other clinical outcomes remain limited. Several other anticonvulsants have also been investigated for FM treatment. Gabapentin, an anticonvulsant used off-label for treating FM, was evaluated in a 2007 RCT. Arnold et al randomly assigned 150 patients to receive gabapentin (1200–2400 mg/day) or placebo. 120 Gabapentin significantly improved response rates compared to placebo and provided benefits in functional impact, sleep, and global assessments, but showed no significant effects on tender point pain threshold or depression, and was generally well tolerated. Mirogabalin is a newer gabapentinoid anticonvulsant with greater selectivity for the α2δ subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels. In 2019, Arnold et al reported results from three 13-week randomized, double-blind, placebo- and active-controlled parallel-group trials and a 52-week open-label extension study. 121 Both mirogabalin dosing regimens (15 mg once and twice daily) were generally well tolerated and showed potential for pain reduction in FM; however, neither achieved a statistically significant improvement in pain compared to placebo for the primary endpoint in any trial. Muscle Relaxants Cyclobenzaprine is a centrally acting muscle relaxant structurally related to first-generation tricyclic antidepressants, such as amitriptyline. In August 2025, the FDA approved a sublingual formulation of cyclobenzaprine hydrochloride specifically for the treatment of FM in adults. 122 This approval was based on three large Phase 3 RCTs: RALLY, RELIEF, and RESILIENT. 123–125 The RELIEF and RESILIENT trials demonstrated significant pain reduction compared with placebo, whereas the RALLY trial did not. However, across all trials, cyclobenzaprine consistently improved the overall disease impact, as reflected by reductions in Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire scores and sleep-related outcomes. It was generally well tolerated, with local adverse events such as oral hypoesthesia, dysgeusia (taste disturbance), and oral paresthesia being the most common, and systemic adverse events including headache and somnolence. This marks the first FDA-approved drug for FM in 15 years, providing a new therapeutic option for patients. Other Pharmacological Treatments Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are generally not recommended or supported only by very low-quality evidence in several guidelines. 47 , 48 , 74 Tramadol, a weak opioid, produces analgesia by acting on μ-opioid receptors and inhibiting norepinephrine and serotonin reuptake. It may be used to treat patients with FM, particularly those with moderate-to-severe pain who do not respond to other therapies. 47 , 48 , 74 Strong opioids are not recommended by some guidelines. 48 , 74 A 2024 study by Rivera et al reported that opioid use in FM has increased over time without correlating with disease severity. 126 Patient-reported improvement did not differ between those who discontinued opioids and those who continued, whereas coping strategies improved significantly in patients who discontinued their use. Therefore, opioids should be used cautiously in patients with FM due to their limited clinical benefits. Cannabinoids are bioactive compounds that interact with the endocannabinoid system, modulating pain, inflammation, mood, and sleep. 127 , 128 A 2023 systematic review by Strand et al reported low-quality evidence supporting short-term pain relief from cannabinoids in patients with FM. 129 Similarly, a 2024 systematic review by Lopera et al reported that cannabinoid-based therapies may provide therapeutic benefits and are relatively safe, although the evidence remains limited. 130 Palmitoylethanolamide (PEA), an endogenous cannabinoid-like compound with anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties, has been explored as a treatment for FM. Evidence from two observational studies by Del Giorno et al in 2015, Schweiger et al in 2019, and an RCT by Salaffi et al in 2023 indicates that PEA may be a safe and effective adjunctive therapy. 131–133 NMDA receptor antagonists may be used in FM treatment by targeting central sensitization. By inhibiting NMDA receptors, they reduce neuronal hyperexcitability and attenuate pain perception. 134 A 2021 systematic review by Pastrak et al reported that a single low-dose intravenous ketamine infusion provides short-term pain relief in FM, with effects lasting for only a few hours. 135 A 2024 systematic review by de Carvalho et al reported that ketamine, administered intravenously or subcutaneously, is safe and effective in the short-term, although responses were inconsistent over longer follow-up. 136 Sodium oxybate, a CNS depressant and sodium salt of gamma-hydroxybutyrate, is approved for treating narcolepsy. 137 Several studies have demonstrated its efficacy in treating FM. In a 2009 multicenter RCT involving 188 patients, Russell et al reported that sodium oxybate (4.5 or 6 g nightly for 8 weeks) is well tolerated and significantly improves pain, overall disease effect, global status, and sleep. 138 Three subsequent phase 3 RCTs further confirmed its efficacy and safety in patients with FM. 139–141 However, sodium oxybate is not FDA-approved for FM treatment, and its potential for abuse and dependence requires careful monitoring. 142 Conclusion FM is a complex and heterogeneous disorder that challenges both diagnosis and management. Its multifactorial pathophysiology involves both central and peripheral mechanisms. Despite advances in understanding its pathophysiology, FM diagnosis still relies primarily on symptom-based assessment, highlighting the need for objective biomarkers. Current management emphasizes a multidisciplinary approach that combines non-pharmacological interventions, including patient education, exercise, and psychological therapies, with pharmacological treatments, such as antidepressants and anticonvulsants. This narrative review provides an overview of the current evidence regarding the pathogenesis, diagnosis, and multidisciplinary management of FM, aiming to enhance diagnostic precision and support personalized treatment. Future research should focus on improving diagnostic accuracy and developing mechanism-based interventions that target the underlying pathophysiology of FM.
