Purpose Systemic corticosteroids are pivotal in managing inflammation-driven critical illnesses, yet bedside translation remains inconsistent. This systematic review establishes an evidence-based framework for clinical decision-making by horizontally synthesizing contemporary evidence across severe community-acquired pneumonia (sCAP), acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and septic shock. Methods A systematic electronic search was conducted across PubMed, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library up to early 2026. Peer-reviewed randomized controlled trials (RCTs), systematic reviews, and meta-analyses published in English were eligible. Data regarding population characteristics, dosing regimens, initiation timing, duration, and safety profiles were independently extracted and qualitatively synthesized. Results Landmark trials define context-dependent efficacy: early hydrocortisone continuous infusion significantly reduces 28-day mortality in sCAP (CAPE COD), while a 10-day dexamethasone regimen improves ventilator-free days and survival in moderate-to-severe ARDS (DEXA-ARDS). In septic shock, early hydrocortisone-fludrocortisone combination accelerates shock resolution and lowers 90-day mortality (APROCCHS). These outcomes align with distinct pharmacological profiles: hydrocortisone provides dual glucocorticoid/mineralocorticoid activity to restore vascular tone, whereas dexamethasone exerts potent transrepression of proinflammatory transcription factors. To mitigate steroid-induced harm, protocolized safety targets are essential, including maintaining blood glucose at 140–180 mg/dL and actively monitoring for secondary infections. Conclusion Corticosteroids do not exert a uniform class effect. Bedside success requires personalized, protocolized regimens optimized by scenario-specific timing, dosing, and proactive adverse-event monitoring. Severe community-acquired pneumonia (sCAP) [ 2 ], acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [ 3 ], and septic shock [ 4 ] are frequently encountered in daily critical care practice and often require complex, time-sensitive therapeutic decisions. Although advancements in ventilation strategies, antimicrobial therapy, and hemodynamic support have improved clinical outcomes [ 5 ], these measures alone may be insufficient to alter the disease course in some patients. Adjunct pharmacologic therapy maybe benefits but its role remains unclear. Evidence suggests that sCAP, ARDS, and septic shock all dysregulate host inflammatory responses, causing tissue injury, organ dysfunction, and circulatory instability [ 6 ]. Because of their anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties, systemic corticosteroids have been evaluated as adjunct treatments in these conditions [ 7 ]. However, early trials yielded heterogeneous results. Concerns regarding secondary infections, metabolic complications, and delayed pathogen clearance limited widespread adoption. Moreover, uncertainty regarding appropriate patient selection, dosing intensity, and treatment duration complicated clinical implementation [ 8 ]. The publication of several large randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating standardized regimens and applying clear inclusion criteria has sparked interest in corticosteroid therapy for various critical illnesses. Trials [ 9 , 10 ] have clarified the potential benefits and risks of corticosteroid initiation at specific disease stages and their administration for limited durations. Differences in study populations, intervention timing, corticosteroid formulation, and cointerventions complicate direct comparison across trials and limit the generalizability of findings. These variations suggest that corticosteroids do not exert a uniform class effect across critical illnesses; treatment effects appear to be highly context-dependent, influenced by etiology, disease severity, inflammatory phenotype, and administration timing [ 6 , 11 ]. Consequently, clinicians frequently encounter unresolved practical questions at the bedside, for example, when corticosteroids should be initiated, which regimen is optimal for a given clinical scenario, and how therapy should be tapered or discontinued to balance potential benefits against adverse effects. In this narrative review, we synthesized contemporary evidence from RCTs (Table 1 ), meta-analyses, and international guidelines addressing systemic corticosteroid use in patients with sCAP, ARDS, or septic shock. By highlighting both areas of consensus and persistent uncertainty, we offer clinical insights for personalized decision-making in the management of critical illnesses (Fig. 1 ). Mechanism of action and pharmacological rationale Systemic corticosteroids commonly used in critical care—for example, hydrocortisone, methylprednisolone, and dexamethasone—are synthetic analogs of endogenous cortisol with distinct immunomodulatory and hemodynamic properties. These drugs exert their effects by binding to cytoplasmic glucocorticoid receptors, reaching the nucleus, and modulating transcription [ 15 ][Fig. 2 ]. Although the underlying molecular mechanisms have been extensively described, their clinical relevance in critical illnesses lies in how they differentially regulate inflammation, vascular tone, and stress adaptation [ 16 ]. Corticosteroids suppress excessive inflammatory signaling predominantly by transrepressing key proinflammatory transcription factors, such as nuclear factor-kappa B and activator protein-1, thereby downregulating cytokines such as interleukin-1, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α [ 17 ]. In parallel, transactivation upregulates anti-inflammatory mediators such as interleukin-10. In patients with critical illnesses, where inflammatory responses are often amplified and poorly regulated, this dual modulation helps attenuate tissue injury without completely abolishing host defense. Corticosteroids exert clinically relevant effects on cardiovascular function. In severe infections and septic shock, dysregulated inflammation is accompanied by vasoplegia and impaired responsiveness to endogenous and exogenous catecholamines [ 18 ]. Corticosteroids enhance vascular smooth muscle sensitivity to vasopressors and reduce nitric oxide–mediated vasodilation by downregulating inducible nitric oxide synthase [ 6 , 19 , 20 ]. These effects explain the reported reductions in vasopressor requirements, even in the absence of consistent mortality benefit. Among available corticosteroids, hydrocortisone uniquely possesses both glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid properties. This pharmacological profile supports its use in septic shock, which is characterized by relative adrenal insufficiency, altered cortisol metabolism, and impaired hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis function—collectively described as critical illness–related corticosteroid insufficiency [ 6 , 21 ]. Notably, corticosteroids vary substantially in terms of receptor affinity, tissue penetration, and pharmacokinetic behavior [ 22 ], exerting variable clinical effects across disease states. Therefore, corticosteroid therapy should be personalized rather than administered as a uniform class intervention in patients with critical illnesses [ 23 ]. For optimal therapeutic decisions, pharmacological principles should be integrated into multidisciplinary critical care management to achieve targeted immunomodulation while minimizing metabolic and systemic adverse effects. The following sections present contemporary clinical evidence and recommendations for corticosteroid use across critical illnesses. Systemic corticosteroids for various critical illnesses sCAP sCAP refers to the progression of community-acquired pneumonia to respiratory failure, sepsis, or both, necessitating ICU admission. It is associated with high rates of mortality and complications. The 2024 guidelines jointly issued by the Society of Critical Care Medicine (SCCM) and the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine (ESICM) recommend the use of systemic corticosteroids as adjunct therapy for sCAP in adults [ 24 ]. This recommendation reflects consistent benefit observed under specific therapeutic regimens in high-quality clinical trials. The CAPE COD trial [ 9 ] is the most influential contemporary study shaping current practice. This multicenter, double-blind, randomized placebo-controlled trial was conducted in France. It included 800 ICU patients with sCAP and compared continuous intravenous hydrocortisone (dose: 200 mg/day) with placebo. Both the intervention and control groups received standard care. After 4 days of treatment, corticosteroid therapy was subsequently adjusted to clinical response, with a maximum duration of 14 days and protocolized tapering upon clinical improvement. The trial initially sought to enroll 1,146 patients, but it was terminated early following interim analysis, given a substantial survival signal favoring hydrocortisone. At 28 days, all-cause mortality was lower in the intervention group than in the control group (6.2% vs. 11.9%), corresponding to an absolute risk reduction of 5.7% ( p = 0.006). This difference persisted at 90 days (intervention group vs. control group: 9.3% vs. 14.7%). Furthermore, the rates of subsequent endotracheal intubation and vasopressor requirements were lower in the intervention group than in the control group, suggesting deceleration of disease progression rather than an isolated survival benefit. Prespecified subgroup analyses revealed generally consistent treatment effects across most categories. Although larger effect sizes were observed in some subgroups, such as older adults, female patients, patients with higher Pneumonia Severity Index scores, and patients without an identified pathogen, the findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the exploratory nature of subgroup analyses. Nevertheless, the overall pattern confirms that early, short-term hydrocortisone therapy may benefit certain patients with sCAP. Notably, not all RCTs evaluating corticosteroids for use in sCAP have yielded positive results. The ESCAPe trial [ 25 ] evaluated the efficacy of prolonged (20 days) low-dose methylprednisolone in managing ICU patients with sCAP. Compared with placebo, the drug did not significantly reduce the rate of 60-day all-cause mortality. These inconsistencies may be partly explained by interstudy differences in corticosteroid formulation, treatment duration, and initiation timing. Taken together, the trials suggest that the benefit of corticosteroids in sCAP neither represents a uniform class effect nor increases with prolonged treatment duration. Rather, clinical efficacy depends on appropriate drug selection and early initiation using clearly defined, time-limited regimens. Compared with the CAPE COD trial, which emphasized early initiation and short-term therapy, the ESCAPe trial opted for delayed initiation and prolonged therapy; this approach might have intensified cumulative metabolic and infection-related adverse effects. Moreover, delayed intervention may have coincided with a later phase of pulmonary inflammation, when corticosteroid-mediated immunomodulation is less effective or potentially detrimental [ 9 , 25 ]. Inter-RCT variations have been quantitatively validated by subsequent systematic and network meta-analyses. A 2025 systematic network meta-analysis [ 26 ] of 11 RCTs (2,042 patients with sCAP) compared multiple corticosteroids—hydrocortisone, dexamethasone, prednisolone, and methylprednisolone—against placebo. Compared with placebo, hydrocortisone significantly reduced all-cause mortality (relative risk [RR]: 0.35; 95% Bayesian credible interval: 0.14–0.64) and mechanical ventilation requirement (RR: 0.73; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.51–0.93). However, the other corticosteroids conferred no significant mortality benefit. Although direct head-to-head comparisons among corticosteroids revealed no single superior drug with high certainty, ranking probability analyses indicated that hydrocortisone had the highest likelihood of reducing mortality (surface under the cumulative ranking curve: 88.7%), whereas prednisone had the lowest likelihood. Notably, hydrocortisone did not increased the risk of severe adverse events. Dose–response analyses suggested that low to moderate dosing (equivalent to methylprednisolone < 1 mg/kg/day) combined with short treatment duration (≤ 7 days) achieved the best balance between efficacy and safety. Low-to-moderate-dose regimens led to approximately 50% reductions in both mortality and intubation rates. Higher-dose strategies conferred no additional benefit. Similarly, short-term therapy outperformed long-term therapy in reducing mortality and ventilator use. Taken together, contemporary evidence and the 2024 SCCM/ESICM guideline consensus indicate that the central question regarding corticosteroid therapy for sCAP is no longer whether corticosteroids should be used but rather how they should be used [ 24 ]. Early initiation in appropriately selected patients, preferential use of hydrocortisone, use of low to moderate dosing, and predefined short duration of treatment can maximize clinical benefit while minimizing excessive immunosuppression and metabolic complications. International expert consensus and guidelines support consideration of adjunct systemic corticosteroids in adults with sCAP requiring ICU admission, particularly when initiated early in the disease course. In clinical practice, corticosteroid therapy is generally considered appropriate for patients exhibiting pronounced systemic inflammation or persistent disease severity despite standard initial management. Typical clinical features include sustained high oxygen requirements, extensive bilateral pulmonary infiltrates, or evolving hemodynamic instability. Early initiation—most commonly within the first 72 h after disease onset or ICU admission—may be crucial for maximizing the anti-inflammatory benefits of corticosteroids before irreversible lung injury or fibroproliferative changes [ 27 ]. Regarding drug selection and dosing, intravenous hydrocortisone at a cumulative dose of 200 mg/day remains the most extensively studied and commonly adopted regimen. This dose may be administered either as a continuous infusion or as intermittent bolus dosing (e.g., 50 mg every 6 h), with both approaches considered clinically equivalent in terms of efficacy. This strategy is consistent with the dosing protocols used in the CAPE COD trial and supported by multiple contemporary meta-analyses; it balances therapeutic benefit with an acceptable safety profile [ 9 , 26 ]. Regarding treatment duration, current evidence favors a short-term approach. A duration of approximately 5–7 days is commonly recommended, with consideration for dose tapering or discontinuation once clear signs of clinical improvement appear—for example, resolution of severe hypoxemia, stabilization of respiratory status, or successful weaning from mechanical ventilation. Treatment duration is limited to prevent corticosteroid-related adverse events without compromising efficacy. The rationale for corticosteroid use differs between patients with sCAP and those with sCAP complicated by septic shock. The CAPE COD trial deliberately excluded patients with septic shock to isolate the pulmonary effects of corticosteroids in sCAP [ 9 ]. Thus, in patients who present with or subsequently develop septic shock requiring vasopressors, corticosteroid use should be guided primarily by evidence and recommendations specific to septic shock management, where the primary goal is hemodynamic stabilization rather than pulmonary inflammation alleviation (discussed in the subsequent section). In summary, for patients with sCAP without concomitant septic shock, early initiation of a clearly defined, short-term, moderate-dose hydrocortisone regimen appears most likely to improve survival and decelerate disease progression. Careful patient selection, adherence to evidence-based dosing, and ongoing clinical reassessment are essential to realize the potential benefits of corticosteroid therapy while minimizing unnecessary exposure and risk. ARDS ARDS is a highly heterogeneous clinical syndrome that arises from diverse pulmonary and extrapulmonary insults, such as pneumonia, aspiration, pancreatitis, shock, severe trauma, drug-related lung injury, near-drowning, fat embolism, and transfusion-related acute lung injury. Despite diverse etiologies, ARDS is pathologically unified by acute, diffuse injury to the alveolar–capillary barrier, which leads to increased vascular permeability, noncardiogenic pulmonary edema, and impaired gas exchange. Clinically, ARDS is characterized by progressive hypoxemia and bilateral pulmonary infiltrates on imaging. Despite advanced management through lung-protective ventilation and supportive care, ARDS remains associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. Marked heterogeneity in ARDS diagnostic criteria, underlying etiologies, and inflammatory phenotypes has historically hindered the development of effective pharmacologic therapies with consistent benefit across patient populations [ 28 ]. Because of their potent anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties, systemic corticosteroids represent a potential therapeutic option. The earlier international guidelines generally discouraged routine corticosteroid use in ARDS. The paradigm began to shift with the accumulation of higher-quality clinical evidence. A pivotal RCT in this field is the DEXA-ARDS study [ 12 ]. This multicenter trial was conducted in Spain. It included 277 patients with moderate to severe ARDS and evaluated the effect of early administration of dexamethasone. The intervention group received intravenous dexamethasone at 20 mg/day for 5 days and then at 10 mg/day for another 5 days. By contrast, the control group received standard supportive care. Compared with the control group, the intervention group exhibited a significant reduction in the rate of 60-day all-cause mortality (36% vs. 21%; p = 0.0047), corresponding to an estimated number-needed-to-treat value of approximately 7. In addition, the number of ventilator-free days within the first 28 days was significantly higher in the intervention group than in the control group (mean: 12.3 vs. 7.5 days; p < 0.0001), indicating faster recovery of pulmonary function and earlier weaning from mechanical ventilation. The safety profile observed in the DEXA-ARDS study was reassuring. The incidence of newly diagnosed infections in the ICU did not differ significantly between the intervention and control groups (24% vs. 25%). Although hyperglycemia occurred more frequently in the intervention group than in the control group (76% vs. 70%), this did not translate into an increased risk of severe infections or other major complications. The investigators concluded that dexamethasone significantly reduced mortality and shortened mechanical ventilation in patients with early-stage (< 48 h from diagnosis) moderate to severe ARDS receiving standard supportive therapy. International perspectives on corticosteroid use in ARDS evolved after the publication of the DEXA-ARDS study. For example, 2020 onward, several European critical care societies supported the consideration of corticosteroid administration during the early phase (first 1 to 2 weeks after onset) of ARDS. This evolving stance was further reflected in the 2024 focused update jointly issued by the SCCM and ESICM [ 23 ], which provided a conditional recommendation for corticosteroid use in adults with ARDS, emphasizing careful patient selection and clinical context. Evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses has reinforced the paradigm shift. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 RCTs (1,607 patients with ARDS) reported that, compared with standard care or placebo, corticosteroid therapy significantly reduced the rate of 28-day mortality (RR: 0.78; 95% CI: 0.70–0.87) [ 29 ]. Secondary analyses revealed a reduction in the rate of ICU mortality (RR: 0.67; 95% CI: 0.45–0.98). However, a moderate interstudy heterogeneity was observed ( I 2 = 52%). Six RCTs reported a significant increase in the number of ventilator-free days, with an average gain of approximately 3.5 days (95% CI: 2.6–4.7; p < 0.01) and a relatively low level of heterogeneity ( I 2 = 31%), supporting consistent efficacy in facilitating ventilator weaning. Regarding safety, the meta-analysis revealed no significant treatment-related increase in the incidence of hyperglycemia necessitating intervention or that of clinically relevant upper gastrointestinal bleeding. Subgroup analyses indicated consistent mortality benefit among adult patients, those with non-COVID-19-related ARDS, using methylprednisolone or hydrocortisone. However, no clear benefit was noted in pediatric populations. These findings align with those of a prior meta-analysis [ 30 , 31 ], which employed trial sequential analysis and underscored the importance of early initiation and low to moderate (or moderately high) dosing in achieving mortality reduction. Overall, systemic corticosteroids can improve survival and shorten mechanical ventilation in select patients with ARDS. However, the magnitude of benefit is closely associated with initiation timing, dosing strategy, and patient phenotype. Although concerns regarding secondary infections and other adverse events persist, available data from contemporary trials do not indicate a substantial increase in severe complications when corticosteroids are administered for a defined duration in appropriately selected patients. Nevertheless, given the residual interstudy heterogeneity and ongoing uncertainty regarding long-term infection risk, further large-scale, well-designed trials are required to refine patient selection and optimize treatment protocols. The timing and dosing of systemic corticosteroid therapy for ARDS should be tailored to disease stage and severity. Accumulating evidence supports consideration of corticosteroid therapy during the early phase of moderate to severe ARDS, when intervention is likely to confer clinical benefit [ 29 ]. In most clinical studies, “early” therapy was defined as treatment initiation within 72 h after ARDS diagnosis. At this stage, corticosteroids can effectively mitigate excessive inflammation, improve oxygenation, and facilitate lung recovery. By contrast, treatment initiation during the later stages of ARDS—particularly after the first week, when fibroproliferative processes predominate—is associated with diminished therapeutic benefit and an increased adverse event risk. Regarding drug selection, methylprednisolone and hydrocortisone have been associated with reduced mortality in patients with ARDS, whereas dexamethasone has consistently yielded favorable trends [ 29 ]. These findings may be partly explained by pharmacokinetic characteristics such as prolonged systemic half-life and increased pulmonary tissue penetration compared with those of other corticosteroids. Experimental pharmacokinetic data [ 22 ] indicate that methylprednisolone achieves higher lung concentrations than does prednisolone, reinforcing that corticosteroids are not fully interchangeable in the management of ARDS and that drug-specific properties should be considered in clinical decision-making. Regarding dosing strategies, the most robust evidence is offered by the DEXA-ARDS study [ 12 ]. The high initial dose (20 mg) with short-term taper approach adopted in the study significantly reduced mortality and shortened mechanical ventilation without increasing the risk of severe adverse events. When dexamethasone is unavailable or contraindicated, alternative regimens with comparable anti-inflammatory potency may be considered—for example, methylprednisolone at 1 to 2 mg/kg/day can be administered in divided doses or hydrocortisone at a cumulative dose of 200–300 mg/day [ 29 , 32 ]. However, clinicians should consider interdrug variations in pharmacokinetics and mineralocorticoid activity. The optimal duration of corticosteroid therapy in ARDS remains unclear. In a study by Chang et al. [ 12 ], short (≤ 7 days), intermediate (8–14 days), and prolonged (≥ 15 days) treatment durations all reduced the rate of 28-day mortality compared with the rate observed with no corticosteroid use. Notably, no clear dose–response or duration–response relationship was observed, and extending treatment duration did not confer additional survival benefit. Further dose-stratified analyses revealed that although low to moderate dosing (methylprednisolone ≤ 2 mg/kg/day) significantly reduced mortality risk, higher-dose regimens conferred no meaningful benefit. Therefore, achieving appropriate immunomodulation, rather than dose escalation, is crucial for therapeutic efficacy. The role of corticosteroids in viral ARDS is highly context-dependent. The balance between benefit and harm varies substantially by the causative pathogen, host immune response, and disease phase. For influenza-associated ARDS, multiple international societies and clinical guidelines consistently advise against routine use of systemic corticosteroids. The 2018 Infectious Diseases Society of America influenza guidelines [ 33 ] explicitly advise against adjunct corticosteroids unless a separate indication has been established—for example, acute asthma exacerbation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbation, or adrenal insufficiency. This recommendation is primarily informed by observational studies and meta-analyses demonstrating associations between corticosteroid use in influenza-associated pneumonia or ARDS and high mortality, increased nosocomial infection risk, and delayed viral clearance. Although these studies had inherent limitations, such as confounding by indication and disease severity, the overall direction of evidence led to broad clinical consensus favoring avoidance of routine corticosteroid therapy in influenza-associated ARDS. This position has been reaffirmed by the World Health Organization 2024/2025 updates on the management of influenza and severe acute respiratory infections, which emphasize early antiviral therapy and supportive care as primary treatment strategies [ 34 ]. By contrast, the role of corticosteroids in COVID-19-related ARDS is supported by high-quality RCTs. The RECOVERY trial [ 13 ] demonstrated that dexamethasone at 6 mg/day for up to 10 days significantly reduced the rate of 28-day mortality in hospitalized patients requiring supplemental oxygen or mechanical ventilation, establishing corticosteroids as a cornerstone of treatment in severe COVID-19. Subsequent trials investigated whether higher corticosteroid doses could further improve outcomes. Large multicenter RCTs—REMED [ 35 ], COVID STEROID 2 [ 36 ], and COVIDICUS [ 37 ]—have demonstrated that escalating dexamethasone doses beyond the standard 6 mg/day does not confer consistent or reliable benefits in terms of mortality, ventilator-free days, or other major clinical outcomes. In the REMED trial, high-dose dexamethasone (20 mg/day for 5 days, followed by 10 mg/day for 5 days) did not significantly increase the number of ventilator-free days or reduce the rate of long-term mortality. Interpretation of these findings is tempered by early trial termination, limited sample size, and potential heterogeneity related to circulating viral variants. Nevertheless, similar neutral results were reported by the COVID STEROID 2 and COVIDICUS trials, reinforcing the absence of clear benefit from dose escalation. Several studies have reported numerically higher rates of thrombotic events, septic shock, or other severe complications in higher-dose corticosteroid groups, suggesting that intensified immunosuppression incurs safety tradeoffs without corresponding clinical gains [ 29 ]. Taken together, these findings indicate that, once an evidence-based standard dose has been established, routine escalation of corticosteroid dosing is unlikely to further improve outcomes in COVID-19-related ARDS and can increase the risk of adverse events. In summary, current evidence suggests that systemic corticosteroid therapy for ARDS should emphasize early initiation, moderate dosing, and a predefined treatment duration. For most patients with COVID-19-related ARDS, dexamethasone 6 mg/day for 7–10 days remains the preferred regimen, with methylprednisolone serving as a reasonable alternative in specific cases. Corticosteroid tapering may be considered as oxygenation and pulmonary function improve, particularly to avoid rebound inflammation following abrupt discontinuation, and early termination should be considered once patients achieve sustained clinical stability and successful ventilation weaning. Overall, corticosteroid use in ARDS should adhere to the principle of “early initiation, appropriate dosing, and timely de-escalation,” with treatment decisions tailored to disease etiology, phase, and patient-specific risk factors. Although contemporary RCTs and meta-analyses support systemic corticosteroids in select ARDS populations, emerging expert reviews caution against overgeneralization of these findings. In nonviral ARDS, the evidence base supporting corticosteroid therapy remains variable, and guideline recommendations may not apply uniformly across patient subgroups. Thus, treatment decisions should prioritize patient-specific factors and be revisited as clinical trajectories evolve [ 38 ]. Septic shock Septic shock is marked by persistent hypotension requiring vasopressor support despite appropriate fluid resuscitation, reflecting severe infection-related circulatory and metabolic dysfunction. Although advances in critical care have improved supportive management, reported mortality in ICU populations remains high, often exceeding 30% [ 1 ]. Systemic corticosteroids for septic shock have been investigated for > 20 years, primarily based on the concept of critical illness–related corticosteroid insufficiency [ 6 ]. Some patients with severe inflammation and persistent vasopressor dependence develop features consistent with relative corticosteroid insufficiency or reduced peripheral sensitivity to cortisol, which may suppress catecholamine responsiveness. Exogenous corticosteroids are administered to improve vascular tone and support earlier shock resolution. Early clinical interest in corticosteroids for septic shock was largely driven by the 2002 RCT conducted by Annane et al. [ 39 ], who reported a reduction in mortality rate with short-term, low-dose hydrocortisone plus fludrocortisone in patients with septic shock and relative adrenal insufficiency. The 2008 CORTICUS trial [ 40 ] enrolled a broader, less selected septic shock population but reported no mortality benefit with hydrocortisone, although a shorter time to shock reversal was observed. Interstudy differences in patient selection, illness severity, and study design likely contributed to the divergent findings and delayed consensus regarding the use of corticosteroids for septic shock. Contemporary evidence emerged in 2018 with the publication of two large RCTs in The New England Journal of Medicine . The ADRENAL trial [ 14 ] enrolled 3,658 patients with septic shock and compared continuous intravenous hydrocortisone at 200 mg/day with placebo, administered for up to 7 days or until ICU discharge or death. Compared with placebo, hydrocortisone did not reduce the rate of 90-day all-cause mortality. However, among intubated patients, hydrocortisone use was consistently associated with early shock resolution (intervention group vs. control group; median: 3 vs. 4 days; hazard ratio [HR]: 1.32; 95% CI: 1.23–1.41), shortened ICU stay (median: 10 vs. 12 days; HR: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.06–1.23), and shortened mechanical ventilation (median: 6 vs. 7 days; HR: 1.13, 95% CI 1.05–1.22). Notably, these benefits were achieved without an increase in the risk of severe adverse events. The multicenter APROCCHSS trial [ 10 ] conducted in France evaluated a combination of hydrocortisone (50 mg intravenously every 6 h) and oral fludrocortisone (50 µg/day) administered for 7 days in patients with septic shock ( N = 1,241). The rate of 90-day all-cause mortality was lower in the intervention group than in the placebo group (43.0% vs. 49.1%; RR: 0.88; 95% CI: 0.78–0.99; p = 0.03), corresponding to an absolute risk reduction of approximately 6% and a number-needed-to-treat value of 16. The combined therapy also reduced the duration of shock. When considered alongside the results of the ADRENAL trial, these findings suggest that hydrocortisone alone predominantly facilitates hemodynamic recovery, whereas the addition of fludrocortisone confers incremental benefit in select patient populations. However, interstudy differences in study design, background care, and illness severity limit direct comparison and warrant cautious interpretation. A potential explanation is that septic shock may involve not only impaired glucocorticoid signaling but also disturbances in mineralocorticoid activity; adjunct fludrocortisone may improve volume regulation and vascular tone in this context. With the accumulation of randomized evidence, international treatment recommendations for corticosteroid use in septic shock have become more aligned. The Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021 guidelines recommend low-dose intravenous hydrocortisone for adults with septic shock exhibiting vasopressor dependence despite adequate fluid resuscitation [ 4 ]. The guidelines also acknowledge a potential benefit from the addition of fludrocortisone while noting that practical considerations such as drug availability and feasibility may influence implementation. Similarly, the 2024 focused update jointly issued by the SCCM and ESICM provides a conditional recommendation supporting adjunct corticosteroid therapy for septic shock in adults, reflecting a shift toward broader acceptance while recognizing uncertainty [ 24 ]. Recent meta-analytic evidence further confirms this evolving position. A 2024 systematic review and Bayesian network meta-analysis of 19 studies with nearly 96,000 patients compared hydrocortisone plus fludrocortisone, hydrocortisone alone, and placebo [ 14 ]. Combination therapy ranked highest for short-term survival, followed by hydrocortisone monotherapy and placebo. Although corticosteroid therapy was associated with an increased risk of hyperglycemia, no significant excess risk of gastrointestinal bleeding or severe infections was observed. These findings support a survival advantage with combination therapy, but the overall certainty of evidence remains moderate and interstudy heterogeneity limits definitive conclusions. Available trial data and guideline recommendations support the use of hydrocortisone at a cumulative dose of 200 mg/day, administered either as intermittent intravenous dosing or continuous infusion, typically for 5–7 days [ 10 , 14 , 41 ]. When feasible, oral fludrocortisone at 50 µg/day may be added. Corticosteroid therapy is generally considered for patients requiring vasopressor support to maintain target mean arterial pressure despite adequate fluid resuscitation, rather than as part of initial resuscitative management. Available RCTs do not support delaying corticosteroid therapy for adrenocorticotropic hormone stimulation testing, given that clinical benefit appears independent of adrenal responsiveness. Corticosteroids should be perceived strictly as adjunct therapy and should not replace prompt antimicrobial therapy, infection source control, or appropriate hemodynamic resuscitation. Regarding discontinuation, most trials discontinued corticosteroids once shock resolution was achieved and vasopressors were no longer required. Given the relatively short treatment duration, abrupt cessation without tapering is common and does not cause clinically significant adrenal suppression. Nevertheless, a brief taper may be considered in certain patients depending on clinician judgment, particularly when treatment duration is extended or clinical stability remains tenuous. Safety considerations and adverse events (Table 3 ) Although systemic corticosteroids confer clinically meaningful benefits in some patients with critically illnesses, they may lead to various adverse events that require active monitoring in routine ICU care [ 51 ]. The safety profile observed in RCTs reflects carefully selected patients receiving moderate doses for limited durations and may not fully capture risks encountered in more heterogeneous real-world populations. Infection risk Given their immunosuppressive properties, corticosteroids may theoretically impair pathogen clearance, particularly when exposure is prolonged or cumulative. Of greatest concern in patients with critical illnesses are hospital-acquired infections and invasive fungal infections. In major RCTs evaluating short-term therapy—for example, CAPE COD [ 9 ] and DEXA-ARDS [ 12 ], no significant increase was noted in the risk of severe infections. For example, the incidence of hospital-acquired infection in the CAPE COD trial was comparable between the intervention and placebo groups. The aforementioned trials generally restricted corticosteroid exposure to 1 to 2 weeks and excluded patients with marked immunosuppression. Prolonged ICU stay, repeated infectious episodes, and concurrent immunomodulatory therapy may amplify infection risk. Accordingly, unexplained fever, recurrent leukocytosis, and delayed clinical improvement during corticosteroid therapy should prompt reassessment for secondary infection and appropriate microbiological evaluation. Hyperglycemia Hyperglycemia represents the most consistently observed adverse effect of systemic corticosteroid therapy. By promoting hepatic gluconeogenesis and peripheral insulin resistance, corticosteroids often increase insulin requirements in patients with critical illnesses who already exhibit stress-induced dysglycemia [ 14 ]. Across trials, corticosteroid-treated patients required more frequent or higher-dose insulin therapy. Although hyperglycemia itself leads to adverse outcomes, it is generally manageable with structured glucose monitoring and protocolized insulin adjustment. Current ICU practice commonly targets blood glucose levels of 140–180 mg/dL, emphasizing avoidance of both severe hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia [ 42 ]. Neuromuscular weakness Prolonged or high-dose corticosteroid therapy has been associated with steroid-induced myopathy, which may overlap with critical illness–associated neuromyopathy driven by inflammation, immobility, and supportive interventions [ 43 , 44 ]. This combination can complicate ventilator weaning and delay functional recovery. Although short-term corticosteroid therapy is unlikely to independently cause clinically significant weakness, the risk may be amplified in patients receiving neuromuscular blocking agents or experiencing extended ICU immobilization [ 45 , 46 ]. Accordingly, careful attention to treatment duration and continual reassessment of corticosteroid requirement are warranted in these patients. Gastrointestinal Bleeding Corticosteroids may impair gastric mucosal defenses and increase susceptibility to stress-related mucosal injury [ 47 ]. In contemporary ICU practice, routine stress ulcer prophylaxis—most commonly with proton pump inhibitors—appears to mitigate this risk. Consistent with this approach, RCTs demonstrated no substantial treatment-induced increase in the risk of clinically significant gastrointestinal bleeding [ 10 , 48 ]. Nonetheless, patients with a history of peptic ulcer disease or concomitant use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs warrant closer observation. Neuropsychiatric and metabolic effects Neuropsychiatric adverse effects, such as delirium, agitation, mood disturbances, and sleep disruption, may occur during corticosteroid therapy and can be difficult to distinguish from delirium due to critical illness [ 49 ]. In practice, temporal association with corticosteroid initiation or dose escalation may provide a vital clue. Older adults, patients with preexisting cognitive impairment, and patients receiving multiple centrally acting medications may be particularly vulnerable. Metabolic disturbances such as hypernatremia, hypokalemia, and fluid retention should also be anticipated and monitored [ 50 ]. Although short-term corticosteroid therapy is unlikely to cause clinically significant suppression of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, cumulative exposure merits consideration in patients who experience recurrent episodes of sepsis or require repeated courses of therapy [ 6 ]. Overall, careful patient selection, appropriate treatment duration, and continual reassessment remain central to minimizing corticosteroid-related harm in patients with critical illnesses. Conclusion In patients with critical illnesses, the safety of systemic corticosteroids depends not on an intrinsic drug property but on the interplay between patient characteristics, disease trajectory, and treatment strategy. When corticosteroids are prescribed for clearly defined indications and administered at low to moderate doses for limited durations, the risk of major adverse events is generally acceptable. However, these recommendations should be interpreted in light of key trial exclusion criteria, such as marked immunosuppression, uncontrolled infection source, and extensive prior exposure to immunomodulatory therapies—conditions common in ICU settings. Furthermore, most data are derived from studies assessing short-term outcomes, such as mortality and organ support duration. Data on longer-term outcomes, such as secondary infections, antimicrobial resistance, and the cumulative effects of repeated or prolonged corticosteroid exposure, remain limited. In routine care, the risks of these conditions are often influenced by baseline infection burden, antimicrobial intensity, critical illness duration, and invasive procedures—factors that evolve over time and are not fully captured in clinical trials. For the aforementioned reasons, corticosteroid therapy in critical illness should not be regarded as a static intervention initiated and continued as per a predetermined protocol. Instead, treatment decisions require continual reassessment based on daily clinical trajectories and objective response indicators. Worsening metabolic control, electrolyte imbalance, secondary infections, gastrointestinal bleeding, or neuropsychiatric changes should prompt reconsideration of dose and duration. Conversely, early clinical improvement in hemodynamics or oxygenation should trigger timely de-escalation or discontinuation to avoid unnecessary exposure. A lack of meaningful response within the first 48–72 h similarly warrants re-evaluation, particularly in patients at increased risk for adverse events. Ultimately, the appropriate use of corticosteroids in critical illness depends less on rigid algorithms than on informed clinical judgment applied in real time. By integrating trial evidence with careful bedside reassessment, clinicians can optimize the balance between potential benefits and harms and apply corticosteroid therapy in a manner that reflects both the strengths and limitations of the current evidence base.
