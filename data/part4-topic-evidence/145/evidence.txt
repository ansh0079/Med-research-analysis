## Diagnosis, pathology and molecular biology
Cervical cancer may present with no symptoms in its early stages. When symptoms occur, they include unusual vaginal bleeding, heavier menstrual bleeding, pain during sex, and vaginal discharge. Locally advanced cervical cancer (LACC) may cause pelvic and back pain, leg swelling, and urination or bowel symptoms. Histopathological diagnosis of cervical cancer is established either by biopsy or conisation. Biopsy is the diagnostic procedure of choice in large tumours; however, for small tumours for which free margins are plausible or when the disease is not completely visible (endocervical tumours), conisation is the preferred method. The pathology report should provide precise information on stromal invasion, lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI), histological type and grade to determine the most appropriate surgical approach. LVSI is an important prognostic parameter, although it does not influence the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM (tumour–node–metastasis) nor the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) staging systems. Unfortunately, there is a lack of standardisation and variability in the recognition of LVSI. The fifth edition of the World Health Organization Classification of Tumours 2020 classifies epithelial tumours of the cervix into three major histological categories: squamous-cell carcinoma (SCC), adenocarcinoma and a group of other epithelial tumours, which includes neuroendocrine tumours (NETs), adenosquamous carcinoma, carcinosarcoma and adenoid basal carcinoma ( Supplementary Table S1 , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108364 ). 1 The vast majority of cervical carcinomas are associated with human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, whereas 10%-15% are HPV independent. SCC and adenocarcinoma account for 70%-80% and 20%-25% of all cervical cancers, respectively. They are HPV associated in 90.6% of cases. 2 The most prevalent HPV-independent subtype is gastric-type adenocarcinoma with a wide range of morphological features. Less frequent HPV-independent adenocarcinomas are clear-cell and mesonephric carcinoma. All the HPV-independent tumours seem to have a poorer prognosis compared with HPV-associated cervical cancers. Grading is currently not recommended for SCC because it does not seem to have prognostic implications. 1 , 3 The Silva pattern-based classification has been shown to have prognostic value for HPV-associated adenocarcinomas. 4 Its three growth patterns or two-tier classification has demonstrated good reproducibility among pathologists. A particular type of cervical cancer is the neuroendocrine neoplasms. This subgroup includes both NETs (NETs G1, G2 and G3)—exceedingly rare—and poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (small- and large-cell). 1 Neuroendocrine carcinomas can be pure or mixed with other components (squamous or adenocarcinoma) and are mostly associated with HPV. This diagnosis should be confirmed by neuroendocrine markers, including chromogranin A, synaptophysin and INSM1. In addition to mitotic count, Ki-67 is useful in distinguishing the different NET grades. HPV detection can help confirm the cervical origin of a primary tumour. The management of these tumours is not covered by this Clinical Practice Guideline (CPG). Lymphadenectomy has long been considered an integral component of the surgical treatment of cervical cancer. More recently, sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) with pathological ultrastaging was developed. Intensive pathological processing of sentinel lymph nodes (SLNs) with ultrastaging allows identification of up to double the number of cases with positive pelvic lymph nodes (LNs). 5 The essential items for pathological reporting of cervical carcinomas are shown in Supplementary Table S2 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108364 ). 3 , 6

## Recommendations
• A biopsy is mandatory to establish a cervical cancer diagnosis [II, A]. • Conisation should be the preferred diagnostic method when a free margin can be achieved or when the disease is not completely visible (endocervical tumours) [III, A]. • SLNs should be processed according to the ultrastaging procedure with at least five serial sections and immunohistochemistry (IHC) for cytokeratins in at least one level [III, A]. ○ The intraoperative assessment of SLNs by frozen section may miss micrometastases and isolated tumour cells and is, therefore, only recommended for grossly suspicious SLNs [III, A]. ○ Total submission of the LN in 2 mm sections into paraffin blocks is recommended [III, A]. ○ The use of one-step nucleic acid amplification for intraoperative evaluation is not recommended due to the interference with benign epithelial inclusions in pelvic LNs [III, E]. • The use of a standardised structured pathology report is strongly recommended [IV, A]. • The minimal requirements for the pathology report should include tumour size, histopathological type, depth of cervical stromal invasion, minimum thickness of uninvolved cervical stroma, presence or absence of LVSI, LN metastases and parametrial and vaginal involvement [III, A]. • Assessing programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression by combined positive score (CPS) is recommended when it has therapeutic implications [I, A]. 
## Staging and risk assessment
Clinical staging requires pelvic examination (under anaesthesia, when indicated) to determine tumour size, parametrial invasion and vaginal wall involvement. 7 Tumour size, however, can be better evaluated by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). 8 MRI is the preferred imaging modality for assessing the locoregional extension of the primary tumours and has higher sensitivity than computed tomography (CT) for evaluating parametrial, bladder and rectal involvement. 9 Ultrasound (US) carried out by an expert operator has also been shown to have good diagnostic accuracy 10 , 11 and, therefore, can be considered a valuable alternative to MRI for local staging. In locally advanced disease, positron emission tomography (PET)–CT has a sensitivity of 0.88 [95% confidence interval (CI) 0.40-0.99] and a specificity of 0.93 (95% CI 0.85-0.97) for the detection of pelvic LNs and a sensitivity of 0.40 (95% CI 0.18-0.66) and a specificity of 0.93 (95% CI 0.91-0.95) for the detection of para-aortic LNs. 12 PET–CT, when available, should be considered the preferred method for LN staging. In patients with negative imaging results, para-aortic LN metastases may still be present in 8%-12% of cases after surgical staging. Compared with surgical LN assessment, imaging-based evaluation demonstrated a sensitivity of 33.3%-62.5% and a specificity of 92%-95.5% for the detection of para-aortic LN metastases. 13 Therefore, the value of pretreatment surgical para-aortic LN assessment in LACC is still a matter for debate and the object of an ongoing trial. 14 Currently, the FIGO and the UICC TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours staging systems (ninth edition) are used to stage cervical carcinomas ( Supplementary Table S3 , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108364 ). 15 , 16 In 2018, the FIGO system was revised to incorporate imaging (r) and pathology (p) findings, when available, for stage assignment. 16 The FIGO 2018 system allows the use of any available imaging modality (i.e. US, CT, MRI, or PET) to assess tumour size, LN status, and local or systemic spread, according to available resources. The FIGO 2018 system incorporates nodal status and distinguishes between pelvic and para-aortic nodal involvement, aligning more closely with the TNM classification. FIGO 2018 classification differentiates between stage IA1 and IA2 based on microscopic examination of a loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP) or conisation specimen that includes the entire lesion. Stage IB is further stratified in FIGO 2018 by tumour size (IB1 <2 cm, IB2 ≥2 and <4 cm, IB3 ≥4 cm). The goal of the current staging system is to more accurately determine disease extent to guide the most appropriate therapeutic approach, with the aim to avoid multimodality therapies such as surgery and adjuvant radiation [with or without chemotherapy (ChT)], and the associated increased morbidity. 
## Prognostic factors
Tumour size, depth of stromal invasion and LVSI are established prognostic factors to be considered in the management of early-stage cervical cancer. LVSI is associated with an increased risk of LN metastases in patients with FIGO 2018 stage IA1 and IA2 cervical cancer and decreased survival in patients with stage IA2 cervical cancer. 17 All these risk factors are evaluated in the Sedlis criteria (a combination of tumour size, depth of stromal invasion and LVSI), which are used to determine the indication of adjuvant therapy in early stages ( Supplementary Table S4 , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108364 ). 18 For patients with locally advanced disease, the size of the tumour and the number and localisation of LN metastases are prognostic. The prognostic value of histopathology in itself is still controversial. Adenocarcinoma and adenosquamous carcinomas have been suggested to have a poor prognosis with ∼10% differences in 5-year overall survival (OS) rates; 19 however, not all published data agree that pathological type is a risk factor. 20 Cervical small-cell neuroendocrine carcinoma and gastric-type adenocarcinoma have consistently been reported to have a higher risk of presenting with distant spread and adverse prognosis. 21 
## Recommendations
• Pelvic examination and imaging tests should be used to stage patients with cervical cancer [III, A]. • Pelvic MRI for locoregional staging and PET–CT for determining distant and LN involvement are both recommended as the preferred imaging techniques [III, A]. • Endovaginal and/or transrectal US carried out by an expert operator can be recommended as an alternative for the assessment of local staging [III, B]. • Thoracic–abdominal–pelvic CT scan can be considered an alternative method for the evaluation of nodal and distant disease when PET–CT is not available [III, B]. • The UICC TNM classification and the FIGO 2018 system should both be used to determine the stage [IV, A]. • Surgical staging of cervical cancer is not recommended [I, E]. 
## Management of local disease
## FIGO 2018 stage IA1
For patients with FIGO 2018 stage IA1, treatment options include conisation, simple or extrafascial hysterectomy (type A). 22 Cold knife conisation is the preferred technique. 23 LEEP can also be carried out as long as adequate margins are achieved, the specimen is properly oriented and no electrosurgical artefacts or fragmentation occur. Endocervical curettage should be carried out as needed, although this technique is not recommended during pregnancy. If margins are negative for both dysplasia and invasive cancer and the patient does not require fertility preservation, a type A hysterectomy may be carried out. If endocervical margins are positive for dysplasia or cancer, repeated conisation or a type A hysterectomy are valid options, provided that a larger residual tumour is ruled out by preoperative imaging. The decision to carry out LN staging depends on the LVSI status. It may only be omitted in FIGO stage IA1 LVSI-negative cases because the risk of LN metastases is <1%. SLNB is an adequate procedure in LVSI-positive tumours, 24 but if an SLN is not detected on either pelvic side, systematic pelvic LN dissection (PLND) should be completed. Brachytherapy may be a valid alternative to surgery in patients deemed to be inoperable due to poor performance status. In this context, in the presence of LVSI, hypofractionated external beam radiotherapy (EBRT) can be given, taking into account the patient’s clinical condition and performance status. 17 
## FIGO 2018 stage IA2-IB2 and IIA1
The recommended management for FIGO 2018 stage IA2 and IB1 low-risk cervical cancer defined as SCC, adenocarcinoma or adenosquamous carcinoma of any histological grade, with or without LVSI, measuring ≤2 cm and with a limited depth of cervical stromal invasion (defined as invasion of <50% of stromal tissue or to a depth of <10 mm, or both) is simple hysterectomy with LN assessment. This is supported by the phase III randomised controlled SHAPE trial that compared simple hysterectomy with radical hysterectomy using a noninferiority design. 25 The primary endpoint was the 3-year pelvic recurrence rate. The results indicated that there were no significant differences in pelvic recurrence in the simple hysterectomy group compared with the radical hysterectomy group (2.17% versus 2.52%, respectively). Additionally, the incidence of urinary incontinence and urinary retention was lower in the simple hysterectomy group. For FIGO 2018 stage IB2 (tumours >2 and <4 cm) and for IB1 cases that do not meet the above-listed low-risk criteria, radical hysterectomy (type C) by laparotomy with LN assessment is the recommended approach. A phase III randomised controlled trial compared minimally invasive versus radical hysterectomy by laparotomy for early-stage cervical cancer (FIGO 2008 clinical stage IA1 with LVSI, IA2 or IB1). 26 Among 631 patients, the 4.5-year disease-free survival (DFS) rate was 85% for minimally invasive surgery versus 95% for open surgery. 26 OS at 4.5 years was also lower in the minimally invasive group [90.6% versus 96.2%, hazard ratio (HR) 2.71, P = 0.007]. 26 Due to the higher recurrence rate and worse OS associated with minimally invasive surgery, radical hysterectomy by laparotomy is currently the standard of care. The preferred method of LN staging in patients with tumours <4 cm is SLNB, which significantly increases the metastatic detection rate. In the prospective SENTICOL study, in which patients underwent both SLNB and PLND, there was 92% sensitivity of SLNB for pelvic LN staging. 27 In the SENTICOL-2 study, which randomly assigned 205 patients, the DFS at 3 years was 92.0% and 94.4% in the SLNB and PLND arm, respectively. 28 In the single-arm prospective SENTIX study of 594 patients with tumours ≤4 cm, the primary endpoint of recurrence rate at 2 years was 6.1%, noninferior to the reference rate of 7% with a noninferiority margin of 3%. 29 The 2-year DFS and OS rates were 93.3% and 97.9%, respectively. 29 If SLN is not detected on either pelvic side, systematic PLND should be completed. In patients who are not candidates for surgery due to performance status, definitive EBRT with brachytherapy (with or without concurrent platinum-containing ChT) is a valid alternative treatment option. ## Adjuvant therapy
The Gynecologic Oncology Group (GOG)-49 study 30 identified risk factors for recurrence after radical hysterectomy and PLND for clinical stage I cervical cancer. Based on these findings, the GOG-92 trial 31 randomly assigned ‘intermediate-risk’ patients defined by the Sedlis criteria, with a predicted risk of recurrence at 3 years ≥30%, to post-operative pelvic radiotherapy (RT) or observation. The 2-year risk of recurrence was reduced from 88% to 79% with adjuvant RT (relative risk 0.53, P < 0.01), without a significant impact on OS. A more recent analysis 18 that combined data from GOG-49, GOG-92 and GOG-141 demonstrated the impact of adenocarcinoma on the risk of recurrence and presented separate nomograms for more refined estimates of risk of recurrence for both SCC and adenocarcinoma separately. For adenocarcinoma, tumour size was the most important risk factor. 18 More recently, multiple retrospective studies have shown improved survival of the intermediate-risk group after surgery without adjuvant treatment compared with the surgery-only arm of the GOG-92 trial and did not find further benefit from adjuvant treatment. 32 , 33 Such an improvement can be explained by more accurate identification of patients with high-risk prognostic factors, who are excluded from the intermediate-risk cohort in more recent studies. 34 Recently, the NRG Oncology/GOG-263/KGOG 1008 randomised phase III trial on chemo-RT (CRT) versus adjuvant RT in intermediate-risk, early-stage cervical carcinoma after radical hysterectomy and lymphadenectomy did not meet its primary endpoint of recurrence-free survival (RFS); the 3-year RFS was 88.5% in the experimental arm compared with 85.4% in the adjuvant RT arm (HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.408-1.192, P = 0.0927). 35 This was confirmed in another large cohort study in intermediate-risk patients with cervical cancer, where the addition of ChT to RT was not associated with improved OS. 36 In the GOG-109 trial, women with high-risk FIGO stage I-IIA cervical cancer—defined as the presence of positive pelvic LNs, positive surgical margins or microscopic parametrial invasion—were randomly assigned to receive post-operative pelvic RT with or without concurrent cisplatin-based ChT. 37 The addition of concurrent ChT significantly improved outcomes, with 4-year progression-free survival (PFS) increasing from 63% with RT alone to 80% with CRT, and OS rising from 71% to 81%, respectively. 37 The NRG Oncology/RTOG 0724/GOG-0724 phase III trial investigated whether adding four cycles of systemic ChT after standard concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) could further improve results in high-risk patients after hysterectomy. 38 The trial found no improvement in DFS or OS, whereas toxicity was increased, indicating that adjuvant ChT in this setting does not provide additional benefit. 38

More than 40% of patients with early cervical cancer are diagnosed during reproductive age and wish to preserve fertility. 39 Fertility-sparing surgery is a valid oncological alternative to simple or radical hysterectomy for patients <45 years old with tumours ≤2 cm (SCC, HPV-associated adenocarcinomas or adenosquamous carcinomas) who wish to preserve fertility. Consultation with reproductive endocrinology and fertility specialists, as well as discussion in a multidisciplinary tumour board, is recommended before making decisions about fertility-preserving procedures. Before considering fertility preservation, a negative LN status is required initially by imaging and subsequently by pathological assessment by SLNB, in all patients except in FIGO 2018 stage IA1 without LVSI, in which the risk of lymphatic metastasis is very low. If SLN is not detected on either pelvic side, systematic PLND should be completed. For patients with FIGO 2018 stage IA1 disease, regardless of LVSI status, conisation with negative margins is a valid treatment option. For patients with stage IA2-IB1 disease, conisation is a valid therapeutic option as long as negative margins can be achieved and they meet the following criteria: SCC, adenocarcinoma or adenosquamous tumours <2 cm, no LVSI and depth of stromal invasion <10 mm. If the initial conisation has positive margins, a second conisation or simple trachelectomy should be carried out. 40 , 41 For patients with stage IA2-IB1 who do not meet the above-reported criteria for conservative surgery, simple trachelectomy remains an option for fertility preservation. In cases of IB2 tumours >2 cm, platinum-based neoadjuvant ChT has been explored as a strategy to reduce tumour size before fertility-sparing surgery; however, this approach is still considered experimental. 42 
## Recommendations
• For patients with FIGO 2018 stage IA1 cervical cancer, simple hysterectomy (type A) by laparotomy is recommended [I, A]. ○ SLNB should be carried out in patients with LVSI-positive tumours [I, A]. If SLN is not detected on either pelvic side, systematic PLND should be carried out [I, A]. ○ Brachytherapy can be considered in patients not suitable for surgery [III, B]. ○ Hypofractionated EBRT can be considered in patients not suitable for surgery with LVSI-positive tumours, taking into consideration patient clinical condition and performance status [IV, C]. • For patients with FIGO 2018 stage IA2-IB1 cervical cancer, who meet the criteria for conservative surgery (SCC, adenocarcinoma or adenosquamous carcinoma; depth of invasion <10 mm on cone/LEEP or MRI with <50% cervical stromal invasion; negative imaging for metastatic disease), simple hysterectomy (type A) by laparotomy in combination with bilateral SLNB should be carried out [I, A]. • For patients with FIGO 2018 stage IA2-IB1 cervical cancer who do not meet any of the above criteria for conservative surgery or are stage IB2-IIA1, radical hysterectomy (type C) by laparotomy in combination with bilateral SLNB should be carried out [I, A]. • If SLN is not detected on either pelvic side, systematic PLND should be completed in patients with FIGO 2018 stage IA2, IB1, IB2 or IIA1 disease [I, A]. • Radical EBRT and brachytherapy, with or without ChT, can be considered in patients with FIGO 2018 stage IA2, IB1, IB2 or IIA1 disease not suitable for surgery [III, B]. • Adjuvant RT should be considered in the presence of a combination of intermediate-risk factors (tumour size, depth of invasion and LVSI) [I, B]. The addition of ChT to RT does not improve prognosis and cannot be recommended [I, D]. • Adjuvant CCRT is recommended in the presence of any high-risk factor: tumour-positive LNs, positive margins or microscopic parametrial involvement [I, A]. 
## Fertility-sparing treatment
• Fertility-sparing surgery could be considered in reproductive-age patients, with tumours ≤2 cm (SCC, HPV-associated adenocarcinomas or adenosquamous carcinomas) and negative LNs [III, B]. • Consultation with reproductive endocrinology and fertility specialists, as well as discussion in a multidisciplinary tumour board, is recommended before making decisions about fertility-preserving procedures [IV, A]. • In patients with FIGO 2018 stage IA1 disease without LVSI, conisation with negative margins is recommended [III, A]. • Cold knife conisation is the preferred technique [I, A]. ○ LEEP can be considered if adequate margins are achieved, the specimen is properly oriented and no electrosurgical artefacts or fragmentation occur [III, B]. • Conisation is recommended in patients with FIGO 2018 stage IA1 disease with LVSI and in patients with IA2-IB1 disease if the following criteria are met: tumours <2 cm, depth of stromal invasion <10 mm, no LVSI, with negative imaging for LN involvement and when negative margins can be achieved [III, A]. ○ If the initial conisation results in positive margins, a simple trachelectomy or a second conisation can be considered [III, B]. • In patients with FIGO 2018 stage IA2 or IB1 disease who do not meet any of the above-reported criteria, simple trachelectomy is recommended [III, A]. • SLNB is recommended as the preferred method to assess LN status in patients with FIGO 2018 stage IA1 (LVSI-positive), IA2, IB1 or IB2 disease [III, B]. ○ If SLN is not detected on either pelvic side, systematic PLND should be completed in those populations [III, A]. • In patients with FIGO 2018 stage IB2 disease, platinum-based neoadjuvant ChT may be considered to reduce tumour size before fertility-sparing surgery [IV, C]. ○ If there is a response, simple or radical trachelectomy may be considered [III, C].

## FIGO 2018 stage IB3, IIA2, IIB, III and IVA
CCRT with a weekly cisplatin-based regimen has remained the cornerstone of treatment for patients with FIGO 2018 stage IB3-IVA since the publication of five large randomised trials in the late 1990s. Subsequently, a meta-analysis found that the addition of ChT improved 5-year OS and DFS rates by 6% and 8%, respectively. 43 For patients with tumours ≥4 cm, with negative pelvic LN on preoperative imaging, a definitive CRT is the preferred treatment. Radical surgery is an option in sites with experience in surgical management of this cohort and should include radical hysterectomy (type C) by laparotomy with systematic PLND. In the last two decades, technological progress has enhanced imaging and RT delivery techniques. EBRT has evolved from conventional multiple field techniques (3D technique) to intensity-modulated RT (IMRT) and volumetric arc RT (VMAT), with a clear benefit in the toxicity profile. A randomised study in the adjuvant setting comparing image-guided IMRT with 3D CRT showed a lower incidence of grade ≥2 toxicity with image-guided IMRT (57% versus 78% with 3D CRT), quality of life (QoL) improvement with image-guided IMRT, and no difference in tumour control. 44 A meta-analysis comparing IMRT with 3D CRT or 2D RT for definitive treatment of cervical cancer showed no significant differences in 3-year OS and DFS between IMRT and the conventional techniques, with IMRT significantly reducing the incidence of acute and chronic gastrointestinal and genitourinary toxicities. 45 The greatest progress in improving outcomes has occurred through the field of brachytherapy. In the 2000s, the Groupe Européen De Curiethérapie and the European Society for Therapeutic Radiology and Oncology (GEC–ESTRO) working group published recommendations on target contouring, implant technique and treatment planning with MRI-based image-guided adaptive brachytherapy (IGABT). 46 , 47 Following these guidelines, the prospective, nonrandomised multicentre French STIC trial, which compared 2D and 3D IGABT, demonstrated improved local control rates (73.9% versus 78.5%; P = 0.003) with reduced severe toxicity compared with 2D dosimetry. 48 The validation of GEC–ESTRO recommendations was conducted through the prospective, multicentre EMBRACE-I study that evaluated the outcomes of 1341 patients treated with standard CRT followed by MRI-based IGABT for LACC. 49 With a median follow-up of 51 months, the 5-year actuarial overall local control rate across all stages was 92% while the 5-year OS rate was 74%. 49 Key treatment characteristics included a median equivalent dose in 2 Gy fractions (EQD2) delivered to 90% (D90) of the high-risk clinical target volume (HR-CTV) of 90 Gy [interquartile range (IQR) 85-94 Gy] and a median overall treatment time of 46 days (IQR 42-50 days). 49 In the retroEMBRACE study, the use of combined intracavitary/interstitial (IC/IS) brachytherapy was associated with an improved local control in patients with the largest residual tumours (HR-CTV >30 cm3). 50 These outcomes were achieved without an increase in treatment-related toxicity or deterioration in QoL. 51 Given these results, MRI-based IGABT has become the cornerstone of the standard treatment of LACC and should be incorporated in any LACC clinical trial. 52 To improve outcomes in high-risk LACC (IIIB, IVA and/or LN-positive), the combination of standard CRT and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has been evaluated in two randomised phase III clinical trials. The randomised, double-blind CALLA trial evaluated durvalumab versus placebo with and following CRT, followed by durvalumab as maintenance for ≤24 cycles. 53 The study did not improve PFS in a biomarker-unselected population (76% versus 73.3%; HR 0.84, P = 0.17). 53 In the randomised phase III ENGOT-cx11/GOG-3047/KEYNOTE-A18 trial, patients with FIGO 2014 IB2-IIB LN-positive and FIGO 2014 stage III-IVA regardless of LN status were randomly assigned pembrolizumab or placebo plus modern and standard CCRT (IMRT/VMAT and IGABT) followed by maintenance pembrolizumab or placebo for 15 cycles. 54 At 36 months of follow-up, a significant improvement in PFS (69.3% versus 56.9%; HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.56-0.84) and OS (82.6% versus 74.8%; HR 0.67, 95% CI 0.50-0.90, P = 0.0040) was reported in patients treated with pembrolizumab. 54 Pembrolizumab in combination with CRT was approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) 55 and by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) 56 for FIGO 2014 stage III-IVA LACC. 
## RT and systemic ChT combinations
## Neoadjuvant or induction ChT
Two randomised phase III trials reported that neoadjuvant ChT followed by radical surgery was inferior to CRT alone in terms of DFS, although OS was comparable. 57 , 58 In addition, the surgery arm in both trials had the worst toxicity profile. 57 , 58 A meta-analysis of individual patient data from 18 randomised trials failed to demonstrate a benefit for neoadjuvant ChT plus CRT compared with CRT alone. 59 Nevertheless, it highlighted cycle length and platinum dose intensity as key factors influencing the effect of neoadjuvant ChT on treatment outcomes. 59 More recently, the randomised phase III Gynecologic Cancer InterGroup INTERLACE trial compared standard CRT alone with induction ChT (weekly carboplatin–paclitaxel for six cycles) followed by standard CRT. 60 The 5-year OS rate was 80% in the induction ChT plus CRT arm versus 72% in the CRT arm (HR 0.60, P = 0.015). 60 The population was mainly FIGO 2008 stage IB2-IIB disease (86%); patients with para-aortic LN involvement and lower-third vaginal involvement were excluded and only 43% of the enrolled patients had positive LNs. 60 These factors indicate that INTERLACE enrolled a population of patients with LACC with a favourable prognosis. 61 INTERLACE has other important limitations: (i) the trial run for over a decade mainly in UK sites; (ii) a large proportion of patients did not receive IGABT (70%), the accepted current standard of care; and (iii) the mean dose to the cervical tumour was 79.4 Gy at point A, which is notably lower than what can be achieved with MRI-based IGABT and is likely insufficient for optimal local control. 61 Therefore, induction ChT appears to compensate for the suboptimal brachytherapy delivered in a specific subgroup of patients with LACC. 62 Surgical approach should not be regarded as standard in LACC. Nevertheless, in FIGO 2018 stage IB3-IIA2 disease, radical hysterectomy (type C) by laparotomy with systematic PLND can be an alternative option in specialised sites for carefully selected patients who will likely not need post-operative RT. 
## Adjuvant ChT
A large randomised phase III trial (OUTBACK) investigated the impact of adjuvant ChT with carboplatin–paclitaxel for four cycles following standard CRT in LACC. 63 The results showed no impact on OS; 5-year OS was 72% in the adjuvant ChT group compared with 71% in the CRT-only group (HR 0.90, P = 0.81). 63 A meta-analysis on adjuvant ChT after standard CRT showed that those strategies not only failed to significantly improve OS (HR 0.78, 95% CI 0.45-1.33, P = 0.36) and PFS (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.65-1.10, P = 0.22) but also increased toxicity. 64 
## Recommendations
• EBRT with concurrent cisplatin-based ChT [I, A], followed by IGABT [I, A] is recommended as the standard treatment (CCRT) for LACC (FIGO 2018 stage IB3-IVA). ○ IMRT or VMAT is the recommended EBRT technique [I, A]. ○ MRI-based IGABT is recommended as the standard approach for brachytherapy and combined IC/IS brachytherapy is recommended for patients with asymmetric or large residual disease [I, A]. ○ The minimal recommended total EQD2 D90 to the HR-CTV should be >85 Gy and the overall treatment should be delivered within 50 days, with a maximum acceptable extension to 56 days [III, A]. • In FIGO 2018 stage IB3-IIB LACC, induction ChT with weekly carboplatin–paclitaxel for six cycles, followed by CCRT plus brachytherapy within 7 days after conclusion of ChT, may be considered when IMRT/VMAT and/or IGABT are not available [II, B]. • In FIGO 2018 stage IB3-IIA2 LACC, radical hysterectomy (type C) by laparotomy with systematic PLND may be an alternative option in specialised sites for carefully selected patients who will likely not need post-operative RT [IV, C]. • The addition of pembrolizumab to standard CCRT, followed by brachytherapy and by pembrolizumab maintenance for 15 cycles, is recommended in FIGO 2018 stage IIIA, IIIB or IVA LACC, regardless of LN involvement [I, A; ESMO-Magnitude of Clinical Benefit Scale (MCBS) v2.0 score: A(AT)]. 
## Management of recurrent and metastatic disease
Recurrent and metastatic cervical cancer remains a significant challenge, requiring a multimodal treatment approach.

Locoregional recurrence of cervical cancer poses a challenging situation. Treatment options depend on factors such as tumour size, location, previous treatments and overall patient condition. 
## No prior RT
EBRT with concurrent ChT followed by brachytherapy is the standard approach for locoregional recurrence of cervical cancer in patients with no prior RT. 
## Prior RT
Patients who experience isolated, central pelvic recurrence after definitive CRT for locally advanced disease may be salvaged by pelvic exenteration with urinary diversion and stoma derivation carried out in specialised centres. 65 Preoperative evaluation should include PET–CT scan to exclude metastatic disease, including positive LNs, and assessment of clinical and psychological conditions. The goal of pelvic exenteration is to remove disease with negative surgical margins. The extent of pelvic exenteration often requires, in addition to a radical hysterectomy, the removal of the rectosigmoid colon and/or the bladder, a portion of the proximal vagina and the pelvic floor (infralevatoric exenteration). The reconstructive phase of exenteration includes pelvic floor reconstruction, colostomy and/or urinary diversion and the creation of a neovagina. OS at 5 years is close to 50% in contemporary series. 65 Careful patient selection and performance of the procedure by skilled surgeons in high-volume, specialised centres have reduced preoperative mortality. 65 After exenteration, available data do not support the use of adjuvant therapy. When salvage surgery is not feasible or indicated, re-RT may be considered, taking into account the previous dose, treatment fields and anatomical location, and potential side-effects. 66 
## Treatment of distant recurrence and metastatic disease
An algorithm for the management of recurrent and metastatic disease can be seen in Figure 4 . A combination of a platinum agent (carboplatin or cisplatin) and paclitaxel is the standard backbone treatment for persistent, recurrent, and metastatic cervical cancer. 67 The randomised phase III GOG-240 study demonstrated that adding bevacizumab to cisplatin–paclitaxel or to topotecan–paclitaxel significantly increases median OS compared with combination ChT alone (16.8 versus 13.3 months; HR 0.77). 68 Topotecan–paclitaxel did not outperform cisplatin–paclitaxel, even in patients previously exposed to cisplatin. When bevacizumab is added to ChT, higher rates of hypertension, proteinuria, fistulas, and thromboembolic events occurred and patients need to be carefully selected for treatment. 68 Following evidence of activity of immunotherapy in cervical cancer in later settings, anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and anti-PD-(L)1 agents have been investigated in combination with platinum–paclitaxel ChT in the first-line setting. The phase III KEYNOTE-826 study investigated the addition of pembrolizumab to platinum–paclitaxel ChT with or without bevacizumab in patients with persistent, recurrent or metastatic cervical cancer. 69 At the final analysis, an improvement in OS was confirmed with pembrolizumab–ChT versus placebo–ChT in the all-comer population (26.4 versus 16.8 months, respectively; HR 0.63), PD-L1 CPS ≥1 (28.6 versus 16.5 months, respectively; HR 0.60) and PD-L1 CPS ≥10 (29.6 versus 17.4 months, respectively; HR 0.58). 69 In the protocol-prespecified subgroup of PD-L1 <1, no statistically significant improvement in OS was reported (HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.50-1.52). 69 The open-label randomised phase III trial BEATcc (ENGOT-Cx10/GEICO 68-C/JGOG1084/GOG-3030) further confirmed the efficacy of ICIs (atezolizumab) and bevacizumab in combination with platinum–paclitaxel ChT in first-line treatment of recurrent, persistent or metastatic cervical cancer. 70 A significant improvement in PFS (13.7 versus 10.4 months; HR 0.62) and interim OS (32.1 versus 22.8 months; HR 0.68) was achieved with atezolizumab–bevacizumab–platinum-based ChT compared with bevacizumab–platinum-based ChT in a population not selected according to the PD-L1 status. 70 Final OS data are pending. 
## Second-line therapy

Patients who progress after platinum-based ChT comprise a high-risk population with a very poor prognosis. Available medicines in the European Union include the anti-PD-1 inhibitors pembrolizumab and cemiplimab for patients not previously treated with ICIs. Pembrolizumab administered as a single agent achieved an objective response rate (ORR) of 14.6% among patients with PD-L1-positive tumours. 71 In the phase III randomised EMPOWER-Cervical 1/GOG-3016/ENGOT-cx9 study, cemiplimab compared with physician’s choice ChT significantly improved median OS (12.0 versus 8.5 months; HR 0.69, P < 0.001) regardless of the PD-L1 status. 72 Tisotumab vedotin, an antibody–drug conjugate targeting tissue factor, demonstrated an OS benefit compared with single-agent ChT in a phase III trial of patients progressing after platinum-based ChT (∼30% of patients had previously received immunotherapy). 73 The 12-month OS rate was 48.7% with tisotumab vedotin compared with 35.3% in the control group, while median PFS and OS were 4.2 and 11.5 versus 2.9 and 9.5 months, respectively. 73 Trastuzumab deruxtecan, an antibody–drug conjugate targeting human epidermal growth factor 2 (HER2), has demonstrated an ORR of 50%, median PFS of 7 months and median OS of 13 months in a population of 40 patients with advanced or recurrent HER2-overexpressing (IHC 1+, 2+, 3+) cervical cancer. 74 In the IHC 3+ population, ORR was 75% (median PFS and OS not reached). 74 If an ICI has been used in the first line and tisotumab vedotin is not available, single-agent ChT (e.g. paclitaxel, nab-paclitaxel, gemcitabine, vinorelbine, irinotecan, pemetrexed, topotecan and docetaxel) might be considered, 75 carefully balancing the limited efficacy, the toxicity and the patient’s QoL. Best supportive care and early integration of palliative care are important in patients with recurrent and metastatic cervical cancer. Enrolment in clinical trials remains a valuable treatment option. 
## The role of RT in metastatic cervical cancer
Several nonrandomised studies have shown that local RT, particularly after a good response to ChT, can help control residual tumour, reduce recurrence risk and further enhance symptom management. Evidence from multiple studies indicates that a significant proportion of FIGO 2018 stage IVB patients receive local treatment, mainly RT, with positive effects on disease control and overall patient wellbeing. 76 Stereotactic body RT (SBRT) has emerged as a promising option for patients with limited metastatic disease, offering precise, high-dose radiation to metastatic sites while minimising damage to surrounding healthy tissues. SBRT has been shown to improve local control, delay disease progression and enhance QoL in carefully selected patients with oligometastatic cervical cancer. 77 , 78 The COMET trial demonstrated that SBRT can significantly improve PFS and OS in patients with limited metastatic disease by effectively targeting and eliminating metastatic lesions while preserving normal tissue function. 42 In FIGO 2018 stage IVB cervical cancer, particularly in cases with widespread metastases, RT has a palliative function. It is employed to alleviate symptoms such as pain, bleeding and obstruction caused by tumour growth. Hypofractionated RT regimens, which deliver higher doses per fraction over a shorter period, are commonly used to provide rapid symptom relief. 79 
## Recommendations
• In patients with locoregional recurrence who have not received prior RT, pelvic CCRT is recommended [III, A]. • In patients with isolated central recurrence who have received prior RT, pelvic exenteration could be considered [III, B]. ○ Preoperative evaluation should include PET–CT scan to exclude metastatic disease, including positive LNs and assessment of clinical and psychological conditions [IV, A]. ○ When salvage surgery is not feasible or indicated, re-RT may be considered, taking into account the previous dose, treatment fields and anatomical location, and potential side-effects [III, C]. • In patients with oligoprogression, local treatment (i.e. SBRT, surgery or thermoablation) with continuation of systemic therapy can be considered as an alternative to switch treatment [IV, B]. • In patients with persistent, recurrent, metastatic cervical cancer and PD-L1 CPS ≥1, pembrolizumab–platinum–paclitaxel ChT with or without bevacizumab is the recommended first-line treatment [I, A; ESMO-MCBS v2.0 score: 4]. • In patients with persistent, recurrent, metastatic cervical cancer and PD-L1 CPS <1, bevacizumab–platinum–paclitaxel ChT (if not contraindicated) is the recommended first-line treatment [I, A; ESMO-MCBS v2.0 score: 3]. • In patients with persistent, recurrent, metastatic cervical cancer and PD-L1 CPS <1 with contraindication to bevacizumab, platinum–paclitaxel ChT is the recommended first-line treatment [I, A]. • Early integration of palliative care is recommended in patients with persistent, recurrent, metastatic cervical cancer [IV, A]. • In patients with FIGO 2018 stage IVB disease, who have achieved systemic control of disease with systemic treatment, pelvic RT can be considered to increase local control and ameliorate patient QoL [III, B]. • For patients with recurrent and metastatic disease, enrolment in a clinical trial is strongly recommended [IV, A]. 
## Second-line therapy
• For patients with recurrent and metastatic disease who have progressed on first-line platinum-based ChT, treatment options include ○ Cemiplimab, if ICI naive and regardless of PD-L1 status [I, A; ESMO-MCBS v2.0 score: 5; EMA approved, not FDA approved] ○ Tisotumab vedotin [I, B; ESMO-MCBS v2.0 score: 3] ○ Trastuzumab deruxtecan for HER2-positive (IHC 3+) [III, A; ESMO-MCBS v2.0 score: 3] ○ Pembrolizumab for PD-L1 ≥1, if ICI naive [III, B; ESMO-MCBS v2.0 score: 1; FDA approved, not EMA approved] ○ Single-agent nonplatinum ChT [III, C] ○ Best supportive care [IV, A]

## Recommendations
• Treatment impact on health-related QoL must be considered in treatment decision making and discussed with the patient [V, A]. • The use of cervical cancer-specific tools [the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire (QLQ)-CX24], in addition to general questionnaires (QLQ-C30), should be considered to capture relevant data [III, A]. • A comprehensive rehabilitation considering optimisation of renal dysfunctions, nutritional aspects and pain control is recommended before, during and after treatment [IV, A]. • A survivorship care plan considering early and long-term side-effects, such as menopause symptoms control, sexual rehabilitation measures, smoking cessation and psychosocial interventions, should be optimised and implemented [IV, A]. • Implementation of pelvic floor exercises after surgery can be considered to reduce lower urinary tract symptoms [III, B]. • Oestrogen-only hormone replacement therapy (HRT) should be offered after hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy for cervical cancer in premenopausal women [IV, A]. • Continuous combined oestrogen–progestogen HRT or tibolone is recommended after CRT for premenopausal women [IV, A]. • Follow-up visits should be conducted by a physician experienced in the surveillance of patients with cancer [IV, A]. ○ The visits with a complete physical examination, including a pelvic exam and patient history, should be conducted every 3-4 months in the first 2 years, every 6-12 months in years 3-5 and then annually [IV, A]. • Imaging and laboratory tests should be carried out based on risk of recurrence, symptoms or findings suggestive of recurrence and/or persistence of disease [IV, A]. • Pelvic MRI and PET–CT should be carried out at 3 months after completion of CCRT and repeated at 6 months if persistence of disease has previously been reported [III, A]. • In case of suspected tumour persistence, recurrence or second primary cancer, histological verification is strongly recommended [IV, A]. 
## Methodology
This CPG was developed in accordance with the ESMO standard operating procedures for CPG development ( https://www.esmo.org/guidelines/esmo-guidelines-methodology ). All recommendations provided are based on current scientific evidence and the authors’ collective expert opinion. Where recommendations for multiple different treatment options exist, prioritisation is illustrated by ordering these options according to level of evidence (LoE), where equal, by grade of recommendation (GoR) and, where equal, by alphabetical order. The relevant literature has been selected by the expert authors (see Supplementary Table S5 , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108364 , for the search strategy). ESMO-MCBS v2.0 80 was used to calculate scores for new therapies/indications approved by the EMA or FDA ( https://www.esmo.org/guidelines/esmo-mcbs ). The scores have been calculated and validated by the ESMO-MCBS Working Group and reviewed by the authors. The ESMO-MCBS scores and corresponding scorecard links included in this CPG are correct at the time of publication. For the most up-to-date scores, please refer to the scorecards linked in Supplementary Table S6 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108364 ). The FDA/EMA or other regulatory body approval status of new therapies/indications is reported at the time of writing this CPG. LoEs and GoRs have been applied using the system shown in Supplementary Table S7 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108364 ). 81 Statements without grading were considered justified standard clinical practice by the authors.
