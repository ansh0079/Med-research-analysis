## 951 — intensive blood glucose control sulphonylureas insulin type 2 diabetes UKPDS

topicId: `05e665df-a30b-4299-bdf4-db20a29dc7eb`

### Mentor intro
UKPDS taught endocrinology its defining lesson: in type 2 diabetes, early intensive glycaemic control with sulphonylureas or insulin does more than lower glucose — it prevents microvascular complications, and its benefits persist for years after the trial ended (the 'legacy effect'). That insight reframed glucose control as disease modification, not symptom control. The modern nuance: glycaemic control remains central but now shares the stage with cardiovascular-risk-directed therapy (statins, BP control, and newer agents with proven CV benefit) — the UKPDS lesson is necessary, not sufficient.

### Teaching points
- UKPDS established that intensive glycaemic control with sulphonylureas or insulin reduces microvascular complications (retinopathy, nephropathy) in type 2 diabetes, with no excess cardiovascular harm. [[PMID 35638331]] [[PMID 39155282]]
- The legacy effect describes durable long-term benefit from early intensive therapy observed years after trial completion — early glycaemic control 'banks' protection (UKPDS, and analogous findings in hypertension and lipids). [[PMID 37915902]]
- Sulphonylurea- and insulin-based intensification remain evidence-based for microvascular protection, though modern guidelines prioritise agents with cardiovascular benefit (GLP-1 receptor agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors) for patients with established disease. [[PMID 39155282]] [[PMID 35638331]]
- Glucose control sits within a hierarchy: glycaemia management is necessary but must be integrated with blood pressure, lipid, and weight management for maximal cardiovascular prevention. [[PMID 39155282]] [[PMID 35638331]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether sulphonylureas retain a role versus newer agents in early intensive therapy is contested by cardiovascular-outcome data.
- The durability of legacy effects with modern agents is untested at UKPDS timescales.

### MCQ angles
- UKPDS: what intensive control achieved and with which drugs.
- The legacy effect and its clinical implication for early treatment.
- Microvascular versus macrovascular prevention hierarchy.
- Sulphonylureas/insulin in modern treatment algorithms.

### Case hooks
- A newly diagnosed type 2 diabetic patient asks whether early tight control 'matters later'; answer using UKPDS and the legacy effect.
- A patient on metformin needs intensification with established cardiovascular disease; reconcile UKPDS-era evidence with modern agent selection.

### Gaps

## 952 — intensive blood pressure control cardiovascular outcomes systolic

topicId: `92abdb6f-3233-4496-aa38-3b47f0ae87ef`

### Mentor intro
SPRINT re-set blood-pressure medicine: intensive control targeting systolic <120 mmHg reduced major cardiovascular events and mortality versus the <140 mmHg standard in non-diabetic high-risk patients — at the price of more hypotension, electrolyte disturbance, and acute kidney injury. The kidney follow-up taught the second lesson: the AKI signal was largely haemodynamic and recoverable, and intensive control did not accelerate CKD progression on extended observation. The modern skill is patient selection: intensive targets for fit high-risk patients, individualised relaxation for frailty, standing hypotension, and advanced CKD.

### Teaching points
- SPRINT showed intensive systolic control (<120 mmHg) reduced major cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality in non-diabetic high-risk patients, driving guideline targets toward lower goals. [[PMID 31646769]] [[PMID 40902616]]
- The benefit-harm balance of intensive control varies by patient characteristics: absolute benefit concentrates in higher-risk patients, while adverse effects (hypotension, syncope, AKI, electrolytes) increase with frailty. [[PMID 40902616]]
- SPRINT's AKI signal was predominantly haemodynamic and largely reversible; on extended follow-up, intensive control did not accelerate chronic kidney disease progression despite early creatinine rises. [[PMID 30218213]]
- Ideal targets are individualised: intensive (<120-130 systolic) for fit high-risk patients, with relaxed targets for frail elderly, standing hypotension, and advanced CKD. [[PMID 31646769]] [[PMID 40902616]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether intensive targets benefit patients with diabetes (ACCORD's negative primary result) or very elderly frail patients remains patient-specific.
- Optimal implementation (home versus office measurement, titration speed) varies by health system.

### MCQ angles

- The AKI signal: haemodynamic versus structural interpretation.
- Benefit-harm trade-offs by patient subgroup.
- Individualising targets: frailty, orthostasis, CKD.

### Case hooks

## 953 — Intensive care high yield clinical guidelines

topicId: `938cd4dd-e69b-4756-9727-f8a948608696`

### Mentor intro
High-yield ICU guidelines share a signature: they convert physiology into checklists that change outcomes. Ventilator care bundles — head-of-bed elevation, sedation breaks, spontaneous breathing trials, oral care, prophylaxis — reduce ventilator-associated pneumonia in meta-analysis, and implementation science shows the bundle matters less than the strategy that embeds it. The ESICM fluid therapy guideline restricts resuscitation fluids to balanced crystalloids as default and questions starches; the ERS/ATS NIV guideline defines which acute respiratory failure phenotypes (COPD exacerbation with acidosis, cardiogenic pulmonary oedema) benefit from noninvasive ventilation and which need intubation. The through-line: guidelines work when they are specific, physiological, and monitored.

### Teaching points
- Ventilator-associated pneumonia care bundles reduce VAP incidence in meta-analysis; bundle elements (positioning, sedation interruption, oral care, prophylaxis) are the highest-yield preventive practice in the ventilated patient. [[PMID 38028633]]
- Implementation strategy determines bundle success: systematic review identifies education, reminders, audit-feedback, and leadership engagement as the effective components, not the bundle document itself. [[PMID 26276569]]
- The ESICM fluid-therapy guideline positions balanced crystalloids as the default resuscitation fluid and recommends against starch-based fluids, with fluid choice and fluid amount addressed separately. [[PMID 38771364]]
- ERS/ATS NIV guidance: noninvasive ventilation benefits acute hypercapnic COPD exacerbation and cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, but not de novo hypoxaemic failure patterns where it may delay intubation. [[PMID 28860265]]

### Uncertainties
- Which bundle-implementation strategies produce durable adherence beyond the study period is incompletely answered.
- Optimal fluid-amount strategies (restrictive versus liberal) continue to be refined across specific ICU populations.

### MCQ angles

- Resuscitation fluid choice per ESICM.
- NIV indications and failure-recognition.

### Case hooks

## 954 — intensive glucose lowering type 2 diabetes

topicId: `6c4dee81-5160-403c-b55b-9b6f0676872b`

### Mentor intro
Where topic 951 covers what intensive control can achieve, this topic covers what it can cost. The modern synthesis of intensive-versus-standard trials finds a consistent pattern: intensive lowering reduces microvascular events but its effect on macrovascular outcomes and mortality depends on context — with ACCORD's excess mortality in aggressively targeted patients as the cautionary counterweight. Regional meta-analysis adds the generalisability caveat: benefits and harms vary with baseline care quality. The clinical rule that emerges: intensive targets earn their risk in younger, healthier, recently diagnosed patients with support systems; they pay it out in the frail, the elderly, and the long-diagnosed.

### Teaching points
- Systematic review of intensive versus conventional glucose targets confirms microvascular benefit while showing macrovascular and mortality effects are context-dependent rather than uniformly favourable. [[PMID 36718676]]
- The ACCORD-era lesson stands: aggressive intensification in higher-risk, longer-diagnosed patients increased mortality and severe hypoglycaemia — patient selection, not the number itself, drives safety. [[PMID 36718676]] [[PMID 25944874]]
- Meta-analysis across regions shows intensive-versus-standard effects vary with baseline control quality, implying diminishing returns where baseline care is already good. [[PMID 25944874]]
- Severe hypoglycaemia is the central harm of intensification — it drives cardiovascular events, falls, and cognitive decline — and its risk must be weighed against microvascular benefit in every target decision. [[PMID 36718676]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether modern agents (GLP-1 RA, SGLT2i) change the risk-benefit of intensive targets independent of glycaemia is an active research area.
- Optimal de-intensification criteria for older patients are guideline-inferred rather than trial-proven.

## 955 — intensive glycemic targets in type 2 diabetes

topicId: `e93ae704-a9d7-4d77-82e2-7ec2971acf45`

### Mentor intro
The HbA1c number is now a shared decision, not an order: guidelines systematically individualise targets by life expectancy, comorbidity burden, hypoglycaemia risk, and patient preference. Younger, healthier patients aim for near-normal glycaemia (6.0-6.5%); healthy older adults relax modestly (7.0-7.5%); frailty, dementia, limited life expectancy, or severe hypoglycaemia history justify 8.0% or higher — where comfort and safety outrank the microvascular arithmetic. The systematic review of guideline recommendations shows this individualisation is now explicit and consistent across major societies; the craft is matching the target to the person, then matching the therapy's hypoglycaemia risk to the target.

### Teaching points
- Major guidelines individualise HbA1c targets: approximately <6.5-7.0% for healthy younger patients, 7.0-7.5% for healthy older adults, and 8.0%+ for frail patients or limited life expectancy. [[PMID 34379732]] [[PMID 30833929]]
- In older patients, intensive control's benefit-harm balance shifts: microvascular gains shrink while hypoglycaemia, falls, and treatment burden grow, driving relaxed targets with advancing frailty. [[PMID 35614863]] [[PMID 30833929]]
- Guideline recommendations consistently frame targets around patient characteristics (life expectancy, comorbidity, hypoglycaemia risk, preference) rather than a single population number. [[PMID 34379732]]
- Therapy selection must match the target: agents with low hypoglycaemia risk (metformin, GLP-1 RA, SGLT2i, DPP-4i) support intensive targets safely; sulphonylureas and insulin require target relaxation or enhanced monitoring in vulnerable patients. [[PMID 30833929]] [[PMID 35614863]]

### Uncertainties
- Exact HbA1c cut-offs by frailty category vary between guidelines; local protocols differ.
- How to operationalise individualisation in busy clinics (frailty scoring uptake) is an implementation, not efficacy, question.

### MCQ angles

## 956 — intensive LDL cholesterol lowering statin meta-analysis

topicId: `c88d8db7-aa4d-4fb1-8853-cb7b86a07cd1`

### Mentor intro
The Cholesterol Treatment Trialists' meta-analyses established the foundational rule of lipid medicine: cardiovascular benefit is proportional to the absolute LDL-C reduction, in every group studied — men and women, primary and secondary prevention — and there is no threshold below which benefit stops. Intensive lowering therefore means driving LDL as low as practically achievable with tolerated therapy. The modern implementation question: whether moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe matches high-intensity statin alone — meta-analytic evidence now suggests comparable LDL lowering with better tolerability, reshaping the escalation ladder.

### Teaching points
- CTT individual-participant meta-analysis shows statin benefit is proportional to absolute LDL-C reduction and is consistent across sexes and prevention settings, supporting intensive lowering targets. [[PMID 25579834]]
- Meta-analysis supports moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe as an alternative to high-intensity statin monotherapy, with comparable LDL-C lowering and improved tolerability in several analyses. [[PMID 39550238]]
- Intensive LDL targets in very-high-risk ASCVD (e.g. <1.4 mmol/L or <55 mg/dL per ESC) operationalise the proportional-benefit rule with combination therapy as needed. [[PMID 25579834]] [[PMID 39550238]]
- Tolerability drives adherence: switching to combination moderate-intensity therapy is preferable to statin discontinuation when high-intensity doses cause adverse effects. [[PMID 39550238]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether combination moderate-intensity therapy reduces hard cardiovascular outcomes identically to high-intensity monotherapy awaits dedicated outcome trials.
- Optimal LDL floors and very-long-term safety of very low LDL remain reassuring but observational.

### MCQ angles

## 957 — Intermediate-risk pulmonary embolism reperfusion

topicId: `6685743b-3692-4075-a7fb-f35014c73514`

### Mentor intro
Intermediate-risk (submassive) pulmonary embolism — haemodynamically stable but with RV dysfunction or troponin elevation — is the discipline's judgement call: anticoagulation alone has a low but real early mortality, while systemic thrombolysis prevents haemodynamic collapse but at a cost of major bleeding and intracranial haemorrhage. PEITHO quantified the trade: thrombolysis averted deterioration but with roughly 2% intracranial bleeding. Meta-analyses frame the modern answer: anticoagulation for most, rescue thrombolysis for the 5-10% who deteriorate, and catheter-directed therapy as the evolving middle path — promising lower bleeding, still awaiting definitive outcome trials.

### Teaching points
- Meta-analysis of intermediate-risk PE shows thrombolysis reduces early clinical deterioration and recurrent PE compared with anticoagulation alone, at the cost of significantly more major bleeding. [[PMID 26384442]]
- The PEITHO-era trade-off defines practice: most intermediate-risk patients are managed with anticoagulation plus close monitoring, with rescue thrombolysis reserved for haemodynamic deterioration. [[PMID 26384442]] [[PMID 37855802]]
- Network and pairwise meta-analyses position catheter-directed thrombolysis between systemic lysis and anticoagulation — lower bleeding than systemic lysis, but without definitive outcome superiority. [[PMID 37855802]] [[PMID 37336568]]
- Risk markers (RV dysfunction on echo/CT, elevated troponin/BNP, PESI class) identify the intermediate-risk category but do not yet reliably identify who benefits from upfront reperfusion. [[PMID 37855802]] [[PMID 26384442]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether catheter-directed therapy improves hard outcomes versus anticoagulation alone is the subject of ongoing trials.
- Optimal selection of intermediate-high-risk patients ('the grey zone') for upfront reperfusion is unresolved.

### MCQ angles

## 958 — Intermittent Haemodialysis: Principles and Adequacy

topicId: `367f56a6-5c57-4dba-8add-40608825f40c`

### Mentor intro
Intermittent haemodialysis works by the physics of diffusion and convection: blood and dialysate flow past a semipermeable membrane in counter-current, clearing small solutes in proportion to time, surface area, and concentration gradient. Adequacy is measured — Kt/V (clearance x time over volume) targeting around 1.2-1.4 per session thrice-weekly, supplemented by the uraemic-solute view that phosphate and middle molecules clear differently from urea. The modern refinement is individualisation: incremental regimens for residual renal function and session tolerance, recognising that the number is a guide, not a verdict. And the commonest session-level complication, intradialytic hypotension, reflects the fine balance between ultrafiltration demand and cardiovascular compensation.

### Teaching points
- IHD adequacy is quantified by Kt/V (spKt/V target ≥1.2 per session, with minimum delivered dose-equivalent of 1.4 by single-pool measures), supplemented by assessment of phosphate, fluid, and middle-molecule clearance. [[PMID 34595991]] [[PMID 27578429]]
- Uremic solute kinetics differ by size and protein-binding: small solutes like urea clear readily, while phosphate and larger 'middle molecules' need longer or more frequent sessions — the rationale for individualised prescriptions. [[PMID 27578429]]
- Incremental haemodialysis — starting with fewer/shorter sessions when residual kidney function remains — is supported as an individualised adequacy strategy rather than a fixed thrice-weekly default. [[PMID 34595991]]
- Intradialytic hypotension results from the interaction of ultrafiltration rate, refilling capacity, and autonomic/vascular compensation; it associates with adverse long-term outcomes and requires active prevention (dialysate temperature, UF profiling, sodium modelling, midodrine). [[PMID 33391741]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether higher session frequency (short daily) improves hard outcomes beyond adequacy numbers is debated.
- Optimal incremental-dialysis protocols and their long-term safety are under study.

### MCQ angles
- Kt/V: calculation logic, targets, and pitfalls.
- Solute-size classes and their clearance implications.
- Incremental dialysis rationale.
- Intradialytic hypotension: mechanisms and countermeasures.

### Case hooks
- A patient's spKt/V is 1.05 thrice-weekly; diagnose the adequacy shortfall and redesign the prescription.
- A patient repeatedly cramps and hypotends mid-session with high interdialytic gains; apply the IDH prevention toolkit.

### Gaps

### Sources

## 959 — Intermittent haemodialysis vs CRRT: selection criteria in critically ill

topicId: `a443c183-b42b-418d-8074-9804fd06994b`

### Mentor intro
The IHD-versus-CRRT debate has matured from ideology to physiology: meta-analyses show no mortality difference between modalities in critically ill AKI, so the choice rests on the patient's haemodynamic tolerance, metabolic burden, and practical constraints. CRRT offers slow, continuous solute and fluid removal — kinder to unstable circuits, better for severe catabolism and raised intracranial pressure, but resource-heavy and anticoagulation-hungry. IHD is efficient, available, and frees the patient between sessions, but tolerates less instability. Hybrid extended-daily dialysis splits the difference, and meta-analysis finds it comparable to CRRT — a legitimate middle path.

### Teaching points
- No mortality difference between IHD and CRRT has been shown in meta-analyses of critically ill AKI; modality selection is therefore physiological and practical, not evidence-dictated. [[PMID 31695978]] [[PMID 36723298]]
- CRRT is favoured for haemodynamic instability, severe fluid overload needing gentle removal, high catabolic states, and raised intracranial pressure; IHD suits stable patients and resource-constrained settings. [[PMID 36723298]] [[PMID 31695978]]
- Extended daily (hybrid) dialysis shows comparable outcomes to CRRT in meta-analysis, offering slower clearance with intermittent logistics — a legitimate alternative where expertise exists. [[PMID 25843704]]
- Practical factors — anticoagulation needs, nursing ratio, vascular access, cost, and the ability to interrupt for procedures — legitimately shape modality choice alongside physiology. [[PMID 36723298]] [[PMID 31695978]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether CRRT's haemodynamic gentleness translates to renal-recovery benefit is unproven.
- Optimal modality-switching protocols during a single AKI episode are not standardised.

### MCQ angles
- The no-mortality-difference evidence base.
- Physiological selection criteria for each modality.
- Hybrid/extended-daily dialysis positioning.
- Practical constraints in modality choice.

### Case hooks
- A septic shock patient with AKI, on high-dose noradrenaline, needs RRT; select and justify the modality.
- A stable post-surgical AKI patient needs RRT in a unit without CRRT capacity; assess IHD candidacy and precautions.

### Gaps
- The retained evidence is review/meta-analysis level; verify selection-criteria details against the current KDIGO AKI guideline before approval.

## 960 — interstitial lung disease

topicId: `06864cad-3a53-4663-a601-92e6236f68d0`

### Mentor intro
Interstitial lung disease is an umbrella, not a diagnosis: the idiopathic interstitial pneumonias, connective-tissue-disease-related ILD, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, and drug-induced disease share radiological patterns but diverge in treatment and prognosis. The modern craft is pattern recognition followed by an aetiology hunt — exposures, autoimmunity, drugs — because the HRCT pattern organises everything: UIP suggests IPF after cause exclusion, while NSIP, organising pneumonia, and HP patterns point to specific treatable entities. Two contemporary frames matter: interstitial lung abnormalities on incidental CT, common and mostly indolent but a marker of future progression; and interstitial pneumonia with autoimmune features, the research category for patients who fall between rheumatology and respiratory clinics.

### Teaching points
- ILD requires a systematic aetiology work-up — exposures (hypersensitivity pneumonitis), connective tissue disease serology, drug history, and family history — because treatment is cause-specific. [[PMID 30695649]] [[PMID 32649920]]
- HRCT patterns organise ILD diagnosis: UIP pattern strongly suggests IPF (after cause exclusion), while NSIP, organising pneumonia, and hypersensitivity patterns point to specific treatable entities. [[PMID 37505195]] [[PMID 40358445]]
- The progressive-pulmonary-fibrosis concept unites fibrosing ILDs by trajectory rather than label: worsening symptoms, physiology, or imaging within a year on appropriate therapy triggers antifibrotic treatment (see topic 909). [[PMID 37156412]]
- Non-IPF ILDs with inflammatory phenotypes — CTD-ILD and chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis — have immunomodulatory options (mycophenolate, azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, rituximab) that IPF lacks. [[PMID 35938712]]

### Uncertainties
- Surveillance intervals and treatment thresholds for ILAs are not standardised.
- Whether IPAF should be treated as inflammatory or fibrotic disease (or both sequentially) is unresolved.

### MCQ angles
- ILD aetiology work-up sequence.
- HRCT pattern recognition and diagnostic weight.
- Antifibrotic versus immunosuppressive selection by ILD type.
- ILA recognition and IPAF classification.

### Case hooks
- An incidental CT shows subpleural reticulation in a 70-year-old; distinguish ILA from clinical ILD and plan surveillance.
- A patient has UIP-pattern ILD with positive ANA but no CTD criteria; discuss the IPAF framework and treatment options.

### Gaps
- The retained evidence is heterogeneous (position paper, research classification, imaging and treatment reviews); verify ILD-specific guideline management against ATS/ERS documents before approval.
