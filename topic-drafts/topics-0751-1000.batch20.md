## 941 — Inflammatory vs mechanical back pain: red flags, MRI indications, AS vs degenerative

topicId: `1f016fc1-f8cf-4ddb-83b9-5700d30584a4`

### Mentor intro
Most low back pain is mechanical and self-limiting; the clinician's job is to find the small fraction that is not. Red flags — cancer history, fever, weight loss, night pain, neurological deficit, bowel/bladder dysfunction — are the screening grammar, and guideline reviews show which flags are actually endorsed and with what strength. The sobering evidence: individual red flags have weak diagnostic accuracy, so clustering and judgment matter more than any single question. Inflammatory back pain suggests axial spondyloarthritis — age under 40-45, insidious onset, morning stiffness, improvement with activity, alternating buttock pain — where early MRI and rheumatology referral change outcomes, while MRI for uncomplicated mechanical pain correlates poorly with symptoms and invites overtreatment.

### Teaching points
- Most low back pain is mechanical/nonspecific; red flags are the screening tool for serious pathology (malignancy, infection, fracture, cauda equina), and their presence — not pain severity — drives imaging and referral. [[PMID 30252425]] [[PMID 27376890]]
- Guideline reviews show variable endorsement of red flags; individual flags have limited diagnostic accuracy in emergency-department cohorts, so clusters of findings and clinical context must guide decisions. [[PMID 27376890]] [[PMID 31278933]]
- Inflammatory back pain (insidious onset before 40-45, morning stiffness >30-60 minutes, improvement with exercise not rest, alternating buttock pain, night waking) suggests axial spondyloarthritis and warrants inflammatory work-up and appropriate imaging. [[PMID 30252425]]
- MRI is indicated for red flags, suspected cauda equina, infection, or inflammatory disease — not for uncomplicated mechanical pain, where degenerative findings are common in asymptomatic people and correlate poorly with symptoms. [[PMID 31278933]] [[PMID 30252425]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal combinations and weighting of red flags for screening rules is not settled across guidelines.
- When to refer possible axial spondyloarthritis with negative imaging (non-radiographic disease) follows expert frameworks rather than trial evidence.

### MCQ angles
- Red-flag clusters and their limited individual accuracy.
- Cauda equina red flags and urgent-MRI thresholds.
- Inflammatory versus mechanical history features.
- Appropriate versus inappropriate MRI indications.

### Case hooks
- A 38-year-old has 4 months of morning back stiffness improving with activity and alternating buttock pain; contrast with a mechanical-pain presentation and plan the work-up.
- A patient with back pain reports new urinary retention and saddle anaesthesia; outline the cauda equina pathway.

## 942 — Influenza antiviral treatment

topicId: `68adb3c3-9074-4913-8ddd-bd69478665f7`

### Mentor intro
Antiviral treatment of influenza is a timing and selection problem. Oseltamivir remains the workhorse neuraminidase inhibitor, ideally started within 48 hours of symptom onset, with benefit extending beyond that window in severe or progressive disease; baloxavir added a single-dose polymerase-acid-inhibitor option. The 2024 network meta-analyses for WHO guidance rank the options separately for severe and nonsevere influenza — an important distinction because evidence and effect sizes differ by severity. Hospitalised and high-risk patients should be treated regardless of illness duration; the '48-hour rule' is a community outpatient rule, not a hospital one.

### Teaching points
- Neuraminidase inhibitors (oseltamivir oral, zanamivir inhaled, peramivir IV) and the cap-dependent endonuclease inhibitor baloxavir are the available classes; oseltamivir is the standard for most patients. [[PMID 35270708]]
- Treatment within 48 hours of symptom onset maximises benefit in outpatient influenza; hospitalised, severe, or high-risk patients should be treated even beyond 48 hours. [[PMID 35270708]] [[PMID 39804622]]
- Network meta-analysis for severe influenza supports oseltamivir-based regimens as the evidence-backed choice, with other classes positioned by severity and resistance patterns. [[PMID 39181595]]
- For nonsevere influenza, comparative effectiveness across agents is modest; single-dose baloxavir offers adherence advantages in selected patients. [[PMID 39804622]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether combination antiviral therapy adds benefit in severe influenza is untested adequately.
- Baloxavir resistance (PA/I38 substitutions) and its clinical significance are evolving concerns.

### MCQ angles

- The 48-hour rule and its hospital exceptions.
- Agent selection by severity from network meta-analysis.
- Treatment of high-risk and pregnant patients.

### Case hooks
- A hospitalised patient with confirmed influenza is on day 4 of symptoms; decide on antivirals and justify beyond the 48-hour window.
- An outpatient with mild influenza and renal impairment asks about baloxavir versus oseltamivir; compare the options.

### Gaps

## 943 — Influenza: neuraminidase inhibitors, oseltamivir timing, high-risk groups, vaccination

topicId: `4732c532-f0e7-4554-b4a9-9db9361400f2`

### Mentor intro
This topic pairs the two halves of influenza control: vaccination as prevention, and neuraminidase-inhibitor prophylaxis as the pharmacological bridge. Annual vaccination is recommended for high-risk groups — older adults, chronic disease, pregnancy, immunosuppression — and meta-analysis confirms effectiveness against laboratory-confirmed illness, though effectiveness varies by season, match, and group. Chemoprophylaxis with oseltamivir after exposure reduces symptomatic influenza in the unvaccinated and outbreak settings; it complements, never replaces, vaccination. The mature clinical skill is timing: vaccinate before the season, treat within 48 hours, and prophylax only where guidelines indicate.

### Teaching points
- Annual influenza vaccination is recommended for high-risk groups (age 65+, chronic cardiopulmonary disease, diabetes, pregnancy, immunosuppression, healthcare workers); healthy-adult benefit is mainly work-loss reduction. [[PMID 29388196]] [[PMID 32109627]]
- Meta-analysis confirms vaccine effectiveness in high-risk groups, though magnitude varies by season and match — protection is real but partial, which is why high-risk patients still need early antiviral treatment when ill. [[PMID 28481673]]
- Neuraminidase-inhibitor post-exposure prophylaxis (oseltamivir) reduces symptomatic influenza after exposure; network meta-analysis supports NIs over other classes for prophylaxis. [[PMID 39181596]]
- Antiviral treatment should not wait for vaccine protection decisions: vaccinated or not, high-risk patients with influenza symptoms warrant early oseltamivir within 48 hours. [[PMID 32109627]] [[PMID 28481673]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal vaccine composition strategies (egg versus cell versus recombinant; high-dose/adjuvanted for older adults) continue to evolve with effectiveness data.
- Duration and indications for extended prophylaxis in immunocompromised patients are not standardised.

### MCQ angles
- Who should be vaccinated annually and why.
- Vaccine-effectiveness magnitudes and seasonal variation.
- Post-exposure prophylaxis indications and agent choice.
- The treat-don't-wait principle in high-risk patients.

### Case hooks
- An unvaccinated nursing-home resident is exposed to a confirmed influenza case; decide on prophylaxis and its duration.
- A vaccinated COPD patient develops influenza symptoms 36 hours ago; reconcile 'vaccinated' with treatment decisions.

### Gaps

## 944 — inhaled corticosteroid long acting beta agonist asthma SMART strategy

topicId: `17453f73-b51c-4420-b94e-5ed25fbc07b3`

### Mentor intro
SMART — single maintenance and reliever therapy — is the most important asthma strategy shift of the GINA era: one inhaler, low-dose inhaled corticosteroid-formoterol, serves as daily preventer and as-needed reliever, guaranteeing an anti-inflammatory dose with every reliever use. Evidence shows fewer severe exacerbations than traditional separate SABA reliever strategies, and real-world network meta-analysis confirms the benefit in populations trial criteria exclude. Implementation demands patient education — the inhaler counts as both preventer and reliever — but converts asthma care from two-inhaler complexity to a single consistent message: treat symptoms early, and every treatment is treatment.

### Teaching points
- SMART uses a single ICS-formoterol inhaler for daily maintenance and as-needed relief, embedding anti-inflammatory treatment in every reliever dose. [[PMID 34666208]]
- ICS-formoterol as controller-and-reliever is associated with significantly fewer severe asthma exacerbations than SABA-reliever-based regimens across severity strata. [[PMID 29554195]] [[PMID 34536699]]
- Network meta-analysis of real-world populations (often trial-ineligible) confirms SMART's exacerbation benefit beyond RCT settings. [[PMID 34536699]]
- The as-needed-ICS principle generalises: anti-inflammatory reliever therapy (SMART or ICS-containing reliever variants) treats the underlying inflammation, not just bronchospasm — a conceptual break from SABA-only rescue. [[PMID 36702246]]

### Uncertainties
- Which patients do best on SMART versus MART-naive step-up pathways, and long-term steroid exposure comparisons, need further data.
- SMART implementation in health systems with formulary constraints remains uneven.

### MCQ angles

- Patient education points for single-inhaler use.

### Case hooks

## 945 — Inherited arrhythmia syndromes: long QT, Brugada, catecholaminergic VT, ARVC

topicId: `1c2269b2-82ce-4176-a2f7-5d85a08d2b36`

### Mentor intro
The inherited arrhythmia syndromes — long QT, Brugada, CPVT, ARVC — are rare conditions where a single missed diagnosis is a young sudden death, and a single over-diagnosis is a lifetime of restriction. Each has a signature trigger: exertion and emotion for long QT and CPVT, rest and fever for Brugada, competitive sport for ARVC. Diagnosis blends ECG patterns, exercise testing, drug challenge, and genetics — where modern panels increasingly identify pathogenic variants, though variants of uncertain significance complicate counselling. Management combines trigger avoidance, beta-blockade (long QT, CPVT), ICD decisions weighted by risk scores, and family cascade screening.

### Teaching points
- Each syndrome has characteristic triggers — exertion/emotion in long QT and CPVT, rest/sleep/fever in Brugada, sustained exercise in ARVC — and trigger-specific history-taking drives diagnosis. [[PMID 40505939]] [[PMID 26897076]]
- Diagnostic work-up combines ECG (including recovery-phase and high-lead recordings), exercise testing, ajmaline/flecainide challenge for Brugada, imaging for ARVC, and genetic testing with informed counselling. [[PMID 40505939]] [[PMID 27761163]]
- Beta-blockade (preferably non-selective, e.g. nadolol/propranolol) is foundational for long QT and CPVT; ICDs are reserved for high-risk profiles given device-related harms in young patients. [[PMID 40505939]]
- Exercise recommendations are syndrome-specific and increasingly individualised; blanket sport bans are giving way to shared decisions weighing relative risk and psychological benefit. [[PMID 26897076]]

### Uncertainties
- Risk-stratification for ICD in Brugada and ARVC remains imprecise; programme-shock trade-offs in the young are actively debated.
- Management of variants of uncertain significance and genotype-positive/phenotype-negative relatives follows expert consensus rather than trial evidence.

### MCQ angles
- Trigger-specific history for each syndrome.
- Diagnostic tests: drug challenge, exercise ECG, genetics.
- Beta-blocker selection and ICD thresholds.
- Exercise restrictions and family screening.

### Case hooks
- A 16-year-old with recurrent syncope during swimming has QTc 490 ms; outline diagnosis, treatment, and family screening.
- A genotype-positive, phenotype-negative relative of a Brugada proband asks about competitive sport; apply the exercise-restriction framework.

### Gaps

## 946 — initial invasive or conservative strategy stable coronary disease

topicId: `d70f8729-9d0d-4b94-a864-d9565c8269bb`

### Mentor intro
The COURAGE-era question — should stable coronary disease get stents first or medicine first? — was substantially answered by ISCHEMIA: in stable patients without left-main disease, an initial invasive strategy did not reduce hard events compared with conservative management with optimal medical therapy, though symptom relief favoured revascularisation in angina-heavy patients. The clinical translation is a triage skill: left-main, severe proximal multivessel with impaired function, and refractory symptoms despite medical therapy point to the cath lab; everything else starts with optimal medical therapy — antiplatelets, statins, beta-blockade, risk-factor control — and escalates by symptoms, not by stenosis percentages alone.

### Teaching points
- In stable coronary disease without left-main or equivalent anatomy, an initial conservative strategy with optimal medical therapy is event-equivalent to routine invasive management (ISCHEMIA-era evidence). [[PMID 37407216]] [[PMID 29231917]]
- Revascularisation retains clear roles: left-main disease, proximal multivessel disease with reduced function, and symptom relief when angina persists despite medical therapy. [[PMID 37407216]] [[PMID 33944871]]
- Optimal medical therapy is itself prognostic: antiplatelet therapy, high-intensity statins, blood-pressure and glycaemic control, beta-blockers for angina, and smoking cessation modify outcomes independent of revascularisation. [[PMID 29231917]] [[PMID 33944871]]
- Symptom burden, not stenosis percentage alone, should drive escalation decisions in stable disease; ischaemia-guided selection refines who benefits from the cath lab. [[PMID 33944871]] [[PMID 37407216]]

### Uncertainties
- Long-term (>10 year) outcomes of initial conservative versus invasive strategies are still accumulating.
- Whether modern high-risk plaque imaging should redirect patients to early revascularisation is under study.

### MCQ angles

## 947 — inotrope use

topicId: `3d5a1308-b59c-4da5-a92e-16f06cf813b3`

### Mentor intro
Inotropes buy time in shock at a price: increased myocardial oxygen demand, arrhythmias, and — in the dopamine tradition — harm shown in trials. The modern grammar: norepinephrine is the vasopressor backbone of cardiogenic shock (renin-angiotensin support with the least tachyarrhythmia), dobutamine adds inotropy when output remains inadequate, and levosimendan or milrinone offer phosphodiesterase/calcium-sensitising alternatives without catecholamine load. Epinephrine and dopamine are de-emphasised after outcome signals. Inotropes are a bridge — to revascularisation, recovery, or temporary mechanical support — not a destination; the toughest modern skill is recognising when they merely prolong dying.

### Teaching points
- Norepinephrine is the first-line vasopressor in cardiogenic shock, providing peripheral vascular support with less tachycardia and arrhythmia than dopamine or epinephrine. [[PMID 38391010]] [[PMID 37489740]]
- Dobutamine is the standard inotrope add-on for low cardiac output; its beta-agonism increases contractility at the cost of oxygen demand and arrhythmia risk, warranting ECG and filling-pressure monitoring. [[PMID 37489740]] [[PMID 38391010]]
- Non-catecholamine options — levosimendan (calcium sensitiser, active metabolites) and milrinone (PDE-3 inhibitor) — suit catecholamine-resistant or beta-blocked patients but cause vasodilatation and hypotension. [[PMID 38391010]]
- Inotropes and vasopressors bridge to definitive therapy — revascularisation, recovery, or escalation to temporary mechanical circulatory support in refractory shock. [[PMID 32682486]] [[PMID 38391010]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether levosimendan improves outcomes over dobutamine in cardiogenic shock remains unproven at trial level.
- Optimal timing and patient selection for mechanical support escalation is centre-dependent and incompletely evidence-based.

### MCQ angles
- Vasopressor selection in cardiogenic shock and why dopamine lost favour.
- Dobutamine pharmacology, monitoring, and arrhythmia risk.
- Levosimendan and milrinone: mechanisms and hypotension caveat.
- The bridge concept: when inotropes yield to mechanical support.

### Case hooks
- A post-MI patient has cold peripheries, BP 80/50, and lactate 4 mmol/L; design the initial vasopressor/inotrope strategy.
- A shock patient on escalating dobutamine develops ventricular ectopy; outline the reassessment and alternatives.

### Gaps
- The retained abstracts lack the primary DOREMI/dopamine trials and SCAI shock-staging consensus; verify shock-staging definitions before approval.

## 948 — Insulinoma: fasting hypoglycaemia, 72-hour fast, C-peptide, surgical resection

topicId: `2ef9f34a-5864-4416-91f3-95a107f2722c`

### Mentor intro
Fasting hypoglycaemia with endogenous hyperinsulinaemia is insulinoma until proven otherwise: the classic diagnostic triad (Whipple's) of symptoms with low glucose, relief with glucose, and — from the supervised 72-hour fast — inappropriately elevated insulin, C-peptide, and proinsulin during hypoglycaemia. The C-peptide is the discriminator that splits the differential: endogenous hyperinsulinaemia (insulinoma, sulfonylurea use) versus exogenous insulin (C-peptide suppressed) and IGF-2-mediated hypoglycaemia. Definitive treatment is surgical resection after localisation — the majority are benign solitary adenomas — with medical bridging (diazoxide, frequent feeds) for the unresectable minority.

### Teaching points
- The supervised 72-hour fast is the reference diagnostic test: symptomatic hypoglycaemia with inappropriately elevated insulin, C-peptide, and proinsulin confirms endogenous hyperinsulinemic hypoglycaemia. [[PMID 28381450]] [[PMID 36459334]]
- C-peptide and insulin antibodies discriminate the causes: elevated C-peptide indicates insulinoma or sulfonylurea ingestion; suppressed C-peptide indicates exogenous insulin; insulin antibodies suggest autoimmune hypoglycaemia. [[PMID 36459334]] [[PMID 28381450]]
- Sulfonylurea screen (blood or urine) is essential before attributing hyperinsulinaemic hypoglycaemia to insulinoma; the biochemical profiles overlap. [[PMID 36459334]]
- Treatment of choice is surgical resection of the localised lesion (most insulinomas are benign and solitary); preoperative localisation and intraoperative glucose monitoring guide the operation. [[PMID 28381450]] [[PMID 36459334]]

### Uncertainties
- Localisation strategy for occult insulinomas (multiphasic CT, MRI, endoscopic ultrasound, selective arterial calcium stimulation) follows centre algorithms without comparative-trial evidence.
- Management of malignant or metastatic insulinoma (diazoxide, everolimus, peptide-receptor therapy) is individualised.

### MCQ angles

- C-peptide and antibody interpretation in the hypoglycaemia differential.
- Sulfonylurea screen necessity.
- Surgical indications and preoperative localisation.

### Case hooks

- An insulinoma is suspected but CT shows no lesion; outline the escalating localisation pathway.

### Gaps
- WEAK EVIDENCE BASE: only two sources, both reviews. The retained abstracts lack insulinoma surgical-outcome series; verify resection cure rates and localisation algorithms before approval.

### Sources

## 949 — Insulin pharmacokinetics: basal, prandial, premixed types - dosing and switching

topicId: `f58e0a92-e2a7-4975-a726-35943218dc0c`

### Mentor intro
Insulin prescribing is applied pharmacokinetics: the basal insulins (glargine, detemir, degludec) provide flat, peakless coverage tuned by duration — degludec's ultra-long profile smoothing day-to-day variability — while prandial analogues (lispro, aspart, glulisine) peak fast and clear quickly to match meals. Premixed and co-formulated products trade flexibility for simplicity: fixed ratios suit predictable routines and limited self-management capacity, at the cost of adjustment granularity. Switching between types — the commonest real-world manoeuvre — requires attention to unit-for-unit conversion caveats, overlap timing, and hypoglycaemia vigilance in the transition window.

### Teaching points
- Basal analogues (glargine, detemir, degludec) provide flat 24-hour-plus coverage; degludec's duration beyond 42 hours confers the lowest day-to-day glucose variability and some dosing-time flexibility. [[PMID 31876563]] [[PMID 30639280]]
- Prandial analogues (lispro, aspart, glulisine) mimic physiologic meal peaks with rapid onset and short duration; they are dosed with meals and allow flexible carbohydrate matching. [[PMID 31876563]] [[PMID 30639280]]
- Co-formulations such as insulin degludec/insulin aspart (70/30) combine basal stability with prandial coverage in a single injection — the two components retain their individual pharmacokinetic profiles. [[PMID 27696221]]
- Premixed insulins suit patients with regular meals and limited self-management capacity, but the fixed ratio prevents independent titration of basal versus prandial components — a key switching consideration. [[PMID 30639280]] [[PMID 27696221]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether ultra-long basal analogues reduce severe hypoglycaemia enough to justify cost over glargine varies by population and setting.
- Optimal conversion ratios when switching between basal analogues or to co-formulations are partly empiric.

### MCQ angles
- Onset/peak/duration tables for basal and prandial analogues.
- Degludec pharmacology and variability advantage.
- Premixed versus basal-bolus trade-offs.

## 950 — Insulin pump therapy CSII: closed-loop systems, patient selection, sick day rules

topicId: `36b5a60d-d3c5-4afb-a9a2-366dfbc53129`

### Mentor intro
Insulin pump therapy — continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion — delivers basal-bolus physiology without injections, and its modern form is converging with continuous glucose monitoring into hybrid closed-loop systems that algorithmically adjust delivery. Candidate selection matters more than the hardware: motivated patients (or parents) able to count carbohydrates, troubleshoot occlusions, and manage the device unlock the benefit; system complexity without skills produces the same glycaemia with more failure modes. Sick-day rules complete the picture: illness, ketones, and pump interruption demand proactive sick-day insulin strategies and never omitting basal insulin — pump suspension plus illness is the classic route to DKA.

### Teaching points
- CSII provides continuous basal infusion with bolus dosing for meals, enabling precise insulin delivery matched to lifestyle; systematic-review evidence supports glycaemic benefit over multiple daily injections in appropriate patients. [[PMID 31833871]]
- Hybrid closed-loop systems link CGM to algorithm-driven basal adjustment, reducing hypoglycaemia and improving time-in-range; systematic review and economic analysis support adoption in type 1 diabetes. [[PMID 39673446]] [[PMID 28585879]]
- Patient selection is the rate-limiting step: carbohydrate-counting ability, device-competence, motivation, and support structure predict success better than any device feature. [[PMID 31833871]]
- Sick-day rules are safety-critical: never omit insulin during illness, monitor glucose and ketones frequently, have an injection backup plan for pump failure, and seek urgent care for vomiting with ketones. [[PMID 31833871]]

### Uncertainties
- Fully closed-loop (unannounced-meal) systems and their real-world performance are maturing.
- Cost-effectiveness thresholds for hybrid closed-loop across health systems are still being established.

### MCQ angles
- CSII versus MDI: indications and evidence.
- Closed-loop components and glycaemic outcomes.
- Candidate selection criteria.
- Sick-day management and pump-failure contingencies.

- A pump patient with gastroenteritis has vomiting, rising ketones, and a suspected line occlusion; outline the sick-day response.
