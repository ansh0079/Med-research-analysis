## 891 — hyponatraemia correction rate

topicId: `fdaf0726-a5b9-4335-b72d-059a6c826082`

### Mentor intro
The correction-rate question in hyponatraemia is medicine at its most precarious: correct too fast and you risk osmotic demyelination syndrome; correct too slowly and observational data now suggest mortality rises. Guidelines recommend limiting the first-24-hour correction increment to prevent ODS, and meta-analytic evidence quantifies the ODS risk associated with overly rapid correction. The practical skill is monitoring: checking sodium frequently enough to detect an over-correction while it is still reversible, with re-lowering strategies ready — because the correction limit is a ceiling, not a target.

### Teaching points
- Guidelines recommend limiting correction of severe hyponatraemia in the first 24 hours to prevent osmotic demyelination syndrome, yet recent evidence suggests slower correction rates are associated with increased mortality. [[PMID 39556338]]
- A systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the association between overly rapid sodium correction and osmotic demyelination syndrome in hospitalised adults. [[PMID 39967825]]

### Uncertainties
- The exact mortality-optimal correction band sits within a contested zone between ODS risk at the top and under-correction harm at the bottom.
- Whether desmopressin-based re-lowering protocols improve outcomes is supported by physiology more than trials.

### MCQ angles
- First-24-hour correction limits and their rationale.
- ODS pathophysiology and clinical presentation.
- Over-correction rescue: stop treatment, desmopressin, D5W.
- Monitoring cadence and who needs the ICU.

### Case hooks
- A patient's sodium rises 14 mmol in 20 hours during treatment; outline the rescue protocol.
- You inherit a correcting hyponatraemia with infrequent monitoring; redesign the surveillance plan.

### Gaps
- Exact numerical limits by guideline, the re-lowering algorithm and ODS-treatment evidence are not covered by the retained abstracts; verify against the European guideline and US expert panel before approval.

## 892 — Hypoparathyroidism: post-surgical causes, calcium replacement, PTH replacement therapy

topicId: `c07eda2a-ad46-4bb8-a28d-8305bd55dca5`

### Mentor intro
Hypoparathyroidism is defined by hypocalcaemia, hyperphosphataemia and inadequate PTH — usually acquired surgically after thyroid or parathyroid operations, but with an important medical differential (autoimmune polyglandular syndromes, activating calcium-sensor mutations). Management has historically meant calcium plus active vitamin D in large doses, trading hypercalciuria and renal-stone risk against symptom control. The post-2022 era adds PTH-replacement therapy — the first hormone-replacement approach that mimics physiology — alongside updated guidance on causes, complications and chronic management.

### Teaching points
- Hypoparathyroidism is characterised by hypocalcemia, hyperphosphatemia and inadequate PTH levels or function; the most common nonsurgical causes are reviewed, with autoimmune disease prominent. [[PMID 30390814]]
- Since the 2022 Second International Workshop summary statement, updates are available in the causes, complications and treatment of adult chronic hypoparathyroidism. [[PMID 38767063]]

### Uncertainties
- Which chronic patients qualify for PTH replacement versus conventional therapy is being refined.
- Long-term renal and neuropsychological outcomes of conventional large-dose therapy remain concerns.

### MCQ angles

- Surgical versus medical causes.
- Calcium plus calcitriol management and its renal complications.
- PTH-replacement therapy positioning.

### Case hooks

## 893 — Hypophosphataemia: FGF-23 excess, X-linked hypophosphataemia, burosumab treatment

topicId: `9a2dfc65-56fc-47bb-8b3f-aefd69809259`

### Mentor intro
Hypophosphataemia becomes a bone disease when FGF23 drives it. FGF23 — the phosphate-wasting hormone acting with Klotho — suppresses tubular phosphate reabsorption and blunts vitamin D activation; when PHEX mutations let it run unchecked, the result is X-linked hypophosphataemia: the commonest inherited phosphate-wasting disorder, with rickets, lower-limb deformity, dental defects and disproportionate short stature in children and persistent complications in adults. Burosumab, the anti-FGF23 monoclonal antibody, converted XLH from a high-burden phosphate-and-burider regimen into targeted therapy aimed at the mechanism itself.

### Teaching points
- X-linked hypophosphataemia is the most common cause of inherited phosphate wasting, causing rickets, lower-limb deformities, pain, poor dental mineralisation and disproportionate short stature in children. [[PMID 31068690]]
- FGF23, with its co-receptor Klotho, regulates phosphate reabsorption and vitamin D hydroxylation in the renal proximal tubules; the most common FGF23-mediated hypophosphatemia is XLH. [[PMID 31485552]]
- XLH is caused by pathogenic PHEX variants with X-linked dominant inheritance, producing metabolic bone disease through dysregulated FGF23. [[PMID 40295317]]

### Uncertainties
- Long-term adult XLH management and burosumab continuation criteria are still being defined.
- FGF23 measurement standardisation limits its use as a routine diagnostic marker.

### MCQ angles
- FGF23 physiology and the PHEX-FGF23 axis.
- XLH inheritance and clinical spectrum across ages.
- Burosumab mechanism and its difference from conventional therapy.
- Refeeding-syndrome versus tumour-induced osteomalacia versus XLH differentiation.

### Case hooks
- A child with bowed legs and low phosphate has high FGF23; confirm XLH and discuss burosumab.
- An adult with unexplained fractures and hypophosphataemia is found to have a PHEX variant; plan management.

### Gaps
- Burosumab dosing schedules, conventional-therapy details and tumour-induced osteomalacia work-up are not covered by the retained abstracts; verify against the full XLH recommendations before approval.

## 894 — Hypopituitarism: causes, axis-by-axis replacement, insulin tolerance test

topicId: `01dddf2f-0052-4bf0-bd39-1a80ed3067e7`

### Mentor intro
Hypopituitarism — deficiency of one or more anterior pituitary hormones, sometimes with posterior involvement — is dangerous less for what it lacks than for what clinicians forget to replace: cortisol deficiency from ACTH loss is the key driver of its excess mortality. Causes span pituitary neuroendocrine tumours, hypothalamic lesions, surgery, radiotherapy and trauma. Management is axis-by-axis and hierarchy-aware: cortisol first, thyroid second (never thyroxine before steroids), then sex steroids, growth hormone where indicated, and desmopressin for posterior deficiency. The insulin tolerance test remains the gold-standard dynamic test of the HPA axis when secondary adrenal insufficiency needs confirmation.

### Teaching points
- Hypopituitarism is deficiency of one or more anterior pituitary hormones and is associated with excess mortality, cortisol deficiency from ACTH loss being a key risk factor. [[PMID 27041067]]
- Updated reviews cover the aetiologies of hypopituitarism — including pituitary neuroendocrine tumours, hypothalamic lesions and genetic mutations — and current diagnostic and management approaches. [[PMID 39458112]]

### Uncertainties
- The insulin tolerance test's role is narrowing as safer stimulation tests gain ground, but equivalence in all scenarios is debated.
- Growth-hormone replacement in older adults and after cancer remains individualised.

### MCQ angles
- Replacement hierarchy: cortisol before thyroid hormone.
- Dynamic testing: insulin tolerance test, synacthen test, and their limitations.
- Axis-by-axis clinical clues and biochemistry.
- Sick-day rules and adrenal-crisis prevention education.

### Case hooks

## 895 — hypothyroidism

topicId: `dfc5a322-0ea1-4d51-b744-75514412ec3d`

### Mentor intro
Hypothyroidism is among the commonest endocrine disorders — prevalence 0.3–12% worldwide depending on iodine intake, commoner in women and older adults — and it touches nearly every organ system, from fatigue, weight gain and cold intolerance to bradycardia, heart failure and, rarely, myxoedema coma. Most cases are primary (Hashimoto's), diagnosed by elevated TSH with low free T4, and treatment is levothyroxine replacement titrated to TSH. The clinical craft lies in the edges: subclinical disease, pregnancy targets, drug interactions and the patient who feels unwell despite normal numbers.

### Teaching points
- Hypothyroidism is a common condition worldwide affecting almost all body systems, with presentations ranging from asymptomatic to, rarely, life-threatening. [[PMID 39368843]]
- Prevalence ranges from 0.3% to 12% worldwide depending on iodine intake, is more common in women and older adults, and untreated disease can cause heart failure and myxoedema. [[PMID 40900603]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether combination T4/T3 therapy benefits a subset of patients remains unresolved against standard T4 monotherapy.

## 896 — Hypothyroidism: TSH targets, levothyroxine dosing, subclinical hypothyroidism management

topicId: `f7a75ab5-9583-4d1d-80b6-66a37a0de603`

### Mentor intro
Subclinical hypothyroidism — elevated TSH with normal free T4 — affects up to 10% of adults, is usually autoimmune in origin, and is where guideline divergence is widest: treat the young and symptomatic, watch the old and asymptomatic, and agonise over everyone in between. TSH targets on levothyroxine similarly depend on age and context, and older-adult dosing starts low and titrates slowly to avoid atrial fibrillation and fractures. The evidence tension is real: mild biochemical abnormality versus modest symptom burden, with cardiovascular-risk signals that have not resolved the treatment question.

### Teaching points
- Subclinical hypothyroidism — elevated TSH with normal free T4 — affects up to 10% of the adult population and is most often caused by autoimmune thyroiditis. [[PMID 31287527]]
- Subclinical hypothyroidism, defined as TSH above the reference range (generally 4.5 mIU/L or higher) with normal free T4, is frequently found in older individuals, and international guidelines differ on management. [[PMID 34953533]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether levothyroxine treatment of subclinical disease improves cardiovascular or cognitive outcomes is not established for most subgroups.
- The TSH threshold for treating older adults is a genuine guideline discordance zone.

## 897 — Hypoventilation from central CNS depressants

topicId: `f218c4be-1f13-452f-85e9-52ed8e7e7e17`

### Mentor intro
Central hypoventilation from CNS depressants is an epidemic-shaped emergency: opioids suppress the respiratory drive at the brainstem, producing a characteristic slow, shallow breathing pattern that progresses to hypoxia-mediated injury. Overdose management centres on airway support and naloxone reversal — titrated to restore ventilation, not full alertness — while a growing literature examines non-opioid respiratory stimulants as adjuncts. In the perioperative and chronic-pain settings the same physiology operates more insidiously, compounded by obesity hypoventilation and benzodiazepine co-exposure, making screening and monitoring the real prevention.

### Teaching points
- Opioid overdose occurs when CNS and respiratory drive are suppressed through opioid-receptor action, and acute management strategies in emergency and critical care are updated in a dedicated review. [[PMID 28952972]]
- Opioid-related adverse effects include respiratory depression, and countering opioid-induced respiratory depression with non-opioid respiratory stimulants is an active research area. [[PMID 32089833]]
- Obesity hypoventilation syndrome is underdiagnosed and carries significant perioperative risk through heightened sensitivity to CNS depressants, warranting screening and adapted management. [[PMID 39274213]]

### Uncertainties
- Routine naloxone co-prescribing impact and the optimal monitoring technology for ward-based opioid use remain debated.
- Respiratory-stimulant adjuncts are investigational rather than standard care.

### MCQ angles
- Opioid toxidrome recognition and naloxone dosing/titration.
- Ventilatory support principles while reversing.
- High-risk combinations: opioids plus benzodiazepines, alcohol, OHS.
- Postoperative monitoring thresholds after opioid-based anaesthesia.

### Case hooks

- An obese OHS patient is prescribed postoperative opioids; design the safe-prescribing and monitoring plan.

### Gaps
- Naloxone infusion protocols, capnography monitoring standards and sedative-stacking risk data are not covered by the retained abstracts; verify against current toxicology and anaesthesia guidance.

## 898 — IBD in Pregnancy

topicId: `a81f4d39-eeb7-4215-b481-72d80e3f9f42`

### Mentor intro
The single most important fact about IBD in pregnancy is that active disease harms the pregnancy far more than the medications do: quiescent disease at conception predicts a smooth course, while active flares drive preterm birth, low birth weight and pregnancy loss. The therapeutic implication runs against maternal instinct but with the evidence: continue 5-ASA, thiopurines and biologics through conception and pregnancy, adjust anti-TNF scheduling around the third trimester, and avoid methotrexate entirely. Preconception disease control is the intervention that changes outcomes.

### Teaching points
- Ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease are chronic heterogeneous inflammatory conditions of the gastrointestinal tract; BSG consensus guidelines provide adult management frameworks relevant to pregnancy planning and care. [[PMID 31562236]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal anti-TNF drug-level monitoring and third-trimester dosing schedules vary by agent and guideline.
- Small-molecule (JAK/S1P) safety data in pregnancy remain limited.

## 899 — ICD deactivation and advanced care directives: ethical framework, shared decision-making

topicId: `98550199-a629-4879-98e2-742e672adbd6`

### Mentor intro
An ICD that keeps firing at the end of life can turn a peaceful death into a sequence of painful shocks — and deactivating it is legally and ethically permissible, amounting to withdrawal of treatment rather than euthanasia, since the device merely prevents arrhythmia death and does nothing else for the patient. The failure is conversational: patients, families and professionals all report discomfort initiating the discussion, so deactivation conversations happen late or never. Guidance from the Resuscitation Council and cardiovascular societies frames advance-care planning that names the device explicitly, while attitude research maps exactly where the reluctance sits.

### Teaching points
- UK resuscitation, cardiovascular and palliative-care councils jointly recognise the importance of clear, consistent guidance on cardiovascular implanted electronic devices towards the end of life and around death. [[PMID 27277710]]
- ICDs prevent sudden cardiac death but, if not deactivated in the terminal phase, may deliver shocks that pose problems during dying; attitudes of patients, relatives and professionals toward deactivation are surveyed. [[PMID 41589554]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether routine advance device planning at implantation changes end-of-life outcomes is not established.
- Decision-making capacity and surrogate-consent pathways for deactivation vary by jurisdiction.

### MCQ angles

- Practical deactivation logistics and who may consent.

### Case hooks

## 900 — ICD for secondary prevention: post-VF/VT indications and programming

topicId: `4a62116e-d37f-4571-9118-8f3ab43b8316`

### Mentor intro
Secondary-prevention ICDs are the most firmly indicated devices in electrophysiology: survivors of ventricular fibrillation or haemodynamically unstable sustained ventricular tachycardia not due to a reversible cause receive them on the strength of the landmark MADIT-lineage trials showing sudden-death reduction. Modern practice adds two refinements: programming that avoids unnecessary shocks (detection intervals and discriminators matter as much as the indication), and the expanding subcutaneous-ICD option that eliminates transvenous lead complications — with a trade-off in pacing capability that defines who qualifies for which system.

### Teaching points
- Implantable devices are indicated in primary and secondary prevention of life-threatening ventricular tachyarrhythmias; early studies including the landmark MADIT trials established ICD benefit. [[PMID 26910804]]
- ICDs reduce sudden cardiac death in primary and secondary prevention; transvenous implantation carries lead-related complications that the subcutaneous approach avoids, with selection trade-offs. [[PMID 35179615]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal programming parameters (detection zones, SVT discriminators) continue to be refined against shock rates.
- Which secondary-prevention patients are better served by subcutaneous versus transvenous systems is individualised by pacing needs and vascular access.

### MCQ angles
- Secondary versus primary prevention indications and trial lineage.
- Reversible causes that negate the indication (ischaemia, electrolytes, drugs).
- Programming basics: detection intervals and SVT discrimination.
- S-ICD versus transvenous ICD: advantages and limitations.

### Case hooks
