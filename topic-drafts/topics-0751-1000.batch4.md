## #781 Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF): diagnosis and emerging therapies  (cluster `HFpEF diagnosis and emerging therapies`)

**Mentor intro.** HFpEF is the commonest form of heart failure in the community and historically the most frustrating to treat. Diagnosis requires symptoms plus objective evidence of elevated filling pressures â€” echo first, cardiac MRI when uncertainty persists â€” and management has finally gained a disease-modifying option in SGLT2 inhibitors, with GLP-1 receptor agonists emerging for the obesity-driven phenotype and comorbidity control (hypertension, MASLD) doing much of the remaining work.

**Teaching points**
- HFpEF patients (LVEF >=50%) comprise nearly half of those with chronic heart failure, and evidence-based treatment options for this population have historically been limited â€” making practical diagnostic and management guidance essential. [[PMID 37140514]]
- HFpEF is a complex syndrome of diastolic dysfunction and elevated filling pressures with normal ejection fraction, and cardiac MRI contributes to its characterisation where echo is inconclusive. [[PMID 40843509]]
- GLP-1 receptor agonists are an emerging therapeutic option in obesity-driven HFpEF, where metabolic dysfunction and structural cardiac remodelling dominate the phenotype. [[PMID 40366488]]
- HFpEF and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) are increasingly prevalent, interrelated conditions driven by the global rise in obesity and metabolic syndrome, and increasingly viewed together rather than in isolation. [[PMID 41735546]]
- In the community, HFpEF is emerging as the most common form of heart failure across the EF spectrum. [[PMID 29414252]]

**Uncertainties**
- GLP-1 receptor agonist outcome evidence in HFpEF is still maturing; positioning relative to SGLT2 inhibitors is not settled.
- Which diagnostic pathway (H2FPEF scoring, exercise echo, CMR) is most efficient for confirming HFpEF is not resolved in the retained abstracts.

**MCQ angles**
- Diagnostic criteria: symptoms plus elevated filling pressures (echo E/e', TR velocity, natriuretic peptides, CMR).
- The obesity-driven HFpEF phenotype and why GLP-1 agonists are biologically plausible there.
- SGLT2 inhibitors as the established therapy; GLP-1 RAs as emerging.
- MASLD as a common comorbidity reshaping the HFpEF phenotype.

**Case hooks**
- A breathless 68-year-old with EF 55%, BMI 38 and ALT elevation; decide on the HFpEF diagnostic pathway and discuss MASLD interplay.
- An exam vignette describes stepwise HFpEF work-up; choose the next test when echo is inconclusive.

**Gaps for the reviewer.** H2FPEF/HFA-PEFF scores, exercise-stress echo protocols, and STEP-HFpEF/DELIVER trial details are not covered by the retained abstracts; verify against primary sources before approval.

**Sources**

## #782 heart failure with reduced ejection fraction  (cluster `HFrEF`)

**Mentor intro.** HFrEF is where modern cardiology most clearly saves lives with drugs. The phenotype is defined by an ejection fraction below 40% with a clinical HF syndrome, and its treatment rests on four mechanistically complementary pillars â€” beta-blocker, RAAS modulation (preferably ARNI), mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, and SGLT2 inhibitor â€” supported by a convergent network meta-analytic literature showing combination therapy beats any single-agent approach.

**Teaching points**
- The 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA guideline is the current American management framework, replacing the 2013 guideline and the 2017 focused update. [[PMID 35363499]]
- Pharmacotherapy for HFrEF rests on four pillars: beta-blockers, renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system modulators, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and SGLT2 inhibitors. [[PMID 41136062]]
- HFrEF accounts for approximately half of the worldwide heart-failure burden (an estimated 23 million people), presenting as dyspnoea or exertional limitation from impaired ventricular ejection. [[PMID 32749493]]
- Network meta-analyses estimate and compare aggregate treatment benefits of HFrEF pharmacological combinations because the effects of contemporary therapy combinations were not well characterised by individual trials. [[PMID 34895860]]
- The 2025 updated network meta-analysis confirmed that combination beta-blocker, ARNi, MRA and SGLT2 inhibitor therapy is the most effective pharmacological approach for reducing all-cause mortality in HFrEF. [[PMID 40892608]]

**Uncertainties**
- Uptitration speed and simultaneous-versus-sequential initiation in frail or hypotensive patients are not standardised in the retained sources.
- How device therapy (ICD, CRT) should be timed relative to medical therapy optimisation is not addressed in the retained abstracts.

**MCQ angles**
- EF thresholds defining HFrEF and the EF-based HF classification.
- The four pillars and the outcome evidence behind each.
- Combination-versus-monotherapy reasoning from network meta-analysis.
- Diagnosis: clinical syndrome plus reduced EF and its common aetiologies.

**Case hooks**
- A 65-year-old presents with dyspnoea and EF 30%; outline the initial pharmacological plan and monitoring.
- An exam question lists four drug classes and asks which combination carries the strongest mortality evidence.

**Gaps for the reviewer.** Acute decompensated HFrEF, device therapy indications, and aetiology-specific work-up (ischaemic vs non-ischaemic) are not covered by the retained abstracts; verify against the full guideline before approval.

**Sources**

## #783 heart failure with reduced ejection fraction management  (cluster `HFrEF management`)

**Mentor intro.** Managing HFrEF well is an implementation problem as much as a knowledge problem: the evidence-based drugs exist, yet real-world studies show most patients never reach target doses of all four pillars. Effective management means systematic diagnosis, rapid GDMT initiation and titration with renal monitoring, deliberate handling of the common CKD comorbidity, and structured follow-up including exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation.

**Teaching points**
- The 2023 ESC focused update introduced new key recommendations in HF management, with SGLT2 inhibitors central, reflecting the last years of trial science. [[PMID 38806171]]
- Heart failure affects around 70 million individuals globally (prevalence roughly 2% in Europe and North America), and accurate diagnosis relies on typical signs and symptoms â€” framing the scale of the management challenge. [[PMID 41319669]]
- Despite major advancements in HF management and guideline recommendations, real-world evidence highlights suboptimal implementation of guideline-directed medical therapy for HFrEF, with low uptake of the four pillars. [[PMID 40012353]]
- CKD is common in patients with heart failure and is associated with high mortality and morbidity â€” even higher in patients on long-term dialysis â€” and evidence-based HF drug and device therapy remains underused in this comorbid group. [[PMID 33495289]]
- People with heart failure carry substantial disease burden including low exercise tolerance, poor quality of life, and increased mortality and hospital admission; exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation addresses these outcomes and was updated in a 2024 systematic review. [[PMID 38451843]]

**Uncertainties**
- Optimal GDMT dosing in advanced CKD and on dialysis is not resolved in the retained sources.
- How to organise care (heart-failure nurses, titration clinics, remote monitoring) to close the implementation gap is not settled in the retained abstracts.

**MCQ angles**
- The implementation gap: why patients miss GDMT doses and how to fix it.
- Managing HFrEF with comorbid CKD: which drugs, what monitoring.
- Role and evidence for exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation.
- Structured follow-up: who titrates, how fast, what triggers escalation.

**Case hooks**
- A man with HFrEF and eGFR 34 has only a beta-blocker prescribed; build the full titration and monitoring plan.
- A service audit finds 30% of HFrEF patients on zero GDMT pillars; design an improvement programme.

**Gaps for the reviewer.** Dosing tables, device therapy (ICD/CRT) indications, and palliative care transitions are not covered by the retained abstracts; verify against the full 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA guideline before approval.

## #784 Heavy menstrual bleeding: assessment and management  (cluster `heavy menstrual bleeding`)

Management is symptom-tailored and escalates from medical therapy to surgery.

**Teaching points**

**Case hooks**

- Peptic ulcer disease remains an important cause of morbidity and mortality with high healthcare costs, and its most important risk factors are H. pylori and NSAIDs. [[PMID 37365037]]

## #788 Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis diagnosis and therapy  (cluster `HLH diagnosis and therapy`)

**Mentor intro.** HLH diagnosis is pattern recognition under pressure: unremitting fever, cytopenias, hepatosplenomegaly and characteristic biomarkers, assembled into criteria sets because no single test is decisive. Therapy has two obligatory components â€” immune-suppressive control of the hyperinflammatory cascade and treatment of the underlying trigger â€” and modern conceptualisation has moved beyond a rigid primary-versus-secondary split, especially in children where interferon-gamma-driven T-cell activation dominates.

**Teaching points**
- Adult HLH is a severe hyperinflammatory syndrome induced by aberrantly activated macrophages and cytotoxic T cells; the primary (genetic) form predominates in children while secondary forms dominate in adults, and dedicated adult management recommendations exist. [[PMID 30992265]]
- HLH is a rare, life-threatening disorder of immune regulation characterised by uncontrolled activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes, NK cells and macrophages, leading to cytokine storm, end-organ damage and death. [[PMID 34347856]]
- HLH in adults carries high mortality when untreated, and a multidisciplinary approach to diagnosis and management is needed. [[PMID 41288529]]
- Paediatric HLH is characterised by heightened T-cell activation and overwhelming interferon-gamma production, and is now best conceptualised along a continuum rather than a binary primary-versus-secondary framework. [[PMID 41481377]]

**Uncertainties**

**MCQ angles**

**Case hooks**

## #789 Hemothorax  (cluster `hemothorax`)

**Mentor intro.** Hemothorax â€” blood in the pleural space, usually after trauma â€” is common (a third of thoracic-trauma patients have a pneumo- or haemothorax) and occasionally lethal. The management skeleton is simple: recognise it early (extended FAST where CT is unavailable), drain it promptly with tube thoracostomy when the patient is haemodynamically abnormal, and escalate when blood does not stop or does not drain â€” retained haemothorax is where the EAST practice guideline earns its keep.

**Teaching points**
- Thoracic trauma occurs in approximately 25% of all traumas, and one third of these patients present with a pneumothorax, hemothorax, or a combination; hemodynamically abnormal patients require expeditious tube thoracostomy drainage. [[PMID 40604368]]
- The Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma produced a practice management guideline for traumatic hemothorax addressing both acute and chronic (simple and retained) management questions. [[PMID 33487403]]
- Blunt chest trauma management includes recognition and drainage of pneumothorax and hemothorax alongside rib-fracture care: supportive care, multimodal analgesia, and pulmonary toilet. [[PMID 38453306]]
- FAST ultrasound addresses the limits of physical examination in detecting hemopericardium, hemoperitoneum and hemopneumothorax, where CT is the gold standard but not always available. [[PMID 37576415]]

**Uncertainties**
- The volume threshold defining 'massive' haemothorax and the initial-output trigger for thoracotomy vary across protocols and are not standardised in the retained abstracts.
- Optimal management of retained haemothorax (video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery timing versus additional tubes) is addressed by the EAST guideline but not detailed in the retained abstract.

**MCQ angles**

**Case hooks**

## #790 Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia  (cluster `heparin-induced thrombocytopenia`)

**Mentor intro.** HIT is the thrombocytopenia that bleeds in name only â€” it is, paradoxically, a profoundly prothrombotic drug reaction. Antibodies against PF4/heparin complexes activate platelets through Fc receptors, and the downstream events (arterial and venous thrombosis, limb loss, death) are driven by continued heparin exposure. The reflexes to build: suspect it on timing and thrombosis pattern, score it pre-test, stop all heparin immediately, and anticoagulate with a non-heparin agent even before confirmatory testing returns.

**Teaching points**
- HIT is an immune reaction to heparin associated with thrombocytopenia, thrombotic risk and high morbidity and mortality; given how commonly heparin is used and how often hospitalized patients are thrombocytopenic, a practical diagnostic and management framework is essential. [[PMID 39644042]]
- HIT is caused by antibodies to complexes of platelet factor 4 (PF4) and heparin; pathogenic PF4/heparin antibodies bind and activate cellular Fc-gamma-RIIA on platelets and monocytes, propagating a prothrombotic state. [[PMID 28416511]]
- Autoimmune HIT is a severe subtype with atypical clinical features caused by highly pathological IgG antibodies that activate platelets even in the absence of heparin. [[PMID 37959386]]
- Thrombocytopenia and heparin exposure are both common in critically ill patients, yet immune HIT rarely accounts for thrombocytopenia in this population â€” pre-test probability assessment is what separates the few true cases from the many false alarms. [[PMID 26539932]]

**Uncertainties**
- Optimal management of autoimmune HIT, including the role of intravenous immunoglobulin and longer anticoagulation, is not settled in the retained abstracts.
- When (and whether) warfarin can be safely introduced, and the duration of anticoagulation after HIT with thrombosis, are not detailed in the retained sources.

**MCQ angles**
- Timing: typical-onset (5-10 days) versus rapid-onset and delayed-onset HIT.
- The paradox: thrombocytopenia with thrombosis, not bleeding.
- 4Ts pre-test probability and functional versus immunologic assays (SRA, ELISA).
- Management reflexes: stop all heparin (including flushes), start a non-heparin anticoagulant, avoid warfarin alone early.

**Case hooks**
- A post-operative patient on unfractionated heparin develops a platelet drop from 240 to 90 on day 6 with a new leg swelling; score, test, and treat.
- A COVID-19 ICU patient on heparin has a falling platelet count; decide how likely HIT really is and what to do meanwhile.

**Gaps for the reviewer.** The 4Ts scoring detail, specific non-heparin agent choice and dosing, and warfarin-transition rules are not in the retained abstracts; verify against ASH 2018 HIT guidelines or the full practical guide before approval.

**Sources**
- PMID 39644042: Practical guide to the diagnosis and management of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia. (review, 2024)
- PMID 28416511: Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia. (review, 2017)
- PMID 37959386: Autoimmune Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia. (review, 2023)
- PMID 26539932: Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia. (review, 2015)
