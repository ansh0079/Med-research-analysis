## 931 — Infectious diarrhea and travelers diarrhea management

topicId: `4538f410-bd4f-4d20-b68b-cb2ff48940a9`

### Mentor intro
Acute diarrhoea is one of the commonest presentations in any setting — and one where restraint is the evidence-based skill. Most adult infectious diarrhoea, traveller's included, is viral or self-limiting bacterial disease managed with rehydration alone; the art is identifying who needs stool testing, who needs antibiotics, and who needs admission. Inflammatory features (blood, mucus, fever, severe pain) predict bacterial aetiology and shape empiric choices, while the pretravel consultation adds prevention: food and water precautions, and standby antibiotics for high-risk itineraries. Norovirus remains the commonest overall cause — a diagnosis of clinical and infection-control consequence.

### Teaching points
- Most acute diarrhoea in adults is self-limiting; oral rehydration is the foundation of therapy, with antibiotics reserved for suspected bacterial dysentery, severe disease, or specific risk factors. [[PMID 35839362]] [[PMID 30115338]]
- Inflammatory features (bloody stool, fever, tenesmus, severe abdominal pain) distinguish dysentery from watery diarrhoea and drive stool-culture and empiric-antibiotic decisions. [[PMID 35839362]]
- Norovirus is the commonest cause of gastroenteritis overall; recognition matters for infection control and because antibiotics have no role. [[PMID 30115338]]
- Traveller's diarrhoea management combines rehydration, loperamide for non-bloody cases, and standby antibiotics (fluoroquinolone or azithromycin) for moderate-to-severe cases on high-risk itineraries. [[PMID 32179056]] [[PMID 35839362]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether routine probiotic use shortens acute diarrhoea in adults remains inconsistent across trials.
- Optimal empiric antibiotic choice is shifting with fluoroquinolone-resistance patterns in Campylobacter and Shigella.

## 932 — Infectious diarrhoea: Campylobacter, Salmonella, STEC O157, norovirus, antibiotic indications

topicId: `96bc9de3-9e3a-4694-8bfd-db5eb8c12b5a`

### Mentor intro
Where topic 931 covers the general management of diarrhoea, this topic is about the organism-level decision that catches clinicians out: whether to give antibiotics. Campylobacter and non-typhoidal Salmonella mostly need no antibiotics in the well adult; suspected Shiga-toxin-producing E. coli (STEC O157) is the dangerous exception in reverse — antibiotics increase haemolytic uraemic syndrome risk and must be avoided. Norovirus needs neither antibiotics nor testing in most settings, but strict infection control. The single most testable pearl: bloody diarrhoea plus exposure to undercooked beef equals no antibiotics, and HUS surveillance — the syndrome that defines thrombotic microangiopathy in this context.

### Teaching points
- Antibiotics are indicated for severe suspected bacterial gastroenteritis, dysentery in specific groups, and confirmed treatable pathogens; most Campylobacter and non-typhoidal Salmonella infections in healthy adults resolve without them. [[PMID 31271003]]
- In suspected or confirmed STEC (E. coli O157) infection, antibiotics are contraindicated because they increase the risk of haemolytic uraemic syndrome; management is supportive with vigilant renal monitoring. [[PMID 31271003]] [[PMID 27723152]]
- HUS is defined by the triad of non-immune haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury from thrombotic microangiopathy; STEC is the dominant trigger and TTP the key differential within the TMA spectrum. [[PMID 27723152]] [[PMID 37949684]]
- Norovirus, the commonest gastroenteritis cause, requires no antibiotics and no specific testing in sporadic adult cases; outbreak settings demand environmental disinfection and exclusion policies. [[PMID 31271003]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether antibiotics benefit bloody Campylobacter dysentery in otherwise healthy adults is debated; practice varies.
- Optimal HUS management (hydration strategy, plasma exchange indications versus TTP) follows centre protocols rather than randomised evidence.

### MCQ angles

- Norovirus management and outbreak control.

- A diarrhoea patient develops pallor, thrombocytopenia, and rising creatinine; distinguish HUS from TTP and outline initial management.

## 933 — infective endocarditis

topicId: `3931a471-a42d-4bcb-b596-4d2ac484553c`

### Mentor intro
Infective endocarditis is a rare disease with outsized consequences, and its modern face has shifted: healthcare-associated infection, prosthetic valves, intracardiac devices, and staphylococci now dominate an epidemiology once ruled by viridans streptococci and rheumatic valves. The AHA scientific statement anchors diagnosis (modified Duke criteria), antimicrobial selection by organism and valve type, and complication management — heart failure, emboli, and the infected-device problems that need a team, not just a drug. Outcome still hinges on early recognition and the endocarditis-team model: infectious diseases, cardiology, cardiothoracic surgery, and microbiology deciding together.

### Teaching points
- Modern IE epidemiology is driven by healthcare exposure, prosthetic valves, and intracardiac devices, with Staphylococcus aureus now the leading organism in many series. [[PMID 31941729]] [[PMID 40185554]]
- The AHA scientific statement standardises diagnosis (modified Duke criteria), antimicrobial therapy by organism and valve type, and management of complications including heart failure, embolism, and device infection. [[PMID 26373316]]
- Outcomes improve with a multidisciplinary endocarditis team; surgery is indicated for heart failure, uncontrolled infection, and prevention of embolism in defined situations. [[PMID 26373316]] [[PMID 31941729]]
- Blood cultures before antibiotics and echocardiography (TTE then TEE) are the non-negotiable first steps; the organism, not the syndrome, dictates the regimen. [[PMID 26373316]] [[PMID 40185554]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal duration and agent choice for device-related and culture-negative endocarditis remain guideline-extrapolated.

## 934 — Infective endocarditis antibiotic duration and surgery

topicId: `4697a887-266a-432e-b9db-81ccc7370ae7`

### Mentor intro
Two numbers govern infective endocarditis treatment: the weeks of antibiotics and the timing of surgery. Duration is set by organism and valve — viridans streptococci on a native valve can be cured in two weeks with combination therapy, while staphylococci on prosthetic material demand six — and reviews of the ESC framework examine where shorter, equally effective courses can replace tradition. Surgery is lifesaving but high-risk: perioperative optimisation, the classic indications (heart failure, uncontrolled infection, large vegetations with embolic risk), and the modern operative challenges of extensive root destruction and device extraction define the contemporary craft.

### Teaching points
- Antibiotic duration in IE is pathogen- and valve-specific: 2 weeks is possible for selected viridans-group streptococcal native-valve disease with suitable agents, while staphylococcal and prosthetic-valve infection typically requires 4-6 weeks. [[PMID 32728356]]
- Reviews of the ESC treatment framework examine strategies to treat more effectively — shorter total courses and early oral switch in stable, selected patients — without loss of cure rates. [[PMID 32728356]]
- Surgical indications remain heart failure from valvular dysfunction, uncontrolled infection (abscess, persistent sepsis, prosthetic dehiscence), and embolism prevention for large/mobile vegetations, particularly with staphylococci. [[PMID 37762834]] [[PMID 38827544]]
- Perioperative mortality in IE surgery is among the highest in cardiac surgery; preoperative optimisation of heart failure, sepsis, and organ dysfunction materially affects outcomes. [[PMID 38827544]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether further duration shortening (e.g. two weeks for staphylococcal native-valve IE with surgery) is safe is untested outside selected series.
- Optimal timing of surgery after embolic stroke remains individualised by stroke severity and neuroimaging.

### MCQ angles
- Duration by organism and valve type.
- Surgical indications: heart failure, uncontrolled infection, embolism.
- Perioperative optimisation priorities.
- Extensive-root-destruction and device-extraction operative challenges.

### Case hooks

## 935 — Infective endocarditis antimicrobial and oral step-down strategy

topicId: `bea57368-e3c9-4438-8a29-d45ea0596404`

### Mentor intro
The POET trial broke the oldest rule in endocarditis treatment: that cure demands six weeks of intravenous antibiotics. In stable, non-septic patients on effective IV therapy with susceptible organisms, switching to oral combination therapy was non-inferior — transforming an inpatient doctrine into an outpatient pathway. The modern literature formalises who qualifies: clinical stability, documented susceptibility, suitable oral-agent bioavailability, no uncontrolled foci or high-resistance organisms, and a reliable patient with follow-up. Not every endocarditis qualifies — but the default of 'IV until week six' is now a choice, not a law.

### Teaching points
- Partial oral treatment (POET) is non-inferior to continued IV therapy in stable left-sided endocarditis caused by susceptible streptococci, enterococci, and S. aureus, after an initial effective IV period. [[PMID 41471204]] [[PMID 42796692]]
- Eligibility for oral step-down demands clinical stability, organism susceptibility, high-bioavailability oral agents, absence of uncontrolled infection foci or abscess, and reliable follow-up. [[PMID 32240296]] [[PMID 42796692]]
- Oral regimens typically use combination therapy (two agents with different mechanisms) to protect against resistance emergence given sub-therapeutic tissue levels risk. [[PMID 32240296]] [[PMID 41471204]]
- Exclusions for oral switch include prosthetic-valve infection in the early post-operative period, uncontrolled sepsis, heart failure awaiting surgery, and organisms with reduced oral-susceptibility options. [[PMID 42796692]] [[PMID 41471204]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether oral step-down applies to prosthetic-valve IE beyond selected low-risk cohorts is unresolved.
- Optimal duration of the initial IV period before switching (2 versus 4 weeks) by organism remains centre-defined.

### MCQ angles
- POET trial design, population, and non-inferiority result.
- Eligibility checklist for oral step-down.
- Combination-oral rationale and agent selection.
- Absolute and relative contraindications to switching.

### Case hooks
- A stable streptococcal mitral-valve IE patient completes 10 days of IV ceftriaxone, improving; assess oral-switch eligibility and design the regimen.
- A prosthetic-valve S. aureus IE patient asks about 'going home on tablets'; explain why the evidence does not support it here.

### Gaps

## 936 — Infective endocarditis: Duke criteria, blood cultures, echocardiography, surgical indications

topicId: `0cb70b0e-ebf6-403d-9ec6-95a271c33217`

### Mentor intro
The diagnostic spine of infective endocarditis is procedural discipline: at least three sets of blood cultures from separate sites before any antibiotics, followed by echocardiography — transthoracic first, transoesophageal when suspicion persists or prosthetic material is involved. The modified Duke criteria organise the result into definite, possible, and rejected, but their known weakness is sensitivity in the era of device infection and culture-negative disease, which is where advanced imaging earns its place. This topic is the instrument-focused view of the endocarditis cluster: get the cultures right, get the echo right, and the diagnosis usually declares itself.

### Teaching points
- Blood-culture discipline: at least three sets from separate venepunctures over 12-24 hours before antibiotics, with cultures held for extended incubation when prior antibiotics were given. [[PMID 31521667]] [[PMID 27582414]]
- Echocardiography follows a TTE-then-TEE logic: TEE is mandatory for prosthetic valves, persistent suspicion after negative TTE, and assessment of complications such as abscess and fistula. [[PMID 28671807]] [[PMID 31521667]]
- The modified Duke criteria classify definite/possible/rejected endocarditis using major (positive echocardiogram, typical organism) and minor criteria; sensitivity limitations are increasingly recognised. [[PMID 31521667]] [[PMID 27582414]]
- Surgical indications detected on imaging — heart failure from valve destruction, abscess/fistula formation, and persistent vegetations after emboli — convert the diagnostic pathway into an urgent surgical referral. [[PMID 28671807]] [[PMID 31521667]]

### Uncertainties
- How to integrate PET/CT and cardiac CT into the Duke framework beyond prosthetic valves and devices is evolving.
- Optimal echocardiographic vegetation-size threshold for surgery is informed by trial subgroup data rather than absolute rules.

- TTE versus TEE indications.
- Duke major/minor criteria and their pitfalls.
- Imaging findings that mandate surgery.

### Case hooks

## 937 — Infective endocarditis: Duke criteria, empiric antibiotics, surgical indications

topicId: `88933724-44ac-4113-ae34-be512307d6a6`

### Mentor intro
Empiric antibiotics in infective endocarditis are a narrowing problem: the 2015 AHA and ESC guidelines converge on organism-driven choices — antistaphylococcal cover for acute community or healthcare presentation, penicillin/ceftriaxone for subacute native-valve disease — while explicitly disagreeing in places that examiners probe. Two modern refinements: aminoglycoside synergism, long dogma for enterococcal and streptococcal IE, now carries documented nephrotoxicity without proven outcome benefit, and guidelines increasingly say goodbye; and enterococcal disease — increasingly ampicillin-resistant — forces regimen individualisation where bacteraemia and endocardia merge clinically.

### Teaching points
- The 2015 AHA and ESC guidelines agree on organism-directed empiric therapy: cover S. aureus for acute presentations and healthcare-associated disease, and streptococcal cover with ceftriaxone/penicillin for subacute native-valve presentations pending cultures. [[PMID 27297743]]
- Empiric choices must anticipate the modern microbiology: staphylococci dominate healthcare-associated and device-related IE, with vancomycin reserved for MRSA risk or beta-lactam allergy. [[PMID 27297743]]
- Aminoglycoside synergy for enterococcal and streptococcal endocarditis is challenged by nephrotoxicity without demonstrated outcome benefit; contemporary analyses question their continued routine use. [[PMID 31669426]]
- Enterococcal bacteraemia and endocarditis management requires source assessment, susceptibility-guided regimens (ampicillin plus ceftriaxone for susceptible strains, daptomycin or linezolid for resistant), and infectious-disease input. [[PMID 33152537]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether combination beta-lactam therapy for MSSA bacteraemia/endocardia improves outcomes is unresolved.
- The place of novel lipoglycopeptides (dalbavancin) and long-acting agents in outpatient IE pathways is under study.

### MCQ angles
- Empiric regimens by acuity and acquisition setting.
- AHA versus ESC guideline differences examiners test.
- The aminoglycoside controversy and evidence against routine synergy.
- Enterococcal IE regimen construction by susceptibility.

### Case hooks

## 938 — inflammatory bowel disease

topicId: `1b78bc66-32bd-41be-94ad-a23c96eb174b`

### Mentor intro
Inflammatory bowel disease — Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis — is managed at a genuinely modern pace: the 2025 British Society of Gastroenterology guidelines codify treat-to-target medicine with early biologic escalation, biomarker-guided monitoring, and an expanding small-molecule armamentarium. The core skill is no longer memorising drugs but running the disease: objective assessment (endoscopy, faecal calprotectin, CRP) rather than symptom guesswork, escalation before damage accumulates, and complication surveillance that changes with disease duration and extent. IBD is now a specialty where induction speed and monitoring discipline determine long-term outcomes.

### Teaching points
- IBD management is treat-to-target: the BSG 2025 guidelines emphasise objective remission targets (mucosal healing, biomarker normalisation) with regular re-assessment rather than symptom-based adjustment. [[PMID 40550582]] [[PMID 38934234]]
- Faecal calprotectin and CRP are the workhorse biomarkers for activity monitoring and relapse prediction, reducing but not replacing endoscopic assessment. [[PMID 35603998]] [[PMID 40550582]]
- Therapy spans 5-ASA and steroids for UC, immunomodulators, biologics (anti-TNF, vedolizumab, ustekinumab), and small molecules (JAK inhibitors, S1P modulators), selected by disease phenotype, severity, and prior response. [[PMID 38934234]] [[PMID 40550582]]
- Complication surveillance — colorectal cancer screening by disease duration/extent in colitis, osteoporosis from repeated steroids, and vaccination before immunosuppression — is part of every IBD treatment plan. [[PMID 40550582]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal first-line biologic selection (anti-TNF versus integrin versus IL-12/23) for individual phenotypes is partially answered by head-to-head data.

## 939 — Inflammatory bowel disease biologic therapy

topicId: `da94cfb1-ef68-42b0-bc40-74b352a11918`

### Mentor intro
Biologic therapy in IBD is a story of expanding options and sharpening precision. Five biologics are approved for ulcerative colitis with differing efficacy and positioning; after anti-TNF failure in Crohn's, indirect-comparison meta-analysis informs the vedolizumab-versus-ustekinumab choice that lacks a head-to-head trial; dual biologic therapy pushes the frontier for refractory disease at accepted infection-cost. And therapeutic drug monitoring has converted biologic prescribing from 'intensify and hope' to pharmacokinetic reasoning — low drug levels with antibodies point to switching class, low levels without antibodies to dose escalation. Modern IBD biologic care is applied pharmacology.

### Teaching points
- Five biologics are approved for UC (infliximab, adalimumab, golimumab, vedolizumab, ustekinumab) with differing efficacy and positioning; selection weighs disease severity, extraintestinal manifestations, and patient factors. [[PMID 33121691]]
- After anti-TNF failure in Crohn's disease, meta-analytic indirect comparison supports ustekinumab and vedolizumab as effective next-line options, with selection informed by phenotype and safety profile. [[PMID 34854100]]
- Dual biologic therapy (anti-TNF plus vedolizumab or ustekinumab) is an emerging strategy for refractory IBD with encouraging short-term outcomes in systematic review, at the cost of increased infection risk. [[PMID 30945576]]
- Therapeutic drug monitoring distinguishes mechanistic loss of response: low drug levels with anti-drug antibodies indicate switching class; low levels without antibodies indicate intensification — applying this prevents inappropriate escalation. [[PMID 38399538]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether dual biologics offer durable remission beyond small series, and their long-term safety, is unknown.
- Optimal TDM thresholds and proactive-versus-reactive monitoring strategies are not fully standardised.

### MCQ angles

- Loss-of-response work-up: TDM interpretation.
- Dual biologic therapy: evidence and risks.

- A refractory IBD patient asks about dual biologics; weigh the systematic-review evidence against infection risk.

## 940 — Inflammatory myopathies: dermatomyositis, polymyositis, IBM, anti-synthetase syndrome

topicId: `b6bbd7e2-bc37-4e4c-a2c1-25b86bc5415a`

### Mentor intro
The idiopathic inflammatory myopathies are a heterogenous family unified by proximal weakness and elevated creatine kinase but divided by skin, lung, antibody, and histology — and the modern classification (dermatomyositis, polymyositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, anti-synthetase syndrome, inclusion body myositis) directly drives treatment. Dermatomyositis brings its cancer-surveillance duty and characteristic rashes; anti-synthetase syndrome brings the mechanic's-hands, fever, and interstitial lung disease that can dominate the prognosis; IBM (topic 925) resists immunotherapy by its degenerative core. First-line treatment remains high-dose corticosteroids with early steroid-sparing agents, with myositis-specific antibodies now guiding both prognosis and escalation choices.

### Teaching points
- Modern classification divides IIM into dermatomyositis, polymyositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, anti-synthetase syndrome, and inclusion body myositis — categories with distinct treatment responses and prognoses. [[PMID 30129477]] [[PMID 34857798]]
- Dermatomyositis carries a malignancy association warranting age-appropriate cancer screening at diagnosis; characteristic cutaneous signs (heliotrope, Gottron papules) complement proximal weakness. [[PMID 30129477]] [[PMID 35976321]]
- Anti-synthetase syndrome (anti-Jo-1 and others) presents with myositis, mechanic's hands, fever, Raynaud, and interstitial lung disease; ILD severity often dictates the prognosis and escalation intensity. [[PMID 34857798]] [[PMID 35976321]]
- First-line therapy is high-dose corticosteroids with early addition of steroid-sparing agents (methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate, IVIG); myositis-specific antibodies increasingly inform prognosis and treatment selection. [[PMID 30129477]] [[PMID 34857798]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal steroid-sparing selection by myositis subtype and antibody profile is not standardised.
- The role of rituximab and new targeted agents beyond refractory disease is still being defined.
