## #791 Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia alternatives: argatroban, fondaparinux, bivalirudin  (cluster `HIT alternative anticoagulation`)

**Mentor intro.** Once HIT is suspected, every heparin source must stop â€” including flushes and coated catheters â€” and a non-heparin anticoagulant must start immediately, because the thrombotic risk is highest in the first days. The ASH 2018 guideline anchors drug choice; argatroban (hepatically cleared, short half-life), fondaparinux (subcutaneous, once daily) and bivalirudin (short half-life, procedural use) each occupy different niches, direct oral anticoagulants are an emerging option in selected patients, and ECMO adds its own specialised dilemmas.

**Teaching points**
- HIT is an adverse drug reaction mediated by platelet-activating antibodies targeting PF4/heparin complexes, with markedly increased thromboembolism risk; the ASH 2018 guidelines provide evidence-based management recommendations. [[PMID 30482768]]
- A systematic review and meta-analysis compared the effectiveness and safety of non-heparin anticoagulants for HIT treatment because the optimal strategy is not fully established. [[PMID 33857342]]
- Prompt suspension of heparin is necessary if HIT is suspected, followed by alternative anticoagulation, because thrombotic complications are potentially fatal. [[PMID 33736552]]
- Direct oral anticoagulants have a potential role in HIT management as an emerging alternative class, given the condition's paradoxical hypercoagulability and the constraints of older agents. [[PMID 31233222]]
- Patients on ECMO who develop HIT face specialised alternative-anticoagulation challenges, summarised in a dedicated narrative review. [[PMID 41301798]]

**Uncertainties**
- Head-to-head outcome data between argatroban, fondaparinux and bivalirudin are lacking; drug choice remains largely informed by patient factors (organ function, procedural needs) rather than comparative trials.
- DOAC use in acute HIT, particularly before platelet recovery, is not definitively established in the retained sources.

**MCQ angles**
- Agent selection by context: hepatic failure (argatroban caution), renal function, need for procedural anticoagulation (bivalirudin), outpatient transition (fondaparinux/DOAC).
- Why heparin must stop completely â€” including flushes and lock solutions.
- Comparative-effectiveness evidence between non-heparin anticoagulants.
- Special situations: ECMO, dialysis circuits, percutaneous coronary intervention.

**Case hooks**
- A patient with new HIT and decompensated cirrhosis needs anticoagulation; select the agent and justify.
- A patient stabilised on fondaparinux for HIT wants outpatient management; plan transition and monitoring.

**Gaps for the reviewer.** Agent-specific dosing, argatroban INR-transition rules, and bivalirudin procedural protocols are not in the retained abstracts; verify against ASH 2018 full guideline and agent monographs before approval.

**Sources**

## #792 Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia: diagnosis and management  (cluster `HIT diagnosis and management`)

**Mentor intro.** HIT diagnosis is a pre-test-probability discipline: most thrombocytopenia on a heparin ward is not HIT, and unselective testing floods the lab with false positives. Score first (4Ts), stop heparin on suspicion, start a non-heparin anticoagulant while functional and immunologic assays resolve the question, and never let a positive antibody test override an incompatible clinical picture.

**Teaching points**
- HIT is an immune reaction to heparin associated with thrombocytopenia, thrombotic risk and high morbidity and mortality; a practical diagnostic and management framework is essential because heparin use and thrombocytopenia are both common in hospital. [[PMID 39644042]]
- Immune-mediated HIT is an infrequent complication of heparin exposure with potentially fatal thrombotic outcome; prompt suspension of heparin is necessary if HIT is suspected, followed by alternative anticoagulation. [[PMID 33736552]]
- HIT is a profoundly dangerous, potentially lethal immunologically mediated adverse drug reaction to unfractionated heparin or, less commonly, low-molecular-weight heparin. [[PMID 27230048]]

**Uncertainties**
- The operating characteristics of the 4Ts score across different patient populations, and how to integrate ELISA optical density with functional assay results, are not detailed in the retained abstracts.
- Optimal anticoagulation duration for HIT without thrombosis is not resolved in the retained sources.

**MCQ angles**
- 4Ts pre-test probability: elements and thresholds for testing.
- Immunologic (ELISA) versus functional (SRA, HIPA) assays and their false-positive/false-negative patterns.
- The management reflex sequence: stop heparin, start non-heparin anticoagulant, avoid warfarin alone early.
- UFH versus LMWH risk and typical-onset timing (5-10 days).

**Case hooks**
- A post-cardiac-surgery patient has a platelet drop on day 7 with heparin flushes running; score, test, and act.
- An ELISA comes back weakly positive in a patient with a low 4Ts score; decide what the result means and what to do next.

**Gaps for the reviewer.** 4Ts scoring detail, assay algorithms, warfarin-transition rules, and anticoagulation durations are not in the retained abstracts; verify against ASH 2018 HIT guidelines before approval.

**Sources**

## #793 Hepatic encephalopathy recurrence prevention  (cluster `hepatic encephalopathy`)

**Mentor intro.** After a first episode of overt hepatic encephalopathy, most patients will recur without prevention, and each recurrence worsens prognosis and quality of life. Secondary prevention rests on two drugs with complementary jobs: lactulose, the non-absorbable disaccharide that traps ammonium in the colon, and rifaximin, the poorly absorbed antibiotic added when episodes break through â€” now supported by meta-analysis as prevention in its own right. Every recurrence should also trigger a search for the precipitant, because most are iatrogenic or gastrointestinal.

**Teaching points**
- Rifaximin is recommended for the treatment of hepatic encephalopathy, and a 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis assessed its safety and efficacy for preventing HE in patients with cirrhosis. [[PMID 40378134]]
- Evidence for long-term (>=6 months) pharmacological management of HE with lactulose and/or rifaximin has been consolidated in a dedicated review, addressing effectiveness and safety/tolerability. [[PMID 30444745]]
- Hepatic encephalopathy is a major neuropsychiatric complication of cirrhosis ranging from mild confusion to coma, and it is an important cause of recurrent admissions â€” framing the prevention priority. [[PMID 35677508]]
- Ammonia is a major neurotoxin implicated in the pathogenesis, progression and severity of HE, with contributions from multiple organs; therapies target ammonia production and handling. [[PMID 40332628]]
- Overt HE is a generally reversible but prognostically serious complication of cirrhosis, associated with poor hospitalisation- and mortality-related outcomes, which makes improving clinical outcomes through pharmacologic treatment a priority. [[PMID 34242619]]

**Uncertainties**
- Whether rifaximin monotherapy prevents first episodes (primary prevention) as effectively as it prevents recurrence is not established in the retained abstracts.
- The comparative effectiveness of lactulose alone versus lactulose plus rifaximin for secondary prevention needs individual-patient interpretation of trial data.

**MCQ angles**
- Secondary prevention after a first overt episode: lactulose titrated to stool output, plus rifaximin for recurrence.
- Precipitant hunt at every recurrence: infection, GI bleeding, constipation, sedatives, dehydration, TIPS.
- Ammonia-centric pathophysiology and why gut-directed therapies work.
- Covert vs overt HE classification and why covert HE still matters for driving and work.

**Case hooks**
- A patient hospitalised twice this year with overt HE is on lactulose alone; review the prevention plan.
- A cirrhotic on rifaximin develops breakthrough confusion; hunt the precipitant before escalating therapy.

**Gaps for the reviewer.** Primary-prevention indications, TIPS-specific prophylaxis, and nutritional/ sarcopenia management are not covered by the retained abstracts; verify against AASLD/EASL HE guidance before approval.

**Sources**
- PMID 40378134: Efficacy and safety of rifaximin in preventing hepatic encephalopathy: A systematic review and meta-analysis. (meta-analysis, 2025)
- PMID 30444745: Long-term management of hepatic encephalopathy with lactulose and/or rifaximin: a review of the evidence. (review, 2019)

## #794 Hepatitis B: natural history, HBeAg, HBsAg, HBV DNA, antiviral indications, tenofovir  (cluster `hepatitis B serology and antivirals`)

**Mentor intro.** Chronic hepatitis B is a disease of phases, and the serology tells the story: HBeAg and its seroconversion mark the immune-reactive chapter, quantitative HBsAg hints at immune control, and HBV DNA is the number that decides whether treatment is needed. When treatment starts, entecavir or tenofovir suppress the virus durably, but stopping before HBsAg loss is possible only in carefully selected patients â€” and HCC surveillance continues regardless of viral suppression.

**Teaching points**
- HBV infection remains a global public health problem with changing epidemiology due to vaccination policies and migration; the EASL 2017 guideline presents updated recommendations for optimal management. [[PMID 28427875]]
- Chronic HBV infection remains a significant global health problem, and evidence-based guidelines are needed to determine when treatment should be initiated, which medication is most appropriate, and when it can safely be stopped. [[PMID 26566246]]
- Meta-analysis addressed whether tenofovir disoproxil fumarate differs from entecavir in reducing hepatocellular carcinoma risk among patients with chronic hepatitis B, where earlier aggregated analyses had yielded conflicting results. [[PMID 35767258]]
- Most guidelines and existing data suggest nucleos(t)ide analogue therapy may be stopped only in carefully selected chronic hepatitis B patients, and discontinuation decisions remain nuanced even before HBsAg loss. [[PMID 30803099]]

**Uncertainties**
- Whether tenofovir truly reduces HCC risk more than entecavir remains contested after meta-analytic effort.
- Functional-cure strategies beyond nucleos(t)ide analogues (interferon, siRNA, capsid assembly modulators) are investigational and not resolved in the retained sources.

**MCQ angles**
- Serologic phases of chronic HBV and what HBeAg seroconversion means.
- Antiviral indications driven by HBV DNA, ALT and fibrosis stage.
- Entecavir versus tenofovir: potency, resistance barriers, renal/bone considerations.
- HCC surveillance continues even with suppressed viral replication.

**Case hooks**
- A 42-year-old with HBeAg-positive chronic HBV, ALT 2x ULN and high HBV DNA; apply treatment-indication criteria.
- A patient on tenofovir with undetectable DNA for 5 years asks whether the drug can be stopped; counsel using guideline criteria.

**Gaps for the reviewer.** Quantitative HBsAg interpretation, immune-tolerant phase management, and peginterferon candidacy are not covered by the retained abstracts; verify against the current EASL 2025 HBV guideline before approval.

**Sources**
- PMID 28427875: EASL 2017 Clinical Practice Guidelines on the management of hepatitis B virus infection. (guideline, 2017)
- PMID 26566246: Antiviral therapy for chronic hepatitis B viral infection in adults: A systematic review and meta-analysis. (meta-analysis, 2016)

**Teaching points**

- Chronic hepatitis B persists as a major global health challenge due to covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) persistence and impaired host immunity; interferon-alpha both directly restricts HBV and modulates immunity toward functional cure. [[PMID 41157628]]

**Case hooks**

## #796 Hepatitis C: DAA therapy, pangenotypic regimens, SVR12, HCV cure, screening  (cluster `hepatitis C DAA therapy`)

**Mentor intro.** Hepatitis C went from a chronic, often silent killer to a curable infection in under a decade: pangenotypic direct-acting antiviral combinations (sofosbuvir/velpatasvir, glecaprevir/pibrentasvir) deliver sustained virological response â€” SVR12, the practical definition of cure â€” in the overwhelming majority of patients, regardless of genotype. The remaining challenges are systems challenges: finding the undiagnosed (screening) and managing the 2-12% who fail therapy, where resistance-associated substitutions guide retreatment.

**Teaching points**
- The approval and adoption of pangenotypic direct-acting antivirals necessitated revision of the 2015 WHO guidelines for managing HCV infection, and systematic-review evidence supports their use. [[PMID 31922124]]
- HCV therapeutic regimens have undergone rapid advancement over the last decade, with structure-based drug design producing increasingly efficacious pangenotypic therapies. [[PMID 30506705]]
- Virological failure occurs in 2%-12% of persons receiving curative DAA treatment and may be presaged by drug resistance-associated substitutions, whose prevalence and impact on retreatment have been quantified in meta-analysis. [[PMID 39361017]]
- An estimated 71.1 million individuals are chronically infected with HCV worldwide (about 1% of the population), with transmission most commonly associated with blood exposure â€” the population screening must reach. [[PMID 33610022]]

**Uncertainties**
- Optimal screening strategies (birth-cohort versus risk-based versus universal) vary by prevalence setting and are not settled in the retained sources.
- Retreatment regimens after DAA failure, guided by resistance testing, are evolving and not detailed in the retained abstracts.

**MCQ angles**
- SVR12 as the definition of cure and its clinical implications.
- Pangenotypic regimens and why genotyping has become optional.
- Resistance-associated substitutions: prevalence and when to test.
- Who to screen: risk groups, birth cohorts, one-time adult screening.

**Case hooks**
- A 55-year-old with abnormal LFTs and a remote injecting-drug history tests HCV-antibody positive; walk from screening to cure.
- A patient relapses after sofosbuvir/velpatasvir; interpret the role of resistance testing in retreatment.

**Gaps for the reviewer.** Specific regimen choice by fibrosis stage, drug-drug interaction management, and perinatal/transmission-prevention counselling are not covered by the retained abstracts; verify against EASL/AASLD HCV guidance before approval.

**Sources**

## #799 Hepatitis D: superinfection vs coinfection, severity, peginterferon, bulevirtide  (cluster `hepatitis D`)

**Mentor intro.** Hepatitis D is the defective satellite virus that only exists alongside hepatitis B â€” it needs HBsAg to enter and propagate â€” and it converts a manageable HBV infection into the most severe form of human viral hepatitis. The clinical fork is coinfection (HDV and HBV acquired together, often transient) versus superinfection (HDV landing on established chronic HBV, which usually becomes chronic and aggressive). Treatment was peginterferon for three decades with minority response rates; bulevirtide, the first-in-class entry inhibitor, has finally given the field an approved targeted drug.

**Teaching points**
- Chronic hepatitis D results from infection with HBV and HDV, and is the most severe form of human viral hepatitis; current treatment options consist of interferon alfa, effective only in a minority of patients. [[PMID 31266627]]
- Chronic hepatitis D results from infection with both HBV and HDV together, and it is the most severe form of human viral hepatitis â€” which is why HDV screening matters in every patient with chronic HBV. [[PMID 31266627]]
- Pegylated interferon-alpha treatment of chronic HDV gives variable and often poor responses; bulevirtide was recently approved with promising results, and meta-analysis now compares regimens comparatively. [[PMID 40668572]]
- Bulevirtide, a first-in-class entry inhibitor, is the first drug formally approved for chronic hepatitis delta, whereas pegylated interferon has been used off-label since the 1990s; current evidence compares bulevirtide monotherapy with combination treatment. [[PMID 41134660]]
- Blocking cell entry is a suitable therapeutic approach, and bulevirtide works by inhibiting the bile-acid transporter NTCP that HDV uses for hepatocyte entry. [[PMID 35028672]]

**Uncertainties**
- Optimal duration of bulevirtide therapy and durability of response after stopping are not settled in the retained sources.
- Whether combination bulevirtide plus peginterferon outperforms either alone needs head-to-head confirmation beyond current evidence.

**MCQ angles**
- Coinfection versus superinfection: clinical course and chronicity risk.
- Why HDV requires HBV (HBsAg dependence).
- Peginterferon limitations: response in a minority, off-label status.
- Bulevirtide: NTCP entry-inhibitor mechanism and approved status.

**Case hooks**
- A patient with chronic HBV deteriorates rapidly with rising ALT; test for HDV superinfection and discuss implications.
- An exam question compares coinfection and superinfection outcomes; justify the prognosis difference.

**Gaps for the reviewer.** EASL HDV guideline treatment algorithms, HDV RNA monitoring endpoints, and liver-transplantation indications are not in the retained abstracts; verify against current EASL HDV guidance before approval.

## #800 hepatitis management  (cluster `viral hepatitis management`)

**Mentor intro.** Viral hepatitis spans five viruses with five different stories: A and E, transmitted faeco-orally and usually self-limiting; B and C, blood-borne chronic infections that drive cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma; and D, the HBV-dependent satellite with the worst prognosis. Modern management is a cascade-of-care problem â€” prevent (vaccination), test, link to treatment, suppress or cure, and monitor for reactivation and cancer â€” and the metrics that matter are how well each health system moves patients through that cascade.

**Teaching points**
- Five hepatitis virus types (A, B, C, D, E) have distinct transmission, prognosis and management: HAV, spread faeco-orally, is typically self-limiting with supportive therapy, while HBV, HCV and HDV drive chronic liver disease. [[PMID 40247447]]
- The updated EASL guidelines provide comprehensive evidence-based recommendations for HBV management across ten thematic sections, addressing diagnostics, treatment goals and more. [[PMID 40348683]]
- Modelling of global HCV prevalence and cascade of care between 2015 and 2020 shows many countries were progressing toward elimination targets before the COVID-19 pandemic â€” measuring the cascade, not just prevalence, is how management is judged. [[PMID 35180382]]
- HBV reactivation can follow diverse immune-modulating exposures; antiviral prophylaxis is effective, and select patients can be monitored without prophylaxis, per the AGA guideline. [[PMID 39863345]]
- HBV and HCV are the leading causes of end-stage liver disease worldwide; despite a potent HBV vaccine, many new infections occur annually where vaccination coverage is weak. [[PMID 38793606]]

**Uncertainties**
- Elimination targets (WHO 2030) remain ambitious; modelling shows progress is fragile and system-dependent.
- Integration of hepatitis services with primary care and harm-reduction programmes is uneven and not standardised in the retained sources.

**Case hooks**
