## 921 — Immune Thrombocytopenia

topicId: `9fc7e91f-3023-482a-8e65-a1999365d8fd`

### Mentor intro
Immune thrombocytopenia is a diagnosis of exclusion made at any age, and modern management is built around two documents: the updated International Consensus Report and the ASH 2019 guidelines. Both shift thinking away from platelet-number reflexes toward bleeding-risk reasoning — adults with counts above 20–30 x10^9/L and no bleeding often need only observation, while treatment is warranted for bleeding, procedures, or lifestyle constraints. Corticosteroids with or without IVIG remain first line, and the guidelines explicitly resist over-treatment, favouring shorter steroid courses and shared decision-making for everything beyond.

### Teaching points
- ITP diagnosis requires exclusion of secondary causes and pseudothrombocytopenia; the international consensus report standardises investigation including HIV, HCV, and Helicobacter pylori testing where appropriate. [[PMID 31770441]]
- ASH 2019 guidelines recommend observation alone for many asymptomatic adults with platelets above 20–30 x10^9/L, and short-course corticosteroids over prolonged courses or IVIG as first-line treatment when therapy is needed. [[PMID 31794604]]
- Second-line options — TPO receptor agonists, rituximab, splenectomy, fostamatinib — are selected by patient factors including durability goals, comorbidities, and surgical candidacy rather than a fixed sequence. [[PMID 31794604]] [[PMID 31770441]]
- Emergency management of severe bleeding uses combined platelet transfusion, IVIG, and high-dose corticosteroids; thrombopoietin agonists are not emergency agents. [[PMID 31770441]] [[PMID 31794604]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal sequencing of second-line therapies (TPO-RAs versus rituximab versus splenectomy) lacks head-to-head trials.
- Whether early TPO-RA use alters long-term natural history is unknown.

### MCQ angles
- Observation thresholds and first-line treatment selection by guideline.
- Work-up for secondary ITP.
- Emergency ITP management with active bleeding.
- Second-line option selection logic.

### Case hooks
- An asymptomatic 30-year-old has platelets 24 x10^9/L on routine testing; decide whether to treat and what to work up.
- An ITP patient has gastrointestinal bleeding with platelets 4 x10^9/L; outline the emergency regimen.

### Gaps

## 922 — Immune thrombocytopenia: thrombopoietin receptor agonists

topicId: `9951dfae-572a-41b8-949d-910895093d64`

### Mentor intro
Thrombopoietin receptor agonists changed the chronic-ITP landscape by stimulating platelet production rather than suppressing destruction: eltrombopag (oral, daily, food and chelation interactions) and romiplostim (weekly subcutaneous peptide) produce durable responses in most patients who fail first-line therapy, and a decade of use has clarified their real profile. They are not benign: arterial and venous thrombosis, paradoxical with a 'bleeding disease', is the signal that systematic review and cohort data quantify — risk concentrates with cardiovascular comorbidity, high normalised platelet counts, and portal-hypertension contexts. Dose is titrated to a safe platelet range, not to normalisation.

### Teaching points
- TPO-RAs (eltrombopag, romiplostim) are second-line agents for persistent/chronic ITP that raise platelet counts through thrombopoietin-receptor stimulation of megakaryopoiesis, with response in a majority of patients. [[PMID 30880797]] [[PMID 31073079]]
- Practical differences matter: eltrombopag is oral but requires separation from food and polyvalent-cation-containing medicines; romiplostim is administered weekly by injection — choice is individualised by adherence and preference. [[PMID 31073079]] [[PMID 30880797]]
- Thrombosis is the principal serious risk: meta-analysis and observational cohorts show increased arterial and venous thrombotic events, modulated by age, comorbidity, and achieved platelet count. [[PMID 35007700]] [[PMID 38357229]]
- Dosing targets a safe hemostatic platelet range (commonly 50 x10^9/L or above) rather than normalisation, balancing bleeding protection against thrombotic risk. [[PMID 31073079]] [[PMID 35007700]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether thrombosis risk is drug-specific (eltrombopag versus romiplostim) or class-wide is unresolved.
- Optimal duration and whether some patients can taper off TPO-RAs after sustained response is not established.

### MCQ angles
- Mechanism and the production-versus-destruction distinction.
- Eltrombopag administration rules and interactions.
- Thrombosis risk quantification and mitigation.
- Platelet targets and monitoring schedules.

### Case hooks
- A chronic-ITP patient on eltrombopag takes antacids and calcium; explain the interaction and redesign the regimen.
- A 70-year-old with prior DVT needs a TPO-RA; weigh thrombosis risk and set the platelet target.

### Gaps
- The retained abstracts lack the primary EXTEND/Long-Term extension trial reports; verify durability and long-term safety rates before approval.

## 923 — Immunosuppression Post-Transplant

topicId: `b00f6dc4-f905-419e-84dd-95dbd0ea22c1`

### Mentor intro
Maintenance immunosuppression after solid-organ transplantation is a balancing act calibrated in milligrams and nanograms: too little invites rejection, too much delivers nephrotoxicity, infection, and malignancy. Tacrolimus — a calcineurin inhibitor with narrow therapeutic index — anchors nearly all modern regimens, combined with mycophenolate and tapered steroids; systematic-review evidence supports tacrolimus-based maintenance as the standard. The craft lies in the detail: trough-level monitoring against a steep concentration-response curve, CYP3A5 pharmacogenetics that explain much inter-patient variability, and long-term stewardship that weighs dose minimisation against the rising cardiovascular and metabolic burden of decades of therapy.

### Teaching points
- Modern maintenance regimens centre on tacrolimus with mycophenolate and tapered corticosteroids; tacrolimus-based maintenance is supported by systematic-review evidence as the standard of care in kidney transplantation. [[PMID 34963678]] [[PMID 33853841]]
- Tacrolimus has a narrow therapeutic window with nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity above range and rejection below; trough monitoring with dose titration is mandatory, especially early post-transplant. [[PMID 29380698]]
- Pharmacogenetics — CYP3A5 expresser status — substantially alters tacrolimus metabolism and dosing requirements, informing genotype-guided initial dosing in some centres. [[PMID 29380698]]
- Long-term management must reconcile competing harms: chronic calcineurin-inhibitor toxicity, cardiovascular and metabolic burden, infection, and malignancy risk against late rejection from over-minimisation. [[PMID 33853841]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal long-term minimisation strategies (steroid withdrawal, CNI reduction with mTOR substitution) remain individualised with incomplete evidence.
- Whether pharmacogenetic dosing improves hard graft outcomes versus trough-guided titration is not established.

### MCQ angles
- Components of standard triple maintenance therapy.
- Tacrolimus monitoring, interactions (azoles, macrolides), and toxicity.
- CYP3A5 pharmacogenetics and dose individualisation.
- Long-term minimisation versus late-rejection trade-offs.

### Case hooks
- A kidney-transplant patient on tacrolimus starts fluconazole; predict the interaction and adjust the plan.
- Five years post-transplant with stable graft and rising metabolic burden; discuss minimisation options and their risks.

### Gaps
- The retained abstracts are kidney-focused; heart/liver/lung-specific regimen differences are not covered — verify against organ-specific guidance before approval.

## 924 — Inclisiran for LDL cholesterol lowering

topicId: `7bdccae3-94b1-491a-bb85-7c67ab205df9`

### Mentor intro
Inclisiran is the first siRNA to move lipid management from adherence-dependent daily pills to twice-yearly hepatocyte-directed dosing: it silences PCSK9 mRNA, lowering LDL-C by roughly 50% on top of statins — a similar magnitude to monoclonal PCSK9 antibodies but delivered in a way that solves the adherence problem that undermines chronic lipid therapy. The ORION programme established efficacy and a reassuring serious-adverse-event profile, and cardiovascular-outcome trials are maturing. Its practical appeal is healthcare-system as much as patient: administration by a clinician every six months converts a daily behaviour into a scheduled event.

### Teaching points
- Inclisiran is a synthetic siRNA that silences hepatocyte PCSK9 production, lowering LDL-C by approximately 50% as monotherapy or on top of statins, with effects persisting between twice-yearly doses. [[PMID 39640378]] [[PMID 34199468]]
- The twice-yearly maintenance dosing schedule (after initial and 3-month doses) addresses non-adherence, the dominant cause of suboptimal lipid control in practice. [[PMID 39439657]] [[PMID 39640378]]
- Meta-analysis of the ORION trials found no significant increase in serious adverse events versus control, with injection-site reactions the commonest specific complaint. [[PMID 37930224]]
- Current positioning is adjunctive for patients not achieving LDL targets on maximally tolerated statins (with or without ezetimibe); cardiovascular-outcome trial evidence is still accumulating. [[PMID 34199468]] [[PMID 39640378]]

### Uncertainties
- Hard cardiovascular-outcome benefit (versus the well-established LDL-C surrogate) is pending outcome-trial completion.
- Long-term (>5 year) safety and durability of response with real-world adherence patterns are not yet mature.

### MCQ angles
- siRNA mechanism versus monoclonal PCSK9 antibodies.
- ORION trial efficacy magnitudes and persistence.
- Dosing schedule and the adherence rationale.
- Safety profile from ORION meta-analysis.

### Case hooks
- A patient with familial hypercholesterolaemia has LDL-C 3.8 mmol/L on high-intensity statin plus ezetimibe with poor pill adherence; argue for inclisiran.
- A clinic debates inclisiran versus evolocumab for equivalent LDL-C lowering; compare mechanisms, frequency, and evidence maturity.

### Gaps

## 925 — Inclusion Body Myositis (IBM): asymmetric weakness, rimmed vacuoles, treatment resistance

topicId: `42c092ce-ee97-4de1-bc09-fad8430ea81c`

### Mentor intro
Inclusion body myositis is the myopathy that refuses to behave: slowly progressive, asymmetric weakness — finger flexors and quadriceps characteristically — in older men, with rimmed vacuoles and protein aggregates on biopsy, and a well-earned reputation for treatment resistance. It is frequently misdiagnosed as polymyositis and exposed only when immunosuppression fails. Modern understanding frames it as a degenerative process with inflammatory features, which explains why immunotherapies that work for other myosities disappoint here; management is therefore functional preservation, falls prevention, and honest prognostic counselling, with clinical-trial awareness the main therapeutic hope.

### Teaching points
- IBM presents in older adults with slowly progressive, often asymmetric weakness with early finger-flexor and quadriceps involvement; falling and grip loss are characteristic functional clues. [[PMID 36537974]] [[PMID 36069417]]
- Biopsy shows rimmed vacuoles, endomysial inflammation, and protein aggregates (including TDP-43 and p62); the degenerative-plus-inflammatory signature underlies treatment resistance. [[PMID 36069417]] [[PMID 39469805]]
- Immunosuppression lacks robust efficacy in IBM — a key discriminator from polymyositis and dermatomyositis; misdiagnosis as treatment-refractory polymyositis should prompt re-evaluation for IBM. [[PMID 39469805]] [[PMID 36537974]]
- Management centres on exercise and rehabilitation to preserve function, falls prevention, dysphagia surveillance, and enrolment into clinical trials; no disease-modifying therapy is established. [[PMID 39469805]] [[PMID 36069417]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether targeted anti-inflammatory or anti-degenerative strategies can modify progression is untested at scale.
- Optimal role of corticosteroids or IVIG in selected subsets (e.g. rapidly progressive or dysphagia-predominant) remains individualised.

### MCQ angles
- Clinical pattern: asymmetric finger-flexor and quadriceps weakness in older men.
- Histology: rimmed vacuoles and inclusions versus other myositides.
- Why immunosuppression fails — the degeneration-inflammation duality.
- Functional management: rehab, falls, dysphagia, trials.

### Case hooks

- An IBM patient develops progressive dysphagia; outline the swallowing-assessment and management pathway.

### Gaps

## 926 — Induction therapy in AAV: rituximab vs cyclophosphamide, RITUXVAS, RAVE trials

topicId: `8a36c078-2591-460f-b823-4fe18c4afdde`

### Mentor intro
Remission induction in ANCA-associated vasculitis was reshaped by two trials: RAVE, which showed rituximab non-inferior — and for relapsing disease superior — to cyclophosphamide for early severe GPA/MPA, and RITUXVAS, which confirmed non-inferiority in a broader severe cohort. The result, codified in the EULAR 2022 recommendations, is that rituximab is now preferred first-line induction for most new and relapsing patients, with cyclophosphamide retained for organ-threatening emergencies and cost-constrained settings. The nuance examiners love: rituximab's advantage is strongest at relapse and in PR3-ANCA disease, and glucocorticoid-sparing regimens (avacopan era) now accompany both arms.

### Teaching points
- RAVE established rituximab as non-inferior to cyclophosphamide for remission induction in early severe GPA/MPA, with superiority in relapsing disease. [[PMID 28155022]] [[PMID 25753806]]
- RITUXVAS confirmed non-inferiority of rituximab-based induction in severe AAV with renal involvement, cementing B-cell depletion as a standard induction option. [[PMID 27471722]] [[PMID 28155022]]
- EULAR 2022 recommendations position rituximab as preferred induction for new and relapsing GPA/MPA, reserving cyclophosphamide for selected organ-threatening presentations and specific contraindications. [[PMID 36927642]]
- Relapse prediction guides intensity: PR3-ANCA positivity and prior relapse favour rituximab, which also serves as maintenance with repeat dosing. [[PMID 36927642]] [[PMID 25753806]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal glucocorticoid taper speed and the integrating role of C5a-receptor inhibition (avacopan) with either induction arm continue to evolve.
- Rituximab dosing schedules for maintenance (fixed interval versus CD19-guided) are not standardised.

### MCQ angles

- Maintenance strategies after rituximab induction.

### Case hooks

## 927 — Infection in pregnancy: GBS screening, listeria, toxoplasmosis, antiviral safety

topicId: `795a5500-21b1-4e6b-9556-cc42ae0ea7d5`

### Mentor intro
Infections in pregnancy demand dual-patient thinking: the mother, the fetus, and the drugs that must be safe for both. Group B streptococcus is the organised success story — universal late-gestation screening with intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis for colonised mothers has slashed early-onset neonatal disease, with ACOG's Committee Opinion defining screening timing, risk-based alternatives, and prophylaxis regimens. Beyond GBS, the clinician must hold the less algorithmic territory: listeriosis and toxoplasmosis as foodborne and cat-associated risks managed by prevention and targeted testing, antiviral safety questions in influenza and herpes infections, and the recognition that sepsis remains a leading cause of maternal mortality with modified (pregnancy-appropriate) early-warning criteria.

### Teaching points
- Universal antenatal GBS screening at 36 0/7–37 6/7 weeks with intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (penicillin first-line) for colonised or high-risk mothers is the standard for preventing early-onset neonatal disease. [[PMID 31977795]] [[PMID 41740033]]
- Approximately half of women colonised with GBS transmit to the newborn; intrapartum prophylaxis reduces early-onset disease transmission, with risk-based prophylaxis for untested women with obstetric risk factors. [[PMID 39584819]] [[PMID 31977795]]
- Maternal sepsis is a leading cause of maternal mortality; major guidelines emphasise early recognition with pregnancy-adjusted warning scores, source control, and foetal surveillance alongside maternal resuscitation. [[PMID 37043300]]
- Prevention education remains central for listeriosis (food hygiene) and toxoplasmosis (cat-litter and undercooked-meat avoidance); serological testing and treatment decisions follow exposure-risk assessment. [[PMID 39584819]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal management of penicillin-allergic GBS-colonised mothers (clindamycin resistance concerns) varies by region and testing availability.
- Toxoplasmosis treatment regimens in pregnancy (spiramycin versus pyrimethamine-sulfadiazine timing) follow regional protocols with limited trial evidence.

### MCQ angles
- GBS screening timing, prophylaxis indications, and antibiotic choice.
- Risk-based prophylaxis when screening status is unknown.
- Pregnancy-adjusted sepsis recognition and management.
- Listeriosis/toxoplasmosis prevention and counselling.

### Case hooks
- A woman at 39 weeks arrives in labour with unknown GBS status and prolonged rupture of membranes; decide on intrapartum prophylaxis.
- A pregnant patient with fever, tachycardia, and uterine tenderness is 'not that unwell' by generic sepsis scores; apply pregnancy-appropriate criteria.

### Gaps
- The retained evidence is GBS- and sepsis-weighted; listeria, toxoplasmosis, and antiviral safety are covered only peripherally — verify management specifics against dedicated reviews before approval.

## 928 — Infection in solid organ transplant: timeline, CMV prophylaxis, PCP, opportunistic pathogens

topicId: `cb900c4b-c985-4fb5-98c8-b7db3e3b2b0f`

### Mentor intro
Infection after solid-organ transplantation is predictable by clock and immunosuppression intensity: the first month brings nosocomial and surgical complications, months one to six bring the opportunists — CMV above all, plus Pneumocystis, Aspergillus, and BK virus — and beyond six months, community-acquired infections dominate with immune-deficiency patterns in over-immunosuppressed patients. CMV is the single most consequential pathogen: direct disease (colitis, pneumonitis) and indirect effects (rejection, graft injury) are addressed by universal or targeted prophylaxis with valganciclovir and preemptive strategies guided by viral-load monitoring, with maribavir now available for refractory disease. PCP prophylaxis with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is standard for the early period — one prescription that prevents the most preventable transplant death.

### Teaching points
- The post-transplant infection timeline structures the differential: healthcare-associated infections in month one, opportunistic infections (CMV, PCP, moulds, BK) in months 1–6, and community-acquired infections thereafter. [[PMID 33879502]] [[PMID 31668195]]
- CMV is the most common significant post-transplant infection; D+/R- serostatus defines highest risk, and direct (tissue-invasive) plus indirect (rejection-linked) effects both demand prevention. [[PMID 37899366]] [[PMID 33879502]]
- Prevention strategies are prophylaxis (valganciclovir for high-risk recipients) versus preemptive therapy guided by quantitative PCR monitoring — both effective, selection depends on serostatus, organ, and logistics. [[PMID 39492698]] [[PMID 37899366]]
- Pneumocystis prophylaxis with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is standard for at least 6–12 months post-transplant; alternatives (atovaquone, dapsone, pentamidine) serve sulfa-allergic patients. [[PMID 31668195]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal duration of CMV prophylaxis and the long-term value of extending beyond six months in high-risk recipients are debated.
- Letermovir's expanding role in SOT (established in HCT) and its positioning versus valganciclovir is still being defined.

### MCQ angles
- The timeline framework by transplant month.
- CMV serostatus risk stratification and prophylaxis-versus-preemptive selection.
- PCP prophylaxis duration and sulfa-allergy alternatives.
- Late infections and over-immunosuppression patterns.

### Case hooks
- A D+/R- kidney recipient at week 9 has fever, diarrhoea, and a rising CMV PCR; outline the diagnostic and treatment pathway including resistance considerations.
- A transplant patient at 3 months has dyspnoea and hypoxia with normal chest X-ray; build the differential and the PCP work-up.

## 929 — Infection prevention and control: standard precautions, contact isolation, hand hygiene evidence

topicId: `0ed494ef-14c1-4f65-8771-3b746eaa5d78`

### Mentor intro
Infection prevention and control is the highest-yield low-technology discipline in medicine: healthcare-worker hands are the dominant transmission vehicle for hospital-acquired infection, and hand hygiene at the WHO five moments remains the single most effective intervention — systematic review confirms compliance reduces HAI, while behaviour-change literature shows multimodal programmes (education, feedback, cues, leadership) outperform posters alone. Above standard precautions, contact precautions (gowns, gloves, single rooms) for multidrug-resistant organisms are standard practice despite contested effectiveness data — the systematic-review evidence is conflicted, and the modern debate centres on targeted versus universal application, balancing transmission prevention against isolation's harms to patients and care processes.

### Teaching points
- Hand hygiene is the cornerstone of standard precautions; systematic-review evidence links healthcare-worker hand-hygiene compliance with reduced hospital-acquired infection rates. [[PMID 34582962]]
- Multimodal interventions — education, performance feedback, reminders, environmental cues, and administrative support — improve and sustain hand-hygiene compliance better than single-strategy approaches. [[PMID 28862335]]
- Contact precautions (gown, gloves, single room) for MDRO-colonised patients are guideline-standard, but systematic-review evidence of their incremental effectiveness over standard precautions shows conflicting results. [[PMID 26051927]] [[PMID 31304525]]
- Isolation carries measurable downsides — fewer clinician visits, adverse events, patient experience harm — informing the shift toward targeted precautions and universal gloving in endemic settings. [[PMID 26051927]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether contact precautions add transmission protection beyond good standard precautions and terminal cleaning is unresolved in endemic settings.
- Optimal isolation discontinuation criteria (clearance cultures versus time-based) are not standardised.

- The evidence controversy: targeted versus universal precautions.

## 930 — Infection risk with biologics: TB, HBV reactivation, PJP prophylaxis thresholds

topicId: `f9ba43c0-42a7-4f1d-b2c5-8b5fa1a1aff9`

### Mentor intro
Biologic and targeted therapies have transformed immune-mediated disease, but each mechanism opens a specific infection door: TNF inhibitors reactivate latent tuberculosis and hepatitis B, B-cell depletion disarms long-term humoral immunity, JAK inhibitors carry herpes-zoster and opportunist signals, and IL-6 blockade masks fever and CRP — the inflammatory alarms that normally announce infection. The ESGICH consensus documents systematise the safety profile agent-by-agent, while the EULAR 2022 recommendations (built on systematic review) define the screening and prophylaxis grammar: latent-TB testing and HBV serology before starting, antiviral prophylaxis for HBV carriers, and PJP prophylaxis when compounded immunosuppression crosses defined thresholds.

### Teaching points
- Screening before biologic initiation: latent-TB testing (IGRA/tuberculin) and HBV serology (HBsAg, anti-HBc) are standard across drug classes, with reactivation risk drug- and regimen-dependent. [[PMID 36323488]] [[PMID 29459143]]
- HBV reactivation in resolved or chronic carriers on B-cell-depleting or TNF therapy requires antiviral prophylaxis (entecavir/tenofovir) or intensified monitoring, per EULAR-informed risk stratification. [[PMID 36323488]] [[PMID 29447988]]
- PJP prophylaxis is recommended when high-dose steroids combine with biologic immunosuppression crossing defined thresholds (e.g. prolonged ≥20–30 mg prednisone-equivalent with second immunosuppressant). [[PMID 36323488]]
- Drug-specific infection signatures matter clinically: TNF inhibition raises TB, HBV, and deep fungal risk; IL-6 blockade blunts fever/CRP responses to infection; JAK inhibitors carry zoster and opportunist signals. [[PMID 29459143]] [[PMID 29447988]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal PJP prophylaxis thresholds for newer agents (JAK inhibitors, IL-23 blockade) are extrapolated rather than trial-defined.
- Whether HBV screening paradigms need updating for lower-risk agents is debated.

### MCQ angles
- Pre-biologic screening panel and timing.
- HBV reactivation risk tiers and prophylaxis decisions.
- PJP prophylaxis thresholds with combined immunosuppression.
- Drug-specific infection signatures (TB, zoster, blunted CRP response).

### Case hooks
- A patient starting rituximab for vasculitis has anti-HBc positive, HBsAg negative serology; outline HBV management.
- A patient on tocilizumab plus 25 mg prednisolone presents afebrile with 'normal CRP' and subtle pneumonia; apply drug-specific vigilance.
