## 861 — hyperemesis gravidarum

topicId: `591e8d76-8165-4d2d-b128-cf4cde0fe482`

### Mentor intro
Hyperemesis gravidarum is the severe end of the nausea-and-vomiting-of-pregnancy spectrum and the leading cause of hospitalisation in early pregnancy, affecting roughly 0.3–3% of pregnancies and carrying real maternal morbidity. Most NVP peaks between 7 and 12 weeks and resolves by week 20, which frames both the natural history and the safety calculus of treatment. Management is stepwise — dietary and ginger first, then antiemetics, with ondansetron's reassuring data quieting much of the first-trimester anxiety — and escalates to rehydration, thiamine and occasionally steroids for refractory disease.

### Teaching points
- Hyperemesis gravidarum, affecting approximately 0.3–3% of pregnancies, is the leading cause of hospitalisation during early pregnancy and is associated with substantial maternal morbidity. [[PMID 40843754]]
- Nausea and vomiting of pregnancy affects about 75% of women, begins early in the first trimester, peaks between 7 and 12 weeks, and abates by week 20 in over 90%. [[PMID 27512487]]
- ACOG Practice Bulletin 153 provides evidence-based management guidance for nausea and vomiting of pregnancy, recognising its impact on quality of life, healthcare costs and work absence. [[PMID 26287788]]
- Ginger supplementation has been evaluated in a systematic review for alleviating hyperemesis gravidarum, reflecting demand for alternatives to prescription medication. [[PMID 40226035]]

### Uncertainties
- The aetiology of HG remains incompletely explained, limiting targeted therapy.
- Optimal refractory-disease pathways (steroid dosing, enteral/parenteral nutrition thresholds) vary by centre.

### MCQ angles

- Stepwise antiemetic ladder and the ondansetron safety data.
- Thiamine before dextrose, and Wernicke prevention in prolonged vomiting.
- When to escalate to corticosteroids and nutrition support.

### Case hooks

- A patient requests 'something natural' before antiemetics; discuss ginger evidence and set expectations.

## 862 — Hypereosinophilic Syndrome

topicId: `51eb38a6-7023-462c-af60-480ae59d71a9`

### Mentor intro
Hypereosinophilic syndrome is where a blood count becomes a multi-organ emergency: abnormal eosinophil accumulation independent of secondary causes, with highly variable manifestations driven by which organs the cells infiltrate — heart, lung, gut, skin, nervous system. The field's modern backbone is classification: separating reactive (secondary) from clonal (primary, e.g. FIP1L1-PDGFRA) disease, because targeted therapy — imatinib for the tyrosine-kinase-driven form — transformed one subtype from chronic illness to molecularly-curable disease. Hypereosinophilia above 1.5 × 10⁹/L with end-organ damage defines the syndrome, and corticosteroids remain the rapid organ-protection bridge.

### Teaching points
- The eosinophilias span non-hematologic (secondary/reactive) and hematologic (primary/clonal) disorders with potential end-organ damage; hypereosinophilia is generally defined as a peripheral blood eosinophil count greater than 1.5 × 10⁹/L. [[PMID 38551368]]
- Hypereosinophilic syndrome comprises disorders of abnormal eosinophil accumulation in blood or peripheral tissues independent of known secondary causes, with highly variable clinical manifestations. [[PMID 26475367]]
- Refined international diagnostic criteria and classification separate eosinophilia from hypereosinophilic syndrome, with organ damage often prompting the HES diagnosis. [[PMID 36207764]]

### Uncertainties
- Organ-damage prediction from eosinophil counts alone is poor, so surveillance intensity is empirical.
- Sequencing of steroids, imatinib and emerging targeted agents in non-clonal HES is individualised.

### MCQ angles
- Definition thresholds and the reactive versus clonal classification.
- End-organ patterns: Loeffler endocarditis, neuropathy, dermatologic and GI involvement.
- FIP1L1-PDGFRA and the imatinib-responsive subtype.
- Steroids as acute organ-protection therapy.

### Case hooks
- A patient with persistent eosinophilia of 8 × 10⁹/L and a new murmur; work through organ assessment and initial therapy.
- A patient with clonal HES is found FIP1L1-PDGFRA positive; explain why imatinib changes everything.

### Gaps
- Molecular testing algorithms, mepolizumab/benralizumab positioning and anticoagulation strategies are not covered by the retained abstracts; verify against current consensus guidance before approval.

### Sources

- PMID 36207764 — Proposed refined diagnostic criteria and classification of eosinophil disorders and related syndromes.

## 863 — hyperglycaemic hyperosmolar state

topicId: `ff1f0546-f853-47d9-b610-1cf178764f8d`

### Mentor intro
Hyperglycaemic hyperosmolar state is the hyperglycaemic emergency of older, type 2, often previously-undiagnosed diabetic patients — profound hyperglycaemia and hyperosmolarity with enough residual insulin to suppress ketogenesis, so no significant acidosis. It carries higher mortality than DKA, driven by the age and comorbidity of its victims, and its treatment is a discipline of slow rehydration and careful osmolality decline: fluids first, insulin lower-dose, and obsessive attention to sodium correction as glucose falls.

### Teaching points
- DKA and HHS are the most serious hyperglycemic emergencies; HHS occurs more in adult and elderly type 2 diabetes patients, with overlapping clinical features. [[PMID 28372715]]
- DKA and HHS are life-threatening complications increasing mortality and morbidity, and reaching uniform consensus on diagnostic criteria and treatment remains a challenge. [[PMID 36405291]]
- DKA and HHS are characterised by insulinopaenia and severe hyperglycaemia, requiring prompt recognition, diagnosis and treatment. [[PMID 26893262]]

### Uncertainties
- Diagnostic boundaries between HHS, HHS-DKA overlap and severe hyperglycaemia are variably defined across guidelines.
- Optimal rehydration tonicity, osmolality decline targets and anticoagulation in HHS remain practice-dependent.

### MCQ angles
- HHS versus DKA: osmolality, acidosis, ketones, patient phenotype.
- Treatment sequence: aggressive fluids, low-dose insulin, potassium management.
- Corrected sodium and the risk of over-rapid osmolality shifts.
- Precipitant search (infection, MI, steroids, thiazides).

### Case hooks
- An 80-year-old has glucose 55 mmol/L, sodium 150, altered consciousness; plan the first 24 hours.
- A patient has features of both HHS and DKA; discuss how overlap changes management.

### Gaps
- Precise diagnostic cut-offs (effective osmolality, pH/bicarbonate thresholds), insulin infusion rates and phosphate management are not extractable from the retained abstracts; verify against the full ADA/EASD hyperglycaemic-crises consensus before approval.

## 864 — hyperkalaemia

topicId: `ba24c9aa-ebbc-4d35-b9b8-0730fa182498`

### Mentor intro
Hyperkalaemia is the electrolyte emergency everyone meets first and understands last: common, frequently iatrogenic, and dangerous because of malignant arrhythmia. Its management has three tiers with different speeds and purposes — membrane stabilisation with intravenous calcium, intracellular shift with insulin-dextrose and beta-agonists, and actual potassium removal with dialysis or binders — and the art lies in knowing which tier the patient needs and how long each buys. Controversies persist, not least around the older exchange resins and the role of the newer binders.

### Teaching points
- Hyperkalaemia is a common, potentially life-threatening electrolyte abnormality because of its association with malignant cardiac arrhythmias and sudden cardiac arrest, encountered across acute and chronic care and disproportionately affecting patients with kidney disease, heart failure and diabetes. [[PMID 42629001]]
- Classic hyperkalemia treatments and their controversies are evaluated in a dedicated review, including new medications for management. [[PMID 29731287]]

### Uncertainties
- Treatment thresholds for mild asymptomatic hyperkalaemia, especially with ECG ambiguity, vary across guidelines.
- The clinical value of newer potassium binders in chronic management versus acute settings is still being defined.

### MCQ angles
- ECG progression: peaked T waves, PR prolongation, sine wave.
- Three-tier management and the speed of each intervention.
- Common precipitants: CKD, RAAS inhibitors, potassium-sparing drugs, tissue breakdown.
- Pseudohyperkalaemia and confirmatory testing.

### Case hooks
- A CKD patient on lisinopril and spironolactone has K+ 6.4 with peaked T waves; sequence the treatment.
- An asymptomatic patient has K+ 6.0 with a normal ECG; decide on urgency and the confirmatory approach.

## 865 — hyperkalaemia emergency treatment

topicId: `ed16c5cb-4e09-425f-a3c5-4ccf6b486a0b`

### Mentor intro
Emergency hyperkalaemia treatment is a study in timelines: calcium works in minutes, insulin-dextrose and salbutamol shift potassium over an hour or two, and dialysis or binders are the only true removal. The KDIGO conference summary anchors the modern ED approach, including the pivotal insight that ECG changes are neither perfectly sensitive nor specific — which pushes treatment decisions toward the clinical picture and potassium trend rather than a single number. In cardiology patients the stakes are doubled: RAAS inhibitors and MRA therapy that prolong life also cause the hyperkalaemia, creating a treat-the-number versus protect-the-therapy dilemma.

### Teaching points
- A KDIGO consensus conference summarises acute hyperkalemia in the emergency department, noting predisposing conditions (kidney disease, heart failure, diabetes, tissue trauma) and medications including RAAS inhibitors. [[PMID 32852924]]
- Treatment options have evolved only modestly since the cation-exchange-resin era, while newer agents have entered the landscape, as reviewed in a 'something old, something new' framework. [[PMID 26880451]]
- Hyperkalaemia is one of the most common electrolyte disorders in cardiovascular patients; in population cohorts up to four in ten developed hyperkalaemia, in addition to drug-related risks. [[PMID 38289453]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether ECG-guided treatment thresholds improve outcomes over potassium-guided thresholds is not established.
- Balancing life-prolonging RAAS-inhibitor therapy against recurrent hyperkalaemia needs individualised protocols.

### Case hooks

## 866 — hyperkalemia diagnosis and management

topicId: `11c212f7-c3ce-4911-9595-0fc1722c824e`

### Mentor intro
No universally accepted consensus exists on hyperkalaemia management — thresholds, monitoring cadence and escalation vary across guidelines — which makes the underlying physiology the only reliable guide. Diagnosis starts with confirming the potassium is real (pseudohyperkalaemia is common), then grading severity and ECG changes. The newest chapter in management is pharmacological removal: the expert-panel literature on patiromer and sodium zirconium cyclosilicate reframes chronic hyperkalaemia as a manageable condition rather than an automatic reason to stop RAAS-inhibitor therapy.

### Teaching points
- Despite various guidelines, no universally accepted consensus exists on hyperkalemia monitoring and management, with variation in potassium thresholds and management approaches. [[PMID 33160639]]
- A multidisciplinary expert panel addresses the role of new potassium binders in hyperkalemia management, a condition frequent in CKD, heart failure, diabetes, hypertension and RAAS-inhibitor use. [[PMID 39604607]]
- Timely recognition and appropriate treatment of acute hyperkalaemia are critical to prevent arrhythmias and death, per an evidence-based emergency-care review. [[PMID 41506858]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether chronic binder therapy to enable RAAS inhibitors improves hard cardiovascular outcomes is not yet proven.
- Guideline heterogeneity in thresholds reflects absent trial-level evidence for specific cut-offs.

### MCQ angles

## 867 — Hypermobility spectrum disorders and hEDS: Beighton score, management, POTS association

topicId: `cf52cbc4-b51b-4f03-9e47-8703fe680f89`

### Mentor intro
Hypermobility spectrum disorders and hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome occupy the space between asymptomatic joint laxity and connective-tissue disease, and they are diagnosed late because they demand heightened clinical suspicion rather than a single test. The Beighton score quantifies laxity, but the clinical burden is systemic: gastrointestinal dysmotility and disorders of gut-brain interaction, autonomic dysfunction including POTS, chronic pain and fatigue. Modern guidance recognises this multi-system reality, with AGA guidance addressing the GI-autonomic overlap that drives much of the quality-of-life impact.

### Teaching points
- AGA guidance addresses evaluation and management of gut-brain-interaction disorders and autonomic or immune dysfunction coexisting with hEDS or hypermobility spectrum disorders, including POTS. [[PMID 40387691]]
- hEDS is a connective tissue disorder often diagnosed only after delays due to reliance on heightened clinical suspicion; hypermobility spectrum disorders bridge asymptomatic joint hypermobility and hEDS. [[PMID 42098539]]

## 868 — hypernatraemia

topicId: `8c2e4480-1436-4521-998d-4f342efda13c`

### Mentor intro
Hypernatraemia is a disorder of water balance, not sodium excess: total-body water deficit relative to sodium, generated either because intake failed (impaired thirst, no access, intubation) or because renal or extrarenal losses outpaced it. That framing makes the diagnostic approach logical — assess volume status, then urine osmolality and sodium — and the treatment dangerous if rushed: chronic hypernatraemia must be corrected slowly to avoid cerebral oedema, while acute-onset hypernatraemia can be corrected faster. Most cases signal an underlying access or consciousness problem and carry real mortality when mismanaged.

### Teaching points
- Hypernatremia is a disorder of the homeostatic balance of body water and sodium content, and that imbalance is the basis of the diagnostic approach. [[PMID 36578134]]
- In chronic dysnatremias a slow correction rate (<10 mmol/L/24 h) is indicated to avoid neurological complications, whereas acute dysnatremias (<48 h onset) may justify more rapid correction. [[PMID 31138251]]

### Uncertainties
- The safe correction ceiling in individual patients, especially with coexisting hyponatraemia risk factors, is approximated rather than precise.
- Whether desmopressin adjuncts to prevent overcorrection improve outcomes is not established.

### MCQ angles
- Diagnostic algorithm: volume status, urine osmolality, urine sodium.
- Central versus nephrogenic diabetes insipidus and the desmopressin test.
- Correction-rate limits and cerebral oedema risk.
- Common hospital-acquired causes: inadequate free water in dependent patients.

### Case hooks
- An intubated patient develops sodium 158 over three days; calculate the free-water deficit and plan the correction.
- A patient with traumatic brain injury passes large volumes of dilute urine with rising sodium; localise the cause.

### Gaps
- THIN EVIDENCE BASE (2 sources): the free-water-deficit formula, urine osmolality cut-offs and specific correction-fluid choices are not covered. Verify against a nephrology reference before approval.

### Sources

## 869 — Hypersensitivity Pneumonitis

topicId: `a8408ad7-e41e-4298-a3a1-ab60753a421d`

### Mentor intro
Hypersensitivity pneumonitis is what happens when an immune response to an inhaled antigen becomes an interstitial lung disease: complex, heterogeneous, and organised in modern guidelines around fibrotic and non-fibrotic phenotypes, which differ in course, management and prognosis. The antigen is often domestic or occupational — bird exposures, moulds, farming dusts — and the occupational-ILD literature reminds us the true burden is underestimated through under-recognition. Diagnosis is a convergence of exposure history, imaging, bronchoalveolar lavage and sometimes biopsy, and treatment begins with antigen removal.

### Teaching points
- Hypersensitivity pneumonitis is a complex, heterogeneous interstitial lung disease occurring when susceptible individuals develop exaggerated immune responses to inhaled antigens; guidelines organise evaluation around fibrotic and non-fibrotic phenotypes. [[PMID 35140104]]
- Millions of workers are exposed to substances causing occupational interstitial lung diseases, with the burden likely underestimated through under-recognition and under-reporting. [[PMID 37535448]]

### Uncertainties
- Antigen identification fails in a substantial minority despite detailed exposure assessment.
- Immunosuppression benefit in fibrotic HP versus non-fibrotic HP differs and is less evidence-based than in other ILDs.

### MCQ angles

- BAL lymphocytosis and its role.
- Occupational history-taking as the highest-yield diagnostic tool.

### Case hooks

## 870 — hypersensitivity pneumonitis diagnosis and antigen avoidance

topicId: `ebb373a1-9739-4952-8208-b1820c3243d8`

### Mentor intro
The single most effective treatment in hypersensitivity pneumonitis is not a drug — it is removing the antigen, and it works best in the non-fibrotic phenotype before irreversible remodelling sets in. But removal only works if you find the antigen, which is why the updated clinical definition and diagnostic recommendations from the international collaboration matter so much in practice: they sharpen exposure elicitation and phenotype assignment. Where fibrosis has established, the conversation shifts to immunosuppression, antifibrotic-adjacent strategies and transplant assessment.

### Teaching points
- An international collaboration has updated the clinical definition and diagnostic recommendations for hypersensitivity pneumonitis, focusing on fibrotic and non-fibrotic phenotypes, and their transfer to clinical practice is shaping management and prognosis. [[PMID 33470673]]
- HP ranges from acute manifestations to progressive fibrosis and is classified as nonfibrotic or fibrotic based on imaging and histopathological features, with pathophysiology driven by inhaled environmental antigens. [[PMID 42781417]]

### Uncertainties
- How aggressively to immunosuppress fibrotic HP, and whether antifibrotic therapy has a role, remains incompletely evidence-based.
- The prognosis impact of strict antigen avoidance versus partial reduction is not quantified.

### MCQ angles

- Fibrotic versus non-fibrotic prognostic divergence.
- Updated international diagnostic criteria and their phenotype focus.
- Escalation pathway: steroids, steroid-sparing agents, antifibrotics, transplant.
