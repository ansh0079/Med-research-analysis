## 961 — Interstitial Nephritis: Acute and Chronic

topicId: `2c075527-5015-498c-ab08-d1881247cfe0`

### Mentor intro
Acute interstitial nephritis is the great mimic of AKI: an idiosyncratic drug hypersensitivity reaction arriving 7-10 days after exposure to the culprit — antibiotics, PPIs, NSAIDs the commonest — with a sterile pyuria, modest proteinuria, and often systemic allergic features. The diagnostic trap is that the classic triad (fever, rash, eosinophilia) is frequently absent; eosinophiluria has fallen from favour, and biopsy remains the reference standard when suspicion is high and improvement does not follow drug withdrawal. Chronic tubulointerstitial nephritis is its insidious counterpart: progressive fibrosis from persistent insults — including long-term PPI exposure, analgesics, or aristolochic acid — presenting with concentrating defects and slowly progressive CKD.

### Teaching points
- Drug-induced AIN is a delayed hypersensitivity reaction appearing 7-10 days after culprit exposure; drugs account for the overwhelming majority of AIN cases, with antibiotics, PPIs, and NSAIDs the leading causes. [[PMID 28284381]]
- The classic allergic triad is frequently absent; diagnosis rests on clinical suspicion (AKI after a new drug, sterile pyuria, modest non-nephrotic proteinuria), and biopsy is the reference standard when suspicion persists. [[PMID 28284381]] [[PMID 30544782]]
- First-line treatment is withdrawal of the culprit drug with corticosteroids for persistent or biopsy-severe disease; early steroid responsiveness is the rule and delayed treatment risks incomplete recovery. [[PMID 28284381]] [[PMID 27072818]]
- Chronic tubulointerstitial nephritis — from prolonged PPI use, analgesics, toxins, or previous AIN — presents with tubular dysfunction (concentrating defects, acidosis, salt wasting) and progressive CKD requiring culprit withdrawal and supportive care. [[PMID 27072818]] [[PMID 30544782]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal steroid dose, duration, and whether early biopsy-guided therapy improves renal recovery lacks randomised evidence.
- The causal contribution of PPIs to CKD progression at population level remains debated despite consistent observational signals.

### MCQ angles
- Culprit-drug timeline and the absent-triad trap.
- Diagnostic work-up: when to biopsy.
- Steroid indications and response expectations.
- Chronic TIN versus glomerular CKD patterns.

### Case hooks
- A patient on a new antibiotic develops AKI with sterile pyuria but no rash or fever; assess for AIN and plan management.
- A long-term PPI user has slowly rising creatinine and bland sediment; evaluate chronic TIN and counsel on deprescribing.

### Gaps

## 962 — Intestinal Lymphoma

topicId: `942ea065-a632-4682-b1fc-1328ebf8962b`

### Mentor intro
The small bowel is the longest segment of the gut and the rarest site for cancer — but primary gastrointestinal lymphoma is the exception, the GI tract being the commonest extranodal site of non-Hodgkin lymphoma. The subtypes each carry a signature: diffuse large B-cell lymphoma as the dominant aggressive form; MALT lymphoma linked to Helicobacter in the stomach; enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma (now MEITL) as the feared complication of coeliac disease; and Burkitt-type disease in the immunocompromised. Presentation is non-specific — pain, obstruction, bleeding, weight loss — so the diagnostic route runs through CT enterography, capsule endoscopy, and deep biopsy, with specialised cross-sectional imaging for staging.

### Teaching points
- The GI tract is the most common extranodal site of non-Hodgkin lymphoma, though primary GI lymphoma remains a minority of all NHL; small-bowel primaries are disproportionately lymphomatous among small-bowel malignancies. [[PMID 31414733]] [[PMID 37652650]]
- Principal subtypes: DLBCL (dominant aggressive form), MALT (gastric, H. pylori-associated), enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma (coeliac-linked), and immunodeficiency-associated lymphomas. [[PMID 31414733]]
- Diagnosis requires endoscopic or surgical deep biopsy with immunophenotyping; cross-sectional imaging (CT/PET) and bone-marrow assessment stage the disease. [[PMID 37652650]] [[PMID 31414733]]
- Treatment is subtype- and stage-directed: H. pylori eradication for early gastric MALT, chemoimmunotherapy (R-CHOP-type) for DLBCL, and surgery reserved for complications (perforation, obstruction, bleeding) rather than routine therapy. [[PMID 31414733]] [[PMID 37652650]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal management of refractory/relapsed enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma is unsatisfactory and individualised.
- The role of surgery versus endoscopic management for small-bowel obstruction in lymphoma is case-based.

## 963 — Intestinal Pseudo-obstruction

topicId: `3ef3cd5a-42d7-45a4-bf0d-a67ad5068964`

### Mentor intro
Chronic intestinal pseudo-obstruction is dysmotility masquerading as obstruction: coordinated propulsive activity fails without a mechanical blockage, producing recurrent distension, pain, vomiting, and malnutrition. Diagnosis is one of exclusion — imaging to rule out obstruction, then manometry, scintigraphy, and full-thickness biopsy when available to characterise the neuromuscular pathology. Secondary causes (scleroderma, amyloid, opioids, paraneoplastic) must be hunted. Management combines nutritional support, prokinetic trials, and small-bowel transplantation for intestinal failure; pyridostigmine's systematic-review evidence makes it a first-line pharmacological option.

### Teaching points
- CIPO is a severe gastrointestinal dysmotility mimicking mechanical obstruction; diagnosis requires exclusion of mechanical causes followed by physiological testing (manometry, transit studies) and consideration of full-thickness biopsy. [[PMID 32073506]] [[PMID 29877952]]
- Aetiology is primary (visceral myopathy or neuropathy) or secondary — scleroderma, amyloidosis, endocrine disease, drugs (opioids, anticholinergics), paraneoplastic syndromes; identifying secondary causes redirects therapy. [[PMID 29877952]] [[PMID 32073506]]
- Management is multimodal: dietary modification and enteral/parenteral nutrition, prokinetic agents (pyridostigmine supported by systematic review), treatment of secondary causes, and decompressive or transplant surgery for intestinal failure. [[PMID 37132128]] [[PMID 32073506]]
- Pyridostigmine, an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor enhancing cholinergic drive, has systematic-review support as a treatment option for CIPO directed at improving colonic and gut motility. [[PMID 37132128]]

### Uncertainties
- The evidence base for most prokinetics and novel agents (prucalopride, mosapride) in CIPO is small and heterogeneous.
- Optimal timing and candidacy for intestinal transplantation in CIPO-related intestinal failure is individualised.

### MCQ angles

- Prokinetic options and pyridostigmine evidence.
- Nutrition and transplantation escalation.

### Case hooks

### Gaps

## 964 — Intestinal Tuberculosis

topicId: `22df1381-bf7a-4cd0-8cac-d0ff04e10c7b`

### Mentor intro
Intestinal tuberculosis returns wherever tuberculosis returns: the ileocaecal region is its favoured site, and the challenge is twofold — diagnosing a disease whose presentation (chronic abdominal pain, weight loss, ileocaecal ulceration, strictures) is near-identical to Crohn's disease, and treating it with a regimen proven for pulmonary TB but applied to sites where drug penetration and duration questions persist. The diagnostic modernisation is molecular and endoscopic: tissue PCR, biopsy culture, and endoscopic-ultrasound guided sampling raise the yield over old serology. Mistaking Crohn's for TB commits patients to unnecessary immunosuppression; mistaking TB for Crohn's delays effective therapy — the differentiating review is the clinical lifeline.

### Teaching points
- The ileocaecal region is the commonest site of intestinal TB; abdominal involvement accounts for about 11% of extra-pulmonary disease, with peritoneal and gastrointestinal forms predominating. [[PMID 27730779]] [[PMID 36899289]]
- Diagnosis combines clinical-radiological suspicion with tissue confirmation: endoscopic biopsy for caseating granulomas, culture, and molecular (PCR) testing; peritoneal fluid analysis and ultrasound/CT-guided sampling assist in peritoneal disease. [[PMID 36899289]] [[PMID 27730779]]
- The Crohn's-disease mimic is the central diagnostic trap; features favouring TB include pulmonary involvement, night sweats, ascites, patulous ileocaecal valve, and transverse ulcers, while longitudinal ulcers and perianal disease favour Crohn's. [[PMID 37468869]]
- Treatment follows standard anti-tubercular therapy (rifampicin-based multi-drug regimens); surgery is reserved for complications — obstruction, perforation, fistula, or bleeding. [[PMID 36899289]] [[PMID 27730779]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal duration of therapy for intestinal versus pulmonary TB, and the role of therapeutic trials, varies by guideline.
- Diagnostic-test accuracy of individual modalities (PCR versus culture versus histology) varies with prevalence and technique.

## 965 — Intra-abdominal infections: source control principles, empirical antibiotics, peritonitis

topicId: `2c2b922e-6e96-40b4-a313-241ac68bcac3`

### Mentor intro
Intra-abdominal infection is a two-front war: source control and antimicrobials, with source control the non-negotiable first front — no antibiotic regimen rescues an uncontrolled leak. The principles are physical: drain the pus, control the leak, restore anatomy, often staged with damage-control surgery in the unstable. Antimicrobial therapy covers the polymicrobial flora of secondary peritonitis (enteric gram-negatives, anaerobes, enterococci) with a source-appropriate, narrowable regimen; observational ICU cohorts show outcomes are dominated by severity, source control adequacy, and resistance patterns rather than agent choice alone.

### Teaching points
- Source control — drainage of infected collections, definitive control of contamination, and restoration of anatomy — is the foundation of intra-abdominal infection management; antibiotics cannot compensate for inadequate source control. [[PMID 27363829]] [[PMID 26568097]]
- Secondary peritonitis is polymicrobial; empirical regimens must cover enteric gram-negative bacilli and anaerobes, with anti-enterococcal cover for healthcare-associated and high-risk cases, then de-escalate on culture. [[PMID 26568097]] [[PMID 34037963]]
- Large ICU cohorts show mortality is driven by severity at onset, anatomical disruption extent, and inadequate source control — reinforcing early intervention and reassessment. [[PMID 34037963]] [[PMID 27363829]]
- Duration of therapy shortens dramatically with adequate source control: 4-7 days is supported for most controlled intra-abdominal infections, extending only for persistent foci or inadequate control. [[PMID 27363829]] [[PMID 26568097]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal duration (short versus standard) in specific sources (pancreatic, biliary) continues to evolve.
- The role of routine enterococcal and antifungal cover in community-acquired peritonitis is debated.

### MCQ angles

## 966 — Intra-aortic balloon pump and Impella: principles and ICU management

topicId: `9314074b-9a21-4a5b-931c-7f886f8d63cc`

### Mentor intro
Two devices carry most of the short-term mechanical-circulatory-support load. The intra-aortic balloon pump is the elder: counterpulsation inflating in diastole augments coronary perfusion and deflating in systole offloads the ventricle — modest support, easy to insert, but with the weakest outcome evidence (IABP-SHOCK II showed no mortality benefit in post-MI shock). The Impella transvalvular axial-flow pump provides far greater flows and true ventricular unloading, at the cost of vascular complications, haemolysis, and bleeding. Combined IABP-plus-Impella strategies appear in systematic review as an escalation option; complications surveillance — access-site, haemolysis, limb ischaemia — is the defining ICU management skill.

### Teaching points
- IABP counterpulsation augments diastolic coronary perfusion and reduces afterload; it provides modest haemodynamic support but IABP-SHOCK II showed no mortality benefit in post-MI cardiogenic shock. [[PMID 32793674]]
- Impella axial-flow pumps deliver higher flows with active ventricular unloading across the aortic valve; they suit higher-severity shock but carry vascular-access, bleeding, and haemolysis complications. [[PMID 32793680]] [[PMID 32793674]]
- Combined IABP-plus-Impella support is described in systematic review as an escalation strategy for refractory cardiogenic shock, adding pulsatility and afterload reduction to axial-flow output. [[PMID 38697884]]
- ICU management centres on complications surveillance: access-site bleeding, limb ischaemia, haemolysis (plasma-free haemoglobin monitoring), device position (echo surveillance), and anticoagulation titration. [[PMID 32793680]] [[PMID 32793674]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether Impella-based strategies improve mortality in cardiogenic shock (versus IABP or medical therapy alone) awaits definitive trials.
- Optimal anticoagulation intensity and device-weaning protocols are centre-specific.

### MCQ angles

## 967 — Intra-articular injections in OA: corticosteroids, hyaluronic acid, PRP: evidence review

topicId: `3de6b3ae-0f82-4745-9989-8fd421a0f529`

### Mentor intro
Intra-articular injections for osteoarthritis sit on contested evidence: guideline recommendations diverge by region and economics, and the placebo-controlled literature is humbling — intra-articular saline performs comparably to corticosteroid, PRP, and hyaluronic acid in hip-OA network meta-analysis, reminding clinicians that much of the injection benefit is contextual. The defensible reading: corticosteroids give short-term symptom relief with diminishing returns and possible cartilage concerns with repeated use; hyaluronic acid offers a modest, delayed benefit some guidelines endorse and others reject; PRP shows promise in comparative meta-analysis but standardisation and cost limit routine use.

### Teaching points
- Clinical-practice-guideline systematic review shows divergent regional recommendations for IA corticosteroid, hyaluronic acid, and PRP in knee OA — reflecting evidence uncertainty and economic context rather than clear efficacy differences. [[PMID 34485586]] [[PMID 35383651]]
- Placebo-controlled network meta-analysis in hip OA found intra-articular saline as effective as corticosteroid, PRP, and hyaluronic acid — a powerful demonstration of contextual and placebo effects in injection trials. [[PMID 32829298]]
- In knee-OA comparative meta-analysis, PRP, bone-marrow aspirate concentrate, and hyaluronic acid outperformed corticosteroids for symptom outcomes, though heterogeneity and commercial bias temper the conclusions. [[PMID 38331363]]
- AAOS guidance supports corticosteroid use for short-term knee-OA symptom relief while not recommending routine hyaluronic-acid injection; repeated steroid injections carry diminishing returns and theoretical cartilage-harm concerns. [[PMID 35383651]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether PRP's comparative benefit survives rigorous placebo-controlled standardised-protocol trials is unresolved.
- Long-term safety of repeated corticosteroid injections on cartilage remains incompletely defined.

## 968 — Intracerebral haemorrhage: blood pressure targets, reversal of anticoagulants

topicId: `a83ed924-603a-4b2c-8b51-3c0b6d6fde93`

### Mentor intro
Two interventions dominate the first hours of intracerebral haemorrhage, and both are about stopping the bleed. Blood-pressure lowering — the INTERACT2/ATACH lineage refined to a target around 130-140 mmHg systolic, achieved smoothly and early — reduces haematoma expansion, the strongest modifiable predictor of outcome. Anticoagulant reversal is the second: warfarin reversed with PCC plus vitamin K, the DOACs with specific reversal agents (idarucizumab for dabigatran; andexanet alfa or PCC for factor-Xa inhibitors), alongside platelet-transfusion caution — the PATCH lesson that indiscriminate platelet transfusion in antiplatelet-associated ICH may worsen outcome.

### Teaching points
- Early, smooth systolic blood-pressure lowering to approximately 130-140 mmHg limits haematoma expansion, the strongest modifiable predictor of poor functional outcome. [[PMID 31188036]] [[PMID 28887767]]
- Haematoma expansion is common within the first hours; blood-pressure variability and peaks are as important as the absolute achieved level — smooth control matters. [[PMID 38099439]] [[PMID 31188036]]
- Anticoagulant reversal is urgent: vitamin K plus four-factor PCC for warfarin; idarucizumab for dabigatran; andexanet alfa or PCC for factor-Xa inhibitors — with the agent's last-dose timing guiding urgency. [[PMID 28887767]] [[PMID 31188036]]
- Platelet transfusion for antiplatelet-associated ICH is not routine: the PATCH trial signal of harm without surgical indication cautions against indiscriminate transfusion. [[PMID 28887767]]

### Uncertainties
- How low to target systolic BP (intensive <130 versus 140-150) and the role of ultra-early aggressive lowering are refined but unsettled.
- The ideal reversal strategy for factor-Xa inhibitor ICH when andexanet is unavailable follows PCC-based protocols.

## 969 — Intracerebral hemorrhage acute management

topicId: `9e7811e0-3630-4ebc-8f14-f0ba8a07836c`

### Mentor intro
Acute ICH management beyond BP and reversal is a choreography of neuroprotection, surveillance, and escalation. The World Stroke Organization's synthesis of global guidelines provides the consensus frame: rapid CT, reversal of coagulopathy, BP control, and early neurosurgical assessment for cerebellar haemorrhage or deterioration — with inter-hospital transfer protocols for centres lacking neurosurgery. Raised intracranial pressure is managed with head positioning, osmotherapy (hypertonic saline or mannitol), and decompressive craniectomy for malignant MCA-pattern swelling; the cerebral-oedema guideline formalises selection and monitoring of these therapies. The through-line is organised systems — 'Code ICH' pathways compressing time-to-CT, time-to-reversal, and time-to-decision.

### Teaching points
- Global stroke-guideline synthesis supports the core acute-ICH bundle: rapid neuroimaging, coagulopathy reversal, controlled BP lowering, structured observation, and early surgical assessment for cerebellar or deteriorating haemorrhage. [[PMID 36725717]]
- Intracranial-pressure management uses tiered escalation: head-of-bed elevation, sedation, osmotherapy (hypertonic saline or mannitol), CSF diversion where indicated, and decompressive craniectomy for refractory malignant swelling. [[PMID 31262419]] [[PMID 32227294]]
- The cerebral-oedema guideline provides practical recommendations for selecting and monitoring osmotherapy and ICP-directed therapies in neurocritical care. [[PMID 32227294]]
- Cerebellar haemorrhage with brainstem compression or hydrocephalus is a surgical emergency for decompression and/or ventricular drainage — early recognition and transfer protocols determine outcome. [[PMID 36725717]] [[PMID 31262419]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether ICP-monitoring-guided therapy improves functional outcome in ICH is unproven.
- Optimal timing and selection for minimally-invasive haematoma evacuation remain under active trial evaluation.

### MCQ angles

## 970 — Intravenous fluid prescribing and fluid balance management

topicId: `4465c605-c116-4d17-b81c-637ad1f5d332`

### Mentor intro
Intravenous-fluid prescribing is the commonest inpatient intervention and among the least audited: fluids are drugs with indications, contraindications, and toxicity — yet they are often prescribed 'to keep the vein open' by the most junior team member. The modern evidence frame is the four Ds and the harm narrative: the evidence base for exactly when and how much resuscitation fluid remains thin in multimorbid acute medicine, while harm accrues quietly through fluid accumulation — positive balance drives oedema, AKI, and delayed recovery even when each bolus looked reasonable. Perioperative and thoracic-surgery data anchor the optimisation principle: goal-directed, moderate-volume strategies beat both liberal flooding and dogmatic restriction.

### Teaching points
- Fluid prescribing should follow drug-prescribing logic: assess need (hypovolaemia versus euvolaemia), choose fluid type deliberately (crystalloid default), define volume and endpoint, and review daily with a stop date. [[PMID 29858432]] [[PMID 34006046]]
- The evidence base for exactly when and how much resuscitation fluid to give in acute medicine is limited; uncertainty should translate into small aliquots with reassessment rather than large empirical boluses. [[PMID 29858432]]
- Cumulative positive fluid balance is an independent harm driver: 'zero fluid accumulation' is the therapeutic goal in critical illness, with deresuscitation (fluid removal) a deliberate phase of care. [[PMID 34006046]]
- Perioperative evidence supports optimised, goal-directed moderate-fluid strategies over liberal administration; thoracic-surgery data show excess fluid drives respiratory complications. [[PMID 34764839]]

### Uncertainties
- Which dynamic measures (passive leg raise, stroke-volume variation, echo) best guide fluid responsiveness at the bedside remains practice-variable.
- Optimal deresuscitation timing and triggers in established positive balance are not standardised.
