Hypersensitivity pneumonitis is not one disease but a family of them, and the antigen defines the member: bird fancier's lung, farmer's lung, hot-tub lung and the summer-type HP of humid climates each carry their own exposure pattern, clinical phenotype and prognosis. Systematic curation of reported HP-associated exposures — long missing — now underpins confident antigen identification, which remains the single most valuable diagnostic and therapeutic act in the disease, because removal of the right exposure is the only intervention that changes the natural history.

## 873 — hypertension guideline blood pressure target for adults

topicId: `0f5825d8-6d1e-4786-a91b-a1223b1b2235`

### Mentor intro
Blood-pressure targets are where guidelines genuinely diverge — and understanding why is more useful than memorising numbers. The American tradition targets ≤130/80 for most adults on intensive-control evidence; European guidance historically tolerated higher targets for achieved-benefit simplicity; and pragmatic primary-care frameworks like Hypertension Canada's exist because control rates regress when guidance is not implementable. An in-depth review of the 2021-era guidelines maps the concordant core (treat early, combine agents, measure properly) against the discordant edges (targets, initial combinations).

### Teaching points
- The Hypertension Canada guideline provides pragmatic primary-care-focused recommendations to improve adult hypertension management, addressing regressing treatment and control rates. [[PMID 40419299]]
- Multiple evidence-based national and international hypertension guidelines published within four years show mostly concordant recommendations with some discordant opinions, mapped in an in-depth review. [[PMID 34757122]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether the intensive <130/80 target generalises to all subgroups outside trial populations is debated.
- How to operationalise individualised targets in routine practice is unresolved across guidelines.

## 874 — Hypertension treatment intensity

topicId: `7b96355c-1d3d-4470-8734-c4becfdeb627`

### Mentor intro
How low to drive blood pressure is hypertension's defining controversy. Intensive control — the ≤130/80 philosophy — has trial evidence for cardiovascular protection, but its net clinical benefit varies by target and patient characteristics, and benefit-harm trade-off analyses now quantify what guidelines assert. The counterpoint literature reminds us the optimal target remains undefined, that more intense therapy carries hypotension, renal and electrolyte costs, and that 'lower is better' is a population claim that needs individual calibration at the bedside.

### Teaching points
- Although intensive blood pressure control is recommended by major guidelines, its overall benefit-harm balance remains uncertain; net clinical benefit varies by blood pressure target and patient characteristics. [[PMID 40902616]]
- The optimal target blood pressure is undefined and controversial; recent American guidelines recommend ≤130/80 mm Hg for essentially all adults, while more intense versus standard therapy trade-offs continue to be debated. [[PMID 35636475]]

### Uncertainties
- The harms threshold — at what achieved BP hypotension-related events offset cardiovascular gains — differs by frailty and age.
- Real-world adherence to intensive targets is substantially worse than trial conditions, diluting population benefit.

### MCQ angles

### Mentor intro
Treating hypertension in the very elderly is where the evidence-based and the humane can collide. Systolic hypertension dominates as arteries stiffen, and trial data support treatment well into the ninth decade — but frailty, orthostasis, polypharmacy and limited life expectancy all argue for targets individualised beyond the population guideline. The Cochrage update on higher targets found the major trials aimed at modest systolic goals and demonstrated cardiovascular benefit, while frailty-focused guidance stresses standing pressures, deprescribing review and shared decision-making over rigid numbers.

### Teaching points

The modern paradigm has shifted upstream: first-trimester combined screening (maternal factors, blood pressure, uterine-artery Doppler, biomarkers) to identify high-risk women, and low-dose aspirin started by 16 weeks to prevent early-onset disease, the subtype with the worst outcomes.

## 878 — Hypertensive emergency: end-organ damage, IV agents, blood pressure targets

topicId: `e35b75f4-bd60-48c4-86db-bd9ad51ef0e5`

### Mentor intro
A hypertensive emergency is not a number — it is acute blood-pressure rise with new or worsening end-organ damage, and everything follows from identifying the damaged organ: encephalopathy, intracranial haemorrhage, acute coronary syndrome, pulmonary oedema, or acute kidney injury. Management is deliberately heterogeneous and agent-matched: labetalol, nicardipine, esmolol, glyceryl trinitrate and sodium nitroprusside each fit specific organ scenarios, and the goal is controlled, partial reduction — typically around 20–25% in the first hours — because normalising pressure too fast steals cerebral and coronary perfusion. Malignant hypertension, defined by retinal haemorrhages or exudates with acute renal deterioration, is the phenotype that most often reaches the ICU.

### Teaching points
- A hypertensive crisis is a sudden, significant BP rise to 180/120 mmHg or higher and is a medical emergency capable of causing heart attack, stroke and other life-threatening problems; management strategies are evaluated in a systematic review. [[PMID 39262522]]
- Patients with hypertensive emergencies, malignant hypertension and acute severe hypertension are managed heterogeneously; initiating therapy and setting acute BP goals requires scenario-specific considerations, per the British and Irish Hypertension Society. [[PMID 36418425]]
- Malignant hypertension is defined by bilateral retinal haemorrhages or exudates, with or without papilloedema, plus acute heart failure and acute deterioration in renal function. [[PMID 32100590]]

### Uncertainties
- The ideal first-line IV agent by clinical scenario remains partly institution- and resource-dependent.
- Precise BP-reduction targets in each end-organ emergency vary across position documents.

### MCQ angles

## 879 — Hypertensive Nephrosclerosis

topicId: `a2e53eff-eca1-4e43-9470-e00278a3084f`

### Mentor intro
Hypertensive nephrosclerosis — nephroangiosclerosis — is the kidney disease that accompanies chronic essential hypertension, known for over a century and still diagnosed more by clinical context than by biopsy, which is reserved for doubtful or atypical cases. The clinical picture is one of slowly progressive chronic kidney disease with subnephrotic proteinuria in a patient with long-standing, often poorly controlled hypertension. The modern nuance is that the causal arrow is not always simple: some 'hypertensive' nephrosclerosis may reflect primary renal microvascular disease driving the hypertension rather than vice versa, particularly in certain ancestries.

### Teaching points
- Nephroangiosclerosis — kidney disease accompanying chronic essential hypertension — has been recognised for over a century; definitive diagnosis is by renal biopsy, reserved for doubtful or atypical cases. [[PMID 36050216]]

### Uncertainties
- The bidirectionality question — whether renal microvascular disease precedes hypertension in some patients — is unresolved.
- Whether aggressive BP control halts established nephrosclerosis beyond general CKD benefit is inferred rather than proven.

### MCQ angles

- When biopsy is indicated (atypical features, active urinary sediment).
- Differential: other secondary hypertension causes with renal involvement.
- Management as CKD with strict BP control and renin-angiotensin blockade.

## 880 — hyperthyroidism

topicId: `314b5d24-82c4-4f8d-bf3c-ae656499c674`

### Mentor intro
Hyperthyroidism is common — roughly 0.2–1.4% of people worldwide — and its management rewards precise diagnosis because the aetiology dictates the treatment. Overt disease is biochemically defined by suppressed TSH with elevated free T4 and/or T3; beyond that, the thyrotoxicosis work-up separates true hyperthyroidism (Graves' disease, toxic nodular goitre — hormone overproduction) from thyroiditis (hormone leak), using TRAb, uptake scanning and clinical signs such as orbitopathy. Each pathway has distinct therapy: antithyroid drugs, radioiodine, surgery, or simply time and beta-blockade.

### Teaching points
- Overt hyperthyroidism — suppressed TSH with high T3 and/or free T4 — affects approximately 0.2% to 1.4% of people worldwide; subclinical disease is defined by low TSH with normal hormone levels. [[PMID 37847271]]
- Clinical suspicion should be confirmed biochemically (low TSH, high FT4 or FT3), with a global prevalence of 0.2–1.3%. [[PMID 36848916]]
- Hyperthyroidism refers to increased hormone synthesis and secretion, most commonly from Graves' disease or toxic nodular goitre, whereas thyroiditis causes thyrotoxicosis through hormone leak. [[PMID 38278171]]

### Uncertainties
- Antithyroid-drug duration, relapse prediction and the long-term Graves' management pathway vary by guideline and patient factors.
- Management of subclinical hyperthyroidism, particularly in older adults, is individualised.

### MCQ angles
- Biochemical patterns: overt versus subclinical; T3 toxicosis.
- Graves' versus toxic nodular versus thyroiditis differentiation.
- TRAb and radionuclide uptake in the diagnostic algorithm.
- Treatment modalities: thionamides, radioiodine, surgery — selection logic.

### Case hooks
- A patient has tremor, weight loss and suppressed TSH with elevated FT4; outline the aetiology work-up.
- A Graves' patient relapses after completing thionamide therapy; discuss definitive-therapy options.

### Gaps
