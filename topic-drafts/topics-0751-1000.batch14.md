## 881 — Hyperthyroidism: Graves disease vs toxic nodular goitre, TSH receptor antibodies

topicId: `77bc8c1a-a626-4abc-ba17-2900b1771c4c`

### Mentor intro
Separating Graves' disease from toxic nodular goitre is the pivotal fork in thyrotoxicosis management, and the ATA guideline supplies the algorithm. Both share suppressed TSH and elevated hormones; they diverge on TRAb — positive in Graves', negative in nodular disease — and on the radionuclide uptake scan, which shows diffuse uptake in Graves' versus patchy or focal uptake in toxic nodular goitre. The distinction is not academic: Graves' warrants consideration of antithyroid drugs as first-line with thionamide complications in view, while toxic nodular goitre virtually never remits on drugs and heads toward radioiodine or surgery.

### Teaching points
- The ATA guideline describes evidence-based diagnosis and management of hyperthyroidism and other causes of thyrotoxicosis, where appropriate treatment requires accurate diagnosis and is influenced by comorbidity and patient preference. [[PMID 27521067]]

### Uncertainties
- TRAb-negative thyrotoxicosis sometimes needs imaging to resolve the aetiology, and uptake scans are contraindicated in pregnancy.
- Whether Graves' first-line should be thionamides or radioiodine varies by guideline, geography and patient factors.

### MCQ angles
- TRAb testing as the first-line differentiator.
- Uptake-scan patterns: diffuse (Graves') versus focal (toxic nodular).
- Thyroid orbitopathy as a Graves'-specific clinical marker.
- Treatment-path divergence between the two aetiologies.

### Case hooks
- A thyrotoxic patient is TRAb-positive with orbitopathy; confirm the aetiology and outline management.
- A TRAb-negative patient has a palpable multinodular goitre; plan the uptake scan and its interpretation.

## 882 — Hypertriglyceridaemia: pancreatitis risk threshold, fibrates, omega-3, dietary management

topicId: `30153238-c897-4193-abe9-25e629ed34a6`

### Mentor intro
Hypertriglyceridaemia is a two-front problem: very high levels drive acute pancreatitis — the traditional emergency — while moderate elevations contribute to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease through triglyceride-rich lipoproteins, a causal role now supported by multiple lines of evidence. Management starts with the threshold logic: pancreatitis risk rises steeply above roughly 10 mmol/L, demanding immediate triglyceride lowering with fibrates and strict dietary fat restriction. The cardiovascular front is where the field is moving fastest, with emerging agents targeting triglyceride-rich lipoproteins addressing the unmet need left by fibrates and omega-3s.

### Teaching points
- Hypertriglyceridemia is associated with increased risk of acute pancreatitis and has more recently been linked to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. [[PMID 33027225]]
- HTG arises from defects in triglyceride-rich lipoprotein metabolism, with increased morbidity and mortality from pancreatitis and ASCVD; traditional therapies include fibrates and omega-3 agents. [[PMID 39964788]]
- Multiple lines of evidence support a causal role of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, driving development of novel targeted therapies. [[PMID 41012446]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether triglyceride-lowering beyond LDL reduction improves cardiovascular outcomes awaits outcomes-trial confirmation for the newest agents.
- The precise pancreatitis-risk threshold varies across studies and guideline statements.

### MCQ angles
- Pancreatitis-risk triglyceride thresholds and acute management (fibrate, no alcohol, low-fat diet).
- Secondary causes: diabetes, alcohol, hypothyroidism, drugs.
- Fibrates, omega-3 and statin-interaction considerations.
- Emerging TRL-targeted therapies and their rationale.

### Case hooks

## 883 — Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy myosin inhibitor therapy

topicId: `91f1d2b9-90bb-46b8-85f3-2d2a0f2d2870`

### Mentor intro
Mavacamten opened a new era in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy by targeting the disease mechanism itself: a selective, reversible cardiac myosin inhibitor that reduces excessive actin-myosin cross-bridging, easing hypercontractility, improving compliance and — critically — reducing the left-ventricular outflow-tract gradient that defines obstructive disease. For patients whose symptoms persisted despite beta-blockers and disopyramide, the myosin-inhibitor class converts 'optimise and accept' into a pharmacological option with measurable gradient and symptom endpoints, at the price of needing careful echo-supervised titration for systolic dysfunction.

### Teaching points
- Mavacamten, the first selective reversible cardiac myosin inhibitor, reduces excessive actin-myosin cross-bridging, decreasing myocardial hypercontractility in obstructive HCM. [[PMID 40624601]]
- Obstructive HCM results from asymmetric septal hypertrophy obstructing left-ventricular outflow and is linked to sarcomeric-protein gene mutations. [[PMID 36278454]]
- HCM involves abnormal left-ventricular hypertrophy without common causes; the most common form involves the basal septum and can obstruct the LV outflow tract, causing exertional symptoms. [[PMID 37064432]]

### Uncertainties
- Long-term outcomes — whether myosin inhibitors alter mortality or just symptoms and gradients — are not yet established.
- Optimal positioning versus septal reduction therapy (myectomy, alcohol ablation) in severe obstruction is evolving.

### MCQ angles
- Myosin-inhibitor mechanism: cross-bridge reduction and its echo-supervised titration.
- EXPLORER-HCM-style endpoints: gradient reduction and symptom improvement.
- Systolic-dysfunction risk and monitoring requirements.
- Where the class fits relative to beta-blockers, disopyramide and septal reduction.

### Case hooks

## 884 — Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: obstruction management, SCD risk stratification, mavacamten

topicId: `81bf1a44-4544-40f8-b3f5-9b6092ac5eb3`

### Mentor intro
Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy has transformed from a feared diagnosis of sudden death into a contemporary, highly treatable condition, and the 2024 AHA/ACC guideline consolidates that shift. Two skills organise care: relieving obstruction — pharmacologically with beta-blockers, disopyramide and now mavacamten, or procedurally with septal myectomy or alcohol ablation — and stratifying sudden-cardiac-death risk to decide who needs an ICD. Personalised pathways along these axes now alter the natural history of a disease that remains the commonest inherited cardiomyopathy.

## 885 — hyperuricaemia

topicId: `b1d8151e-e8f4-4251-9ab5-081908782930`

### Mentor intro
Hyperuricaemia is the biochemical substrate of gout — monosodium urate crystals form when urate exceeds its solubility threshold — and gout is the commonest inflammatory arthritis worldwide, affecting 41 million adults and still rising. Management remains stubbornly suboptimal: renal and gut urate excretion sit centrally in regulation, which is why urate-lowering therapy targets excretion pathways, and why diet — while contributory — is rarely sufficient alone. The modern frame is multifactorial: genetic predisposition, environmental stimuli and gut dysbiosis all feed a disease that is curable with properly titrated urate-lowering therapy.

### Teaching points
- Gout is a chronic disease of monosodium urate crystal deposition forming at increased urate concentrations; renal and gut excretion of urate is central to serum-urate regulation. [[PMID 27112094]]
- Gout affects 41 million adults worldwide with increasing burden over three decades, yet management remains suboptimal, and diet's impact on hyperuricemia is an active review area. [[PMID 34802900]]
- Gout incidence has risen steadily and management remains unsatisfactory, with multifactorial aetiology spanning genetic predisposition, environmental stimuli and gut dysbiosis alongside recent therapeutic advances. [[PMID 40153315]]

### Uncertainties
- The treat-to-target serum-urate threshold and how aggressively to push in tophaceous disease varies by guideline.
- When asymptomatic hyperuricaemia itself warrants treatment is unresolved.

- Dietary contributors: purines, fructose, alcohol (beer, spirits).

## 886 — hypocalcaemia

topicId: `260981dc-f2c0-480b-a467-2e9e64272cfd`

### Mentor intro
Hypocalcaemia ranges from an asymptomatic biochemical finding to tetany, seizures and laryngospasm, and its urgency tracks both the level and the speed of fall — chronic hypocalcaemia is tolerated at concentrations that would be dangerous if acute. The diagnostic frame mirrors the PTH axis: hypocalcaemia with low or inappropriately normal PTH points to parathyroid failure (surgical hypoparathyroidism is the commonest cause after thyroid surgery), while high PTH indicates secondary hyperparathyroidism from vitamin D deficiency or chronic kidney disease. Acute symptomatic disease is a calcium-and-magnesium emergency.

### Teaching points
- Normal serum calcium is 8–10 mg/dL, and abnormal levels should be confirmed with albumin-adjusted or ionized calcium before acting. [[PMID 28806048]]
- Hypoparathyroidism is the most common complication of bilateral thyroid operations, and strategies for minimising and preventing it are defined by an ATA Surgical Affairs Committee statement. [[PMID 29848235]]

### Uncertainties
- Calcium-replacement thresholds for asymptomatic chronic hypocalcaemia are individualised.
- Whether calcimimetics or PTH-replacement therapy improve long-term outcomes in chronic hypoparathyroidism is still being defined.

### MCQ angles
- Adjusted-versus-ionized calcium and confirmation before treatment.
- PTH-based differential: hypoparathyroidism versus vitamin D deficiency versus CKD.
- Chvostek and Trousseau signs; QT prolongation.
- Acute management: IV calcium gluconate, then oral with vitamin D; check magnesium.

### Case hooks
- A post-thyroidectomy patient develops perioral tingling and a positive Chvostek sign; manage the acute phase and anticipate hypoparathyroidism.
- A malnourished patient has hypocalcaemia with high PTH; identify the mechanism and treat.

## 887 — Hypoglycaemia: causes, treatment thresholds, hypoglycaemia unawareness

topicId: `e2f68ff5-1321-4645-97c6-b749486051b0`

### Mentor intro
Hypoglycaemia is the limiting toxicity of insulin and sulfonylurea therapy: common, frightening, and associated with distress, medication nonadherence and cardiovascular events. The classification matters — level 1 (alert), level 2 (clinically significant, <3.0 mmol/L), level 3 (severe, requiring assistance) — because treatment thresholds and documentation obligations differ. The cruellest complication is hypoglycaemia unawareness: repeated episodes blunt the autonomic warning system, so neuroglycopenia arrives without warning, a problem addressed by hypoglycaemia avoidance and structured education.

### Teaching points
- Hypoglycemia unawareness describes diminished autonomic and neuroglycopenic warning symptoms, carrying major clinical implications for people with diabetes and others. [[PMID 38397994]]
- An Endocrine Society guideline addresses management of people with diabetes at high risk of hypoglycaemia on insulin and sulfonylureas, where hypoglycaemia drives distress, nonadherence and morbidity. [[PMID 36477488]]
- Hypoglycaemia complicates management of both type 1 and type 2 diabetes and remains a barrier to optimal glycaemic control despite newer technologies. [[PMID 35775070]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether CGM-driven alert systems eliminate severe hypoglycaemia in all risk groups is still being tested.
- The best protocol for restoring hypoglycaemia awareness (strict avoidance versus technology) is individualised.

### MCQ angles

- The 15-15 rule and glucagon indications.

## 888 — Hypogranular APL Morphology Pitfalls

topicId: `1820ce8a-c9bc-49cf-8e7b-9c0abd530630`

### Mentor intro
The hypogranular (microgranular) variant of acute promyelocytic leukaemia is the morphology trap of haematopathology: blasts so fine-grained that the diagnostic granules are invisible on light microscopy, sometimes masquerading as monocytic leukaemias — yet carrying the same t(15;17) PML::RARA rearrangement, the same life-threatening coagulopathy, and the same curative ATRA-arsenic pathway. Worse, a minority have cryptic or variant rearrangements invisible to standard cytogenetics and even FISH, where RT-PCR and optical genome mapping rescue the diagnosis. Missing it delays ATRA, and every hour of delay costs lives.

### Teaching points
- APL is a distinct AML subtype with a unique morphological appearance, associated coagulopathy and canonical balanced translocation between chromosomes 15 and 17. [[PMID 28352191]]
- APL management evolved with ATRA plus anthracycline chemotherapy providing cure rates exceeding 80%, and modern regimens continue to refine this combination. [[PMID 27084953]]
- APL is characterised by abnormal promyelocytes and t(15;17) PML::RARA, but rare patients have cryptic or variant rearrangements where RT-PCR and optical genome mapping secure the diagnosis; ATRA/arsenic is largely curative provided the diagnosis is made. [[PMID 39858554]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether all cryptic-variant APL responds identically to standard ATRA-arsenic is extrapolated from small series.
- How broadly optical genome mapping should be deployed when morphology and FISH conflict is unsettled.

### MCQ angles
- Hypogranular variant morphology and its monocytic mimics.
- The t(15;17) / PML::RARA cytogenetics and cryptic forms.
- Immediate ATRA on suspicion and the coagulopathy management principle.
- ATO-ATRA therapy lineage and cure rates.

### Case hooks
- A leukaemic picture with monocytic features and DIC prompts morphological review; recognise the hypogranular trap.
- A patient with APL morphology has negative cytogenetics and FISH; decide the molecular next step.

### Gaps

## 889 — Hypokalaemia

topicId: `4d526c58-d64b-4a21-a721-4cbe4da03411`

### Mentor intro
Hypokalaemia is a potassium- and magnesium-dependent problem with a blood-pressure and acid-base fingerprint: the combination of hypokalaemia with hypertension points to mineralocorticoid excess (primary aldosteronism, Cushing's, licorice), while hypokalaemia with metabolic alkalosis and normotension suggests renal losses from diuretics, vomiting or the inherited tubulopathies — Gitelman syndrome being the classic hypokalaemic-metabolic-alkalosis-with-hypomagnesaemia diagnosis. Iatrogenic causes matter too: beta-lactam antibiotics are an under-recognised cause of potentially serious hypokalaemia. ECG effects — U waves, flattened T waves, arrhythmia — and rhabdomyolysis mark severity.

### Teaching points
- Beta-lactam antibiotic-associated hypokalemia is a potentially life-threatening electrolyte disorder reviewed in a dedicated narrative review. [[PMID 39161253]]
- Gitelman syndrome is a rare salt-losing tubulopathy characterised by hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis with hypomagnesemia and hypocalciuria, caused by inactivating SLC12A3 mutations. [[PMID 28003083]]

### Uncertainties
- Potassium-replacement route and speed thresholds, especially with ECG changes, vary by protocol.
- Optimal magnesium co-repletion targets and chronic tubulopathy management are individualised.

## 890 — hyponatraemia

topicId: `f6d71ac0-1636-40aa-897a-b94cf7ba6461`

### Mentor intro
Hyponatraemia is the commonest electrolyte disorder — around 5% of adults and 35% of hospitalised patients — and even mild cases carry falls, cognitive impairment and mortality signal. The diagnostic spine is volume assessment and urine osmolality, separating hypotonic from non-hypotonic causes, then narrowing within the hypotonic group by urine sodium: SIAD dominates the euvolaemic category through non-osmotic AVP release, while diuretics and hypovolaemia lead the low-urine-sodium group. The treatment paradox frames everything: too slow risks osmotic demyelination from chronic hyponatraemia, too fast risks cerebral oedema in acute hyponatraemia — and severe symptomatic disease earns hypertonic saline under close monitoring.

### Teaching points
- Hyponatremia is the most common electrolyte disorder, affecting approximately 5% of adults and 35% of hospitalised patients; it is defined by serum sodium below 135 mEq/L and most commonly results from water retention, with even mild cases carrying risk. [[PMID 35852524]]
- SIAD is the most frequent cause of hypotonic hyponatraemia, mediated by non-osmotic arginine-vasopressin release, and affects more than 15% of hospitalised patients overall. [[PMID 36974717]]
- Emergency-department hyponatraemia presents with unspecific symptoms including recent falls, weakness and vertigo, with common causes including diuretics. [[PMID 35870366]]

### Uncertainties
- Vaptan use outside ICU settings and fluid-restriction-versus-salt-plus-loop strategies in SIAD vary by guideline.
- The chronicity cut-off for choosing correction speed is often unknowable at presentation, forcing cautious defaults.

### MCQ angles
- Diagnostic algorithm: volume status, urine osmolality, urine sodium.
- SIAD criteria and its mimic checklist (hypothyroid, adrenal insufficiency, diuretics).
- Correction-rate limits and osmotic-demyelination prevention.
- Hypertonic saline indications for severe symptomatic hyponatraemia.

### Case hooks
- A confused elderly patient on bendroflumethiazide has sodium 118; classify and manage the correction.
- A euvolaemic patient post-pneumonia has sodium 124 with urine osmolality high; confirm SIAD and treat.

### Gaps
