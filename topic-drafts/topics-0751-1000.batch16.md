## 901 — Icosapent ethyl residual cardiovascular risk

topicId: `04ea1a12-b804-492a-8bb4-c335b9180d0f`

### Mentor intro
Icosapent ethyl sits at the intersection of residual-risk medicine and trial controversy. In REDUCE-IT, high-purity EPA reduced cardiovascular events among statin-treated patients with elevated triglycerides, but the magnitude of benefit, the mineral-oil comparator question, and the failure of the DHA-containing STRENGTH trial have kept the indication debated. Clinically, the drug is not for everyone with high triglycerides: the benefit signal concentrates in statin-treated patients with established ASCVD (or high risk) and triglycerides in the 135–499 mg/dL band, making patient selection the core skill.

### Teaching points
- REDUCE-IT established that high-purity eicosapentaenoic acid (icosapent ethyl) reduces cardiovascular events on top of statins in high-risk patients with persistent hypertriglyceridaemia. [[PMID 31715195]]
- Eligibility nuance matters: benefit concentrates in statin-treated patients with triglycerides roughly 135–499 mg/dL and LDL-C controlled, not in unselected hypertriglyceridaemia. [[PMID 32770402]]
- The neutral DHA-containing STRENGTH trial and the mineral-oil comparator debate mean EPA-specific benefit is contested and generalisation to mixed omega-3 preparations is not supported. [[PMID 35499805]] [[PMID 33651748]]
- Adverse-effect monitoring includes atrial fibrillation and bleeding signals that weighed into regulatory and formulary decisions. [[PMID 35499805]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether the REDUCE-IT benefit is fully attributable to EPA versus comparator effects remains debated; biomarker mediation analyses continue.
- Cost-effectiveness in lower-risk primary-prevention populations is unresolved in most health systems.

### MCQ angles
- REDUCE-IT population, triglyceride band, and endpoint hierarchy.
- IPE versus mixed EPA/DHA preparations after STRENGTH.
- The mineral-oil comparator controversy and how to counsel patients.
- AF and bleeding adverse effects and drug interactions (anticoagulants).

### Case hooks
- A statin-controlled post-MI patient has triglycerides 210 mg/dL; decide whether icosapent ethyl is indicated and defend the answer against a STRENGTH objection.
- A patient on apixaban with intermittent AF is offered icosapent ethyl for residual risk; weigh benefit against bleeding.

### Gaps
- The retained abstracts are reviews rather than the REDUCE-IT primary report; verify exact eligibility thresholds, endpoint rates, and NNT against the NEJM 2019 primary publication before approval.

## 902 — ICU-acquired delirium: prevention, assessment, and treatment

topicId: `f4b618f6-d303-4f02-81d8-94e778f15b7e`

### Mentor intro
ICU delirium is the syndrome the PADIS guidelines were built to prevent — and the 2022 SCCM update sharpened the message: routine deep sedation should be minimised, spontaneous awakening and breathing trials paired, early mobility promoted, and sleep protected. Pharmacological prevention and treatment with antipsychotics lacks outcome evidence, so management is environmental and process-based. The arc of care extends past discharge: post-intensive care syndrome bundles rehabilitation for the cognitive, physical, and psychological sequelae that outlast the ICU stay.

### Teaching points
- The PADIS (2018) and 2022 SCCM guidelines frame delirium management around ABCDEF bundle elements: analgesia-first sedation, spontaneous awakening/breathing trials, choice of lighter sedation, delirium monitoring, early mobility, and family engagement. [[PMID 30113379]] [[PMID 35119438]]
- Non-pharmacological prevention — reorientation, sleep hygiene, early mobilisation, sensory aids — is the evidence-backed core; antipsychotics are not recommended for routine prevention or treatment. [[PMID 30113379]] [[PMID 35119438]]
- Delirium in older persons is common, serious, and under-recognised, with lasting effects on function and cognition that justify systematic screening in at-risk populations. [[PMID 28973626]]
- Post-intensive care syndrome (physical, cognitive, psychological impairments after critical illness) warrants structured multimodal rehabilitation and follow-up after ICU discharge. [[PMID 37525219]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether any pharmacological agent (dexmedetomidine contexts, melatonin-agonists) improves delirium-free days is unresolved; trial signals have not translated into routine recommendations.
- Optimal intensity and setting of post-ICU rehabilitation for PICS is still being defined.

### MCQ angles
- ABCDEF bundle components and the evidence for each.
- Why haloperidol is not recommended for routine delirium prophylaxis.
- Dexmedetomidine versus benzodiazepine sedation in delirium risk.
- Recognising and triaging PICS after discharge.

### Case hooks
- A ventilated septic patient is deeply sedated on day 4 with a RASS of -4; outline the bundle-based de-escalation and delirium-prevention plan.
- A 78-year-old post-ICU patient has new confusion, weakness, and low mood at home; structure the PICS follow-up.

### Gaps
- The retained abstracts do not include the primary randomised trials of individual bundle elements (e.g. SAT/SBT pairing trials); verify specific effect sizes against source trials before approval.

## 903 — ICU admission criteria and triage

topicId: `2cbe58d0-d0ed-46ae-888a-61a1dba7e1b4`

### Mentor intro
ICU triage is allocation under scarcity: who benefits most from a bed, who can be managed elsewhere, and who — despite need — will not benefit. The ConICTri consensus statement operationalises these judgments with explicit prioritisation criteria, while a qualitative systematic review shows how variable real-world selection criteria are across systems. For surgical patients, 'intensive care without borders' reframes the question as matching monitored-care intensity to risk rather than geography — using prediction scores and early-warning escalation to decide who needs the unit at all.

### Teaching points
- Structured triage frameworks (e.g. ConICTri) use explicit prioritisation criteria — diagnosis, severity, reversibility, and life expectancy — to make bed allocation auditable and consistent under scarcity. [[PMID 31456540]]
- Real-world triage and inter-facility transfer criteria vary widely across systems, with clinical judgment, bed pressure, and prognosis all influencing decisions; standardised criteria are inconsistently applied. [[PMID 32858530]]
- For surgical patients, risk prediction scores and timely recognition of postoperative deterioration can match patients to the right level of care, including high-dependency and ward-based monitoring alternatives. [[PMID 30299312]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether formal scoring systems outperform structured clinical judgment for ICU admission decisions lacks definitive trial evidence.
- Ethical frameworks for rationing during full-capacity crises (e.g. pandemic triage) remain contested across jurisdictions.

### MCQ angles
- Prioritisation criteria: benefit, reversibility, prognosis under scarcity.
- Triage decisions when the unit is full — referral, transfer, or ward care.
- Prediction scores for postoperative escalation.
- Documentation and audit of triage decisions.

### Case hooks
- Two patients compete for the last bed: a ventilated pneumonia with good reversibility and an end-stage malignancy with sepsis; apply structured triage reasoning.
- A postoperative patient scores high-risk but the ICU is full; design the ward-based monitoring escalation plan.

### Gaps
- The retained abstracts do not include SOFA-based or frailty-based ICU-admission prediction validation studies; verify score performance data before approval.

### Sources

## 904 — ICU delirium: CAM-ICU assessment, prevention, and management

topicId: `4395e32e-ba65-4c03-a927-1b407dfbb1da`

### Mentor intro
Where topic 902 covers the prevention and management architecture of ICU delirium, this topic is about the instrument itself: the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU. CAM-ICU remains the most widely validated bedside tool for non-psychiatrists — an acute change in mental status with inattention, plus either altered consciousness or disorganised thinking. Psychometric synthesis confirms strong accuracy in general critically ill adults, but the tool only works when it is actually applied: most delirium in the ICU goes unrecognised without systematic screening, which is why guidelines embed CAM-ICU into hourly-to-daily assessment pathways.

### Teaching points
- CAM-ICU is a valid, reliable instrument for delirium diagnosis in critically ill adults, including ventilated patients unable to speak, when administered by trained non-psychiatrists. [[PMID 37905383]] [[PMID 29437077]]
- The tool's logic is an acute change or fluctuation in mental status plus inattention, disorganised thinking, or altered level of consciousness — features that distinguish delirium from dementia and depression. [[PMID 29437077]]
- Without systematic screening, the majority of ICU delirium goes undetected; routine instrument-based assessment is the prerequisite for any prevention or treatment bundle. [[PMID 37905383]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal screening frequency (every shift versus daily) and who should perform screening (nurses versus physicians) is institution-dependent.
- Performance of CAM-ICU in deeply sedated or severely encephalopathic patients is less certain; combined instruments (e.g. with EEG correlates) are under study.

### MCQ angles
- CAM-ICU feature algorithm: acute change, inattention, disorganised thinking, altered consciousness.
- CAM-ICU versus ICDSC: strengths and feasibility at the bedside.
- Sensitivity/specificity of instruments in ventilated patients.
- Screening frequency and integration into sedation interruptions.

### Case hooks
- A ventilated patient fails an attention screening with RASS +1; walk through the CAM-ICU algorithm and state the diagnosis and next steps.
- A unit reports delirium rates near zero without routine screening; critique the data and design the screening rollout.

### Gaps
- WEAK EVIDENCE BASE: only two sources, both assessment-focused. The retained abstracts do not cover CAM-ICU performance in specific subgroups (neuro-ICU, ECMO); verify before approval.

### Sources
- PMID 37905383 — Validity and predictability of the confusion assessment method for the intensive care unit for delirium among critically ill patients.
- PMID 29437077 — Delirium Assessment Tools for Use in Critically Ill Adults: A Psychometric Analysis and Systematic Review.

## 905 — ICU renal replacement therapy initiation timing

topicId: `b08ec429-688b-41b2-a881-1548d38b367a`

### Mentor intro
When to start renal replacement therapy in ICU acute kidney injury has been one of nephrology's most contested questions, and the contemporary answer is restraint: large randomised trials and the Cochrane synthesis found no survival advantage to early, protocol-driven initiation over a watchful strategy that waits for absolute indications — refractory hyperkalaemia, severe acidosis, pulmonary oedema, or uraemic complications. Starting RRT is therefore a decision about concrete complications, not creatinine alone. Once started, dosing, anticoagulation, and membrane choice follow the prescription-pathway and core-curriculum frameworks that standardise delivery across ICUs.

### Teaching points
- Randomised evidence and systematic review show no mortality benefit from early versus delayed RRT initiation in AKI; a strategy awaiting absolute indications is supported. [[PMID 30560582]]
- Traditional absolute indications remain the trigger points: refractory hyperkalaemia, severe metabolic acidosis, diuretic-resistant volume overload, and uraemic complications (encephalopathy, pericarditis). [[PMID 30560582]] [[PMID 32144519]]
- Once CKRT is started, prescription quality — effluent dose, anticoagulation strategy, circuit monitoring, and drug dosing adjustment — determines delivered dose and outcomes. [[PMID 32144519]] [[PMID 40072400]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether specific phenotypes (septic AKI with fluid overload, severe hypercatabolism) benefit from earlier initiation is still being tested.
- Biomarker-guided (e.g. furosemide stress test, NGAL) initiation timing has not yet changed standard practice.

### MCQ angles
- AKIKI/IDEAL-ICU-style trial results: early versus delayed RRT and mortality.
- Absolute versus relative indications for RRT in AKI.
- CRRT prescription essentials: dose, anticoagulation, filter life.
- Drug dosing on CRRT and common errors.

### Case hooks
- An oliguric septic patient has K+ 6.2 mmol/L, pH 7.18, and rising creatinine on day 2; decide on RRT timing and modality with justification.
- A CKRT circuit keeps clotting within 12 hours; review the prescription pathway (dose, anticoagulation, blood flow).

### Gaps
- The retained abstracts do not include the primary AKIKI/IDEAL-ICU trial reports; verify exact eligibility criteria and secondary-endpoint results before approval.

### Sources

- PMID 32144519 — Prescription of CRRT: a pathway to optimize therapy.
- PMID 40072400 — Continuous Kidney Replacement Therapies: Core Curriculum 2025.

## 906 — Idiopathic facial nerve palsy (Bell's palsy): corticosteroid timing, eye care

topicId: `aa1f89a0-497b-4e55-abe6-e52ab9443f1f`

### Mentor intro
Bell's palsy is the commonest cranial neuropathy, and its management hinges on two time-sensitive acts: starting corticosteroids early — ideally within 72 hours of onset, when the facial nerve is still inflamed within the fallopian canal — and protecting the eye, whose blink reflex is paralysed. Meta-analytic evidence supports adding antivirals to steroids for the best recovery rates in severe palsy, and guidelines (Japanese 2023 update among them) converge on early steroid therapy with graded add-on antivirals. The prognosis is favourable in most, but complete palsy, older age, and absent recovery by three months warrant referral for specialist assessment of synkinesis and surgical options.

### Teaching points
- Corticosteroids started within 72 hours of onset improve facial-nerve recovery and are the cornerstone of treatment for adults with Bell's palsy. [[PMID 37054419]] [[PMID 39079445]]
- Meta-analytic evidence indicates combination antiviral-plus-steroid therapy outperforms steroid alone in recovery, with greatest benefit in severe palsy — consistent with a likely viral (HSV) aetiology. [[PMID 41019239]] [[PMID 25857657]]
- Eye protection is mandatory from day one: lubricating drops, ointment, and taping or patching at night to prevent corneal exposure and ulceration. [[PMID 37054419]] [[PMID 25857657]]
- Red-flag exclusions before treating: vesicles suggest Ramsay Hunt (needs antivirals urgently), and no forehead sparing — but any atypical features (bilateral, recurrent, slow progression) demand imaging for alternative causes. [[PMID 25857657]]

### Uncertainties
- The incremental benefit of antivirals in mild-to-moderate palsy is modest and debated across guidelines.
- Role of electrophysiology (ENoG) and surgical decompression in complete early paralysis is unresolved and specialist-dependent.

### MCQ angles
- The 72-hour steroid window and dosing.
- When to add antivirals — severity and evidence.
- Corneal protection regimens and red-eye warning signs.
- Distinguishing Bell's palsy from Ramsay Hunt syndrome and central facial weakness.

### Case hooks
- A 45-year-old presents 36 hours into a complete right facial palsy; prescribe management including eye care and counselling on prognosis.
- A patient with 'Bell's palsy' has slow progression over 6 weeks; list the features that should have prompted earlier imaging.

### Gaps
- The retained abstracts do not include the primary trials (e.g. Scandinavian steroid trial) behind the steroid recommendation; verify effect sizes against primary sources before approval.

### Sources

- PMID 41019239 — Efficacy of Combination and Single Therapy in Bell's Palsy: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.
- PMID 39079445 — Summary of Japanese clinical practice guidelines for Bell's palsy - 2023 update.

## 907 — Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension (IIH): lumbar puncture opening pressure, acetazolamide, stenting

topicId: `d47bef89-e4eb-45d3-bc79-08bb3b5e2d72`

### Mentor intro
IIH is a syndrome of raised intracranial pressure in obese women of childbearing age, diagnosed by the modified Dandy criteria: symptoms (headache, pulsatile tinnitus, visual disturbance), elevated lumbar-puncture opening pressure with normal CSF composition, and normal neuroimaging — with venous imaging to exclude cerebral venous sinus thrombosis. Management is tiered: weight loss is disease-modifying, acetazolamide is first-line pharmacotherapy with modest but real benefit on vision, and escalating interventions — CSF shunting, venous sinus stenting, optic nerve sheath fenestration — are reserved for rapidly declining vision, with stenting increasingly used where venous sinus stenosis with a pressure gradient is demonstrated.

### Teaching points
- Diagnosis requires elevated LP opening pressure (typically ≥25 cm CSF in adults, lateral decubitus) with normal CSF composition and neuroimaging excluding secondary causes, especially venous sinus thrombosis. [[PMID 34929642]] [[PMID 26700907]]
- Weight reduction is the only disease-modifying intervention; even modest sustained loss lowers intracranial pressure and can induce remission. [[PMID 40459312]] [[PMID 26700907]]
- Acetazolamide is first-line medical therapy; trial evidence shows modest benefit on visual field outcomes when added to diet, and it is titrated against symptom control and tolerability. [[PMID 40459312]] [[PMID 34929642]]
- For medically refractory or rapidly declining vision, surgical options include CSF diversion (shunt), optic nerve sheath fenestration, and venous sinus stenting — the latter for patients with documented sinus stenosis and a trans-stenosis pressure gradient. [[PMID 34929642]] [[PMID 40459312]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal acetazolamide dosing and duration, and whether it alters long-term vision loss independently of weight change, remains uncertain.
- Patient selection criteria for venous sinus stenting (pressure-gradient thresholds, stenosis morphology) are not standardised across centres.

### MCQ angles
- Modified Dandy criteria and correct LP technique for opening pressure.
- Weight loss as disease modification — magnitude and mechanisms.
- Acetazolamide: evidence, titration, side-effect management.
- Escalation ladder: when to shunt, fenestrate, or stent.

### Case hooks
- An obese 29-year-old woman with daily headache and pulsatile tinnitus has papilloedema; outline the diagnostic pathway through LP and imaging.
- A patient on maximal acetazolamide has worsening visual fields; argue the case for venous sinus stenting versus shunt.

### Gaps
- The retained abstracts do not include the IIHTT primary trial report or stenting case-series outcomes; verify visual-field effect sizes and complication rates before approval.

### Sources
- PMID 34929642 — Idiopathic intracranial hypertension: Pathophysiology, diagnosis and management.
- PMID 26700907 — Understanding idiopathic intracranial hypertension: mechanisms, management, and future directions.
- PMID 40459312 — Treatment and Monitoring of Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension.

### Evidence log — dropped

## 908 — Idiopathic intracranial hypertension vision protection

topicId: `51d40989-0d17-4506-ae23-b4414e418eae`

### Mentor intro
In IIH, vision is the organ at stake: papilloedema reflects axonal compromise at the optic nerve head, and the clinical mandate is early, quantitative monitoring of visual function so that decline triggers escalation before permanent field loss. Automated perimetry (Humphrey 24-2 or 30-2) and optic-disc photography/OCT form the monitoring backbone, interpreted alongside symptoms — transient visual obscurations are a warning sign. The nuances that distinguish expert practice include recognising atypical presentations (men, lean patients, fulminant IIH with rapid field loss), the management of IIH in pregnancy, and the discipline of urgent surgical referral when fields deteriorate despite therapy.

### Teaching points
- Visual field testing by automated perimetry is the primary outcome measure in IIH; regular, comparable field testing detects deterioration earlier than symptoms or fundoscopy alone. [[PMID 38575259]] [[PMID 35103000]]
- Papilloedema grading and OCT of the retinal nerve fibre layer complement perimetry; discordance between disc appearance and field loss is common and the field takes precedence. [[PMID 38575259]]
- Fulminant IIH — rapid, severe visual decline over days to weeks — is a neuro-ophthalmic emergency requiring urgent surgical intervention rather than medical titration. [[PMID 35103000]] [[PMID 38575259]]
- Atypical phenotypes (male, non-obese, paediatric, or secondary pseudotumour cerebri) require scrupulous exclusion of secondary causes while applying the same vision-protection framework. [[PMID 35103000]] [[PMID 38575259]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal monitoring intervals (monthly versus three-monthly fields in stable patients) are practice-defined rather than trial-defined.
- Whether optical coherence tomography can replace or triage formal perimetry in resource-limited settings is under evaluation.

### MCQ angles
- Choosing and interpreting perimetry in IIH.
- Transient visual obscurations versus field loss — significance.
- Fulminant IIH: definition and emergency escalation.
- Monitoring IIH in pregnancy and atypical phenotypes.

### Case hooks

- A patient presents with severe papilloedema and a collapsing visual field over one week; outline the emergency pathway.

### Gaps
- THIN EVIDENCE BASE: only two sources, both reviews. The retained abstracts do not include perimetry-reliability studies or surgical-vision-outcome series; verify escalation thresholds against neuro-ophthalmology guidance before approval.

### Sources

## 909 — idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

topicId: `57ae565d-bcd3-4514-b24b-41475daf5cd1`

### Mentor intro
IPF is the prototypic progressive fibrosing interstitial lung disease: irreversible parenchymal remodelling, honeycomb change on HRCT or UIP-pattern histology, and a median survival measured in years without treatment. Two antifibrotics — pirfenidone and nintedanib — slow forced vital capacity decline, and the progressive-pulmonary-fibrosis consensus extends the antifibrotic principle beyond IPF to other fibrosing ILDs that worsen despite management. Diagnosis is multidisciplinary, prognosis is tracked by FVC trajectory, and transplant referral is timed by risk stratification — the modern skill is recognising progression early and treating the trajectory, not the label.

### Teaching points
- Diagnosis rests on HRCT UIP-pattern (or multidisciplinary confirmation with histology), exclusion of known causes, and recognition of the usual interstitial pneumonia pattern's prognostic weight. [[PMID 39264073]]
- Pirfenidone and nintedanib slow FVC decline in IPF; indirect-comparison evidence supports efficacy of antifibrotics in progressive fibrosing ILD beyond IPF. [[PMID 34895203]] [[PMID 36517177]]
- The progressive-pulmonary-fibrosis framework defines worsening across symptoms, physiology, and imaging within a year of appropriate management, triggering antifibrotic therapy regardless of specific ILD label. [[PMID 36517177]]
- Monitoring relies on serial FVC and DLCO, with exacerbations — acute worsening with new ground-glass opacity — carrying high mortality and requiring aggressive management. [[PMID 39264073]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal sequencing and combination of antifibrotics, and treatment of acute exacerbations, lack randomised evidence.
- Antifibrotic efficacy in early or asymptomatic IPF and in non-IPF progressive fibrosing ILD beyond progressive phenotypes is extrapolated rather than proven.

### MCQ angles
- UIP-pattern HRCT criteria and when biopsy/multidisciplinary review is needed.
- Antifibrotic mechanisms, monitoring, and side-effect management (photosensitivity, diarrhoea, LFTs).
- Defining progressive pulmonary fibrosis and when to treat non-IPF ILDs.
- Acute exacerbation of IPF: recognition and management.

### Case hooks
- A 68-year-old man with exertional dyspnoea and velcro crackles has probable UIP on HRCT; walk through diagnosis, antifibrotic counselling, and monitoring.
- A patient with rheumatoid-arthritis ILD declines over 8 months despite immunosuppression; apply the PPF definition and argue for antifibrotic therapy.

### Gaps
- The retained abstracts do not include the primary INPULSIS/ASCEND trial reports or the current ATS/ERS guideline; verify eligibility criteria and effect sizes against primary sources before approval.

### Sources
- PMID 39264073 — Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis: Review of Current Knowledge.

## 910 — IgA Nephropathy

topicId: `8d347e02-de98-401a-a5f7-334beefd5469`

### Mentor intro
IgA nephropathy, the commonest primary glomerulonephritis, is now managed as a progressive-risk disease: the KDIGO 2021 guideline established risk-based surveillance, and the 2025 update adds an evidence-treatment armamentarium that has expanded rapidly — supportive care with RAS blockade and blood-pressure control remains the foundation, with SGLT2 inhibitors, targeted-release budesonide (Nefecon), sparsentan, and endothelin-receptor antagonists layered by proteinuria and eGFR trajectory. The validation cohorts underpinning these additions showed proteinuria reduction as a surrogate for kidney outcomes, shifting practice from watchful waiting to early, goal-directed combination therapy.

### Teaching points
- All patients receive optimised supportive care — blood-pressure control, ACEi/ARB titrated to maximally tolerated dose — which remains the backbone regardless of risk. [[PMID 34556300]] [[PMID 40975525]]
- Risk stratification uses proteinuria (particularly the persistently ≥0.75–1 g/day threshold), eGFR trajectory, and the International IgAN Prediction Tool to time immunosuppression. [[PMID 40975525]] [[PMID 34556300]]
- The KDIGO 2025 update incorporates newer agents: SGLT2 inhibitors for residual proteinuria, targeted-release budesonide for high-risk primary IgAN, sparsentan, and endothelin-A receptor antagonists, based on proteinuria-lowering as a validated surrogate endpoint. [[PMID 40975525]]
- Systemic corticosteroids are reserved for high-risk patients after optimised supportive care, weighing infection and toxicity risk; tonsillectomy is considered in selected recurrent-synpharyngitic-haematuria populations. [[PMID 34556300]] [[PMID 40975525]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal sequencing and combination of the newer agents (SGLT2i, TR-budesonide, sparsentan) and their additive proteinuria effects are not yet defined.
- Long-term (>5-year) hard-endpoint data for the newer agents beyond proteinuria surrogates are pending.

### MCQ angles
- KDIGO 2021 versus 2025: what changed in IgAN therapy.
- Proteinuria thresholds that trigger treatment escalation.
- Nefecon (targeted-release budesonide): indication and evidence.
- When steroids are justified in IgAN and their risks.

### Case hooks
- A 34-year-old with biopsy-proven IgAN has proteinuria 1.4 g/day on maximally dosed ACEi; apply the 2025 KDIGO pathway and justify each addition.
- A patient with recurrent synpharyngitic haematuria asks about tonsillectomy; weigh the evidence and regional practice variation.

### Gaps
