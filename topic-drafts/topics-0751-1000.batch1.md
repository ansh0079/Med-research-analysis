## #751 Gram-negative bloodstream infection duration  (cluster `gram-negative bloodstream infection duration`)

**Mentor intro.** Gram-negative bloodstream infection is one of the settings where antimicrobial stewardship has most visibly shortened therapy. A decade of non-inferiority trials and meta-analyses now supports 7-day courses for many patients, while multidrug-resistant organisms remain the key evidence gap.

**Teaching points**
- A 2025 systematic review and noninferiority meta-analysis of randomized trials found 7 days of antibiotics noninferior to 14 days for gram-negative bloodstream infection in hospitalized adults. [[PMID 40116824]]
- IDSA's 2025 complicated-UTI guideline suggests 5-7 days of a fluoroquinolone or 7 days of a non-fluoroquinolone when the patient is improving on effective therapy, regardless of associated gram-negative bacteremia. [[PMID 41419448]]
- In Enterobacteriaceae bacteremia, meta-analysis showed no significant difference between short (<=10 days) and longer courses for 30- and 90-day mortality, clinical cure, or relapse. [[PMID 30803971]]
- Short courses are consistently noninferior when the diagnosis is confirmed, the antimicrobial is appropriate, and the patient shows clinical improvement. [[PMID 36075498]]
- For MDR gram-negatives (CRE, difficult-to-treat Pseudomonas, CRAB), IDSA and ESCMID give explicit agent-selection advice but lack detailed duration recommendations; evidence for short courses is limited here. [[PMID 40725151]]

**Uncertainties**
- Optimal duration for carbapenem-resistant organisms and difficult-to-treat Pseudomonas aeruginosa bacteremia.
- How source control status should modify the 7-day default.

**MCQ angles**
- Seven versus fourteen days: what the noninferiority meta-analysis supports and its boundaries.
- cUTI with gram-negative bacteremia: translating the IDSA 5-7/7-day recommendation to a clinical vignette.
- Which patients qualify for short-course therapy (confirmed diagnosis, effective agent, improving).
- The MDR-GNB duration evidence gap versus agent-selection guidance.

**Case hooks**
- E. coli bacteremia from pyelonephritis, afebrile and improving on day 3 - stop at day 7?
- Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae bacteremia in a stable patient - how long, and on what evidence?

**Gaps for the reviewer.** Pediatric durations, Pseudomonas-specific BSI data, and pathogen-disease combinations beyond Enterobacteriaceae/UTI are not covered by the retained abstracts; verify against a full BSI guideline before approval.

**Sources**
- PMID 40116824: Seven vs Fourteen Days of Antibiotics for Gram-Negative Bloodstream Infection: A Systematic Review and Noninferiority Meta-Analysis (meta-analysis, 2025)
- PMID 41419448: IDSA 2025 Guidelines on Management and Treatment of Complicated Urinary Tract Infections--Duration of Antibiotics for Complicated UTI (guideline, 2026)
- PMID 30803971: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Antibiotic Treatment Duration for Bacteremia Due to Enterobacteriaceae (meta-analysis, 2019)
- PMID 36075498: Short-course antibiotics for common infections: what do we know and where do we go from here? (review, 2023)
- PMID 40725151: Antibiotic Therapy Duration for Multidrug-Resistant Gram-Negative Bacterial Infections: An Evidence-Based Review (review, 2025)

## #752 Gram-negative resistance: ESBL, carbapenemase-producing organisms, colistin, ceftazidime-avibactam  (cluster `gram-negative resistance esbl carbapenemase colistin ceftazidime-avibactam`)

**Mentor intro.** The last decade has transformed treatment of carbapenem-resistant gram-negatives: ceftazidime-avibactam, meropenem-vaborbactam and imipenem-relebactam have moved KPC-producing Enterobacterales from the colistin era into targeted beta-lactam therapy, while KPC variant evolution now threatens the new agents themselves.

**Teaching points**
- Ceftazidime-avibactam is a first-line option for KPC-producing Enterobacterales, with meropenem-vaborbactam and imipenem-relebactam as alternatives; ceftazidime-avibactam is preferred for OXA-48-like producers. [[PMID 40185559] [PMID 34752560]]
- For KPC strains resistant to ceftazidime-avibactam, meropenem-vaborbactam and imipenem-relebactam have relative advantages. [[PMID 40185559]]
- Newer beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations expanded the armamentarium against MDR Enterobacterales and Pseudomonas aeruginosa beyond the polymyxin era. [[PMID 33177185] [PMID 34169446]]
- In-vivo emergence of ceftazidime-avibactam resistance in KPC-E is documented in a systematic review: most cases were Klebsiella pneumoniae ST258 with the D179Y substitution, occurring during treatment of deep-seated infection. [[PMID 33895414]]
- More than 150 blaKPC variants are now reported, most identified in the last three years, and variants restore ceftazidime affinity while weakening avibactam binding - a moving resistance target. [[PMID 37937997]]

**Uncertainties**
- Optimal sequencing of newer agents when first-line BLBLI therapy fails.
- Whether combination therapy (e.g. BLBLI plus aminoglycoside or polymyxin) improves outcomes in high-inoculum infections.

**MCQ angles**
- Agent selection by carbapenemase type: KPC versus OXA-48 versus MBL.
- Recognizing and responding to ceftazidime-avibactam resistance emerging on therapy (ST258/D179Y).
- Why colistin has been displaced as default therapy for KPC-producing CRE.
- The blaKPC variant explosion and what it means for susceptibility reporting.

**Case hooks**
- Blood culture grows KPC-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae on day 4 of ceftazidime-avibactam - what changed and what next?
- OXA-48-producing E. coli bacteremia: choosing between ceftazidime-avibactam and a carbapenem-sparing alternative.

**Gaps for the reviewer.** Metallo-beta-lactamase therapy (aztreonam-avibactam, cefiderocol) is not covered by the retained abstracts; add an MBL-specific source before approval. ESBL-specific guidance (when carbapenems can be spared) is also not represented.

**Sources**
- PMID 34169446: Treatment for carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales infections: recent advances and future directions (review, 2021)
- PMID 34752560: Review of Ceftazidime-Avibactam, Meropenem-Vaborbactam, and Imipenem/Cilastatin-Relebactam to Target KPC-producing Enterobacterales (review, 2020)
- PMID 40185559: Management of Severe Infections: Multidrug-Resistant and Carbapenem-Resistant Gram-Negative Bacteria (review, 2025)

## #753 Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis  (cluster `granulomatosis with polyangiitis`)

**Mentor intro.** Granulomatosis with polyangiitis is the commonest ANCA-associated vasculitis and a medical emergency when active: modern regimens pair glucocorticoids with rituximab or cyclophosphamide for induction, and the 2022 EULAR update redefined how newer trial evidence maps to treatment choices.

**Teaching points**
- EULAR's 2022 AAV recommendations were built on a systematic literature review plus expert consensus, incorporating randomized trials published since the 2016 version that change clinical care. [[PMID 36927642]]
- Rituximab is now an established induction option alongside glucocorticoids for ANCA-associated vasculitis, reflected across EULAR, KDIGO and ACR guidance. [[PMID 39196354]]
- Major clinical trials underpinning current ANCA vasculitis treatment have reduced morbidity and improved survival; guideline summaries track these trial milestones. [[PMID 41460038]]
- Japanese 2023 AAV guideline development used a dedicated systematic review and meta-analysis, an example of rigorous national adaptation of the same evidence base. [[PMID 36112482]]
- AAV is a frequently relapsing disorder; treatment aims are rapid control of active disease, then prevention of relapse and organ damage. [[PMID 41051263]]

**Uncertainties**
- Optimal glucocorticoid-sparing intensity and the role of avacopan in routine practice.
- How to sequence maintenance therapy and define relapse triggers for re-treatment.

**MCQ angles**
- Induction therapy selection: rituximab versus cyclophosphamide for new organ-threatening GPA.
- What changed between EULAR 2016 and 2022 and which trials drove it.
- Maintenance and relapse prevention after successful induction.
- Organ-specific complications (pulmonary haemorrhage, RPGN) and how they modify therapy.

**Case hooks**
- 45-year-old with haemoptysis, saddle-nose deformity and c-ANCA - induction regimen and monitoring plan?
- GPA in remission on azathioprine with rising ANCA titre - escalate, switch, or watch?

**Gaps for the reviewer.** Avacopan (ADVOCATE), plasma exchange (PEXIVAS) and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole prophylaxis specifics are not in the retained abstracts; add the primary trial/guideline sources before approval.

**Sources**
- PMID 36927642: EULAR recommendations for the management of ANCA-associated vasculitis: 2022 update (systematic review, 2024)

## #754 graves disease antithyroid and radioiodine therapy  (cluster `graves disease antithyroid radioiodine therapy`)

**Mentor intro.** Graves' hyperthyroidism offers three effective definitive strategies - antithyroid drugs, radioiodine and thyroidectomy - and no modality has proven universally superior. Modern practice starts with antithyroid drugs to restore euthyroidism, then individualises the definitive step around orbitopathy, pregnancy plans and patient preference.

**Teaching points**
- Guideline-based strategy first restores euthyroid status with antithyroid therapy, then selects a definitive modality; no treatment modality has demonstrated superiority, so the choice is shared with the patient. [[PMID 30193753]]
- Radioiodine-131 and thyroidectomy abolish hyperthyroidism but create hypothyroidism requiring lifelong levothyroxine - a trade of one disease for another rather than a cure. [[PMID 30912336]]
- Radioiodine can aggravate Graves' orbitopathy and requires pre-treatment assessment; pregnancy or pregnancy plans change both treatment choice and monitoring. [[PMID 30193753]]
- A beta-blocker relieves tachycardia, anxiety and tremor while awaiting definitive control. [[PMID 30730622]]
- Long-term antithyroid drug courses are an increasingly used strategy, with contemporary narrative reviews updating their role against radioiodine and surgery. [[PMID 42119974]]

**Uncertainties**
- Which patients achieve durable remission after antithyroid drugs and how to predict relapse.
- Optimal role and duration of long-term low-dose antithyroid drug maintenance.

**MCQ angles**
- First step in a newly diagnosed thyrotoxic patient: restore euthyroidism before definitive therapy.
- How orbitopathy and pregnancy plans shift the modality choice.
- Radioiodine versus antithyroid drugs: what 'cure' actually means.
- Symptom control with beta-blockers while definitive therapy takes effect.

**Case hooks**
- 28-year-old woman with Graves' disease planning pregnancy - which therapy, and what monitoring?
- Active smokers with moderate orbitopathy - why radioiodine needs a second look.

**Gaps for the reviewer.** Antithyroid drug adverse effects (agranulocytosis, hepatotoxicity) and their monitoring are not detailed in the retained abstracts; add an ATA/EUGOGO-aligned source before approval.

**Sources**
- PMID 30193753: Treatment of adult Graves' disease (guideline, 2018)
- PMID 30730622: Graves' disease in adults: antithyroid drugs, radioactive iodine, or sometimes thyroidectomy (review, 2017)
- PMID 42119974: Long-Term Antithyroid Drug Therapy in the Management of Graves' Hyperthyroidism: An Updated Narrative Review (review, 2026)
- PMID 30912336: Graves' Disease: Can It Be Cured? (review, 2019)

## #755 Graves disease: antithyroid drugs, radioiodine, thyroidectomy - comparative outcomes  (cluster `graves disease comparative outcomes`)

**Mentor intro.** Comparing antithyroid drugs, radioiodine and thyroidectomy for Graves' disease is less about finding a winner than matching trade-offs: remission potential versus definitive control, and the burden of lifelong thyroid hormone replacement after destructive approaches. Guidelines consistently report no modality as universally superior.

**Teaching points**
- Treatment options for Graves' hyperthyroidism have not changed in decades - antithyroid drugs, radioactive iodine and thyroidectomy - and reviews of disease burden confirm all three remain the active comparators. [[PMID 42683117]]
- No randomized comparison has established superiority of one modality; guideline strategy therefore emphasises informed, preference-sensitive choice after euthyroidism is restored. [[PMID 30193753] [PMID 30730622]]
- Radioiodine and thyroidectomy reliably abolish hyperthyroidism but commit the patient to lifelong levothyroxine; antithyroid drugs preserve thyroid function but carry relapse risk. [[PMID 30912336]]
- Antithyroid drug treatment duration relates to remission rates, though the meta-analytic evidence to date is in children - extrapolation to adults needs caution. [[PMID 39501471]]
- Long-term antithyroid drug therapy is an increasingly recognised strategy whose updated narrative review maps its place against radioiodine and thyroidectomy. [[PMID 42119974]]

**Uncertainties**
- Adult remission rates by antithyroid drug duration and predictive factors (TRAb, goitre size).
- Long-term quality-of-life and cardiovascular outcome differences between modalities.

**MCQ angles**
- A patient-centred modality choice: orbitopathy, pregnancy plans and smoking status as decision modifiers.
- What 'definitive therapy' commits the patient to: lifelong levothyroxine after RAI/surgery.
- Relapse after antithyroid drug withdrawal: who, when, and predicted by what.
- Evidence limits: why guidelines decline to crown a winner.

**Case hooks**
- 35-year-old man with recurrent Graves' relapse after 18 months of carbimazole - radioiodine, surgery, or prolonged ATD?
- Patient asks which treatment is 'best' - structuring a preference-sensitive consultation.

**Gaps for the reviewer.** Adult comparative-effectiveness data (remission rates, relapse predictors) are thin in the retained abstracts - the pediatric duration meta-analysis is the only quantified source; adult RCT/Registry data needed before approval.

**Sources**

- PMID 30193753: Treatment of adult Graves' disease (guideline, 2018)
- PMID 30730622: Graves' disease in adults: antithyroid drugs, radioactive iodine, or sometimes thyroidectomy (review, 2017)

## #756 Group 1 pulmonary arterial hypertension: ERA, PDE5i, prostacyclin, combination therapy  (cluster `group 1 pulmonary arterial hypertension era pde5i prostacyclin combination`)

**Mentor intro.** Group 1 pulmonary arterial hypertension is managed by three pathways - endothelin receptor antagonists, phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors and prostacyclin analogues - but the defining shift of recent years is treatment strategy: risk-stratified, combination-first, then escalation.

**Teaching points**
- The 2022 ESC/ERS guidelines introduced a refined three-category risk stratification (low, intermediate, high) that guides both initial and subsequent therapy; estimated 1-year mortality exceeds 20% in high-risk patients. [[PMID 37085727]]
- Initial combination therapy for PAH was first proposed for high-risk patients in the 2009 European guidelines, extended to low/intermediate-risk patients by 2015 and later consensus meetings - a concept now central to practice. [[PMID 33720578]]
- Modern progress in PAH therapy comes not from new pathways but from combination strategies across the endothelin, nitric-oxide and prostacyclin pathways plus systematic escalation based on risk. [[PMID 30545971]]

**Uncertainties**
- Which initial doublet (ERA+PDE5i versus including a prostacyclin pathway agent) best serves intermediate-risk patients.
- How to time escalation and when to refer for lung transplantation assessment.

**MCQ angles**
- Risk-stratified treatment selection: which patient gets initial combination therapy.
- The three drug pathways and their representative agents.
- Treatment goals: moving a patient from intermediate to low risk as the therapeutic target.
- Recognising refractory disease and the transplant window.

**Case hooks**
- Newly diagnosed idiopathic PAH, intermediate risk on refined scoring - initial regimen?
- Patient deteriorating on ERA+PDE5i despite 6 months of therapy - escalation options.

**Gaps for the reviewer.** Named pivotal trials (AMBITION, GRIPHON, SERAPHIN) and prostacyclin route practicalities are not in the retained abstracts; add ESC/ERS 2022 guideline summary and AMBITION primary paper before approval.

**Sources**

## #757 Gut Microbiome in GI Disease  (cluster `gut microbiome gi disease`)

**Mentor intro.** The gut microbiome has moved from speculation to mechanism in GI disease: dysbiosis is now associated with IBS, IBD and colorectal cancer, diet and food additives are established modulators, and prebiotic/probiotic strategies are being refined - though causality and strain specificity remain the frontier.

**Teaching points**
- Dysbiosis - imbalance between beneficial and opportunistic microbiota - is associated with IBS, IBD (ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease) and colorectal cancer. [[PMID 36157114]]
- Dietary fibre from unrefined whole foods benefits gastrointestinal health, with regulatory effects on transit time, stool formation and the gut milieu. [[PMID 33208922]]
- Diets rich in ultra-processed foods are associated with gut disease including IBD, colorectal cancer and IBS; some additives (e.g. certain emulsifiers) directly affect gut health. [[PMID 38388570]]
- Prebiotics and probiotics beneficially modulate the gut microbiome, but formulating specific recommendations for GI conditions remains challenging. [[PMID 38542689]]
- Acute enteric infection is the best-described risk factor for IBS, linking microbiome disturbance to post-infectious functional disease. [[PMID 33049223]]

**Uncertainties**
- Causality: whether dysbiosis drives disease or mirrors it.
- Strain-specific probiotic efficacy and which patients benefit from which preparations.

**MCQ angles**
- Mechanism questions: dysbiosis across IBS, IBD and colorectal cancer.
- Diet as therapy: fibre, ultra-processed foods and additives.
- What probiotics can and cannot claim in GI conditions.
- Post-infectious IBS as a microbiome-mediated entity.

**Case hooks**
- IBS patient asking about probiotics and diet - what does the evidence support?
- Crohn's patient in remission asking whether diet can modify relapse risk.

**Gaps for the reviewer.** FMT (faecal microbiota transplantation) indications and outcomes are not covered by the retained abstracts; add an FMT-specific source before approval.

## #758 Gynaecomastia: causes, drug-induced, physiological, investigation, management  (cluster `gynaecomastia causes drug-induced physiological investigation management`)

**Mentor intro.** Gynaecomastia - benign proliferation of male breast tissue - is common at the physiological extremes of age and, when acquired, a signal to review drugs, hypogonadism and systemic disease. Most adolescent cases need reassurance alone; the clinical task is separating the benign from the pathological.

**Teaching points**
- Gynaecomastia reflects an altered androgen-to-estrogen ratio with contributions from growth hormone, IGF-1, prolactin and aromatase activity. [[PMID 37592101]]
- Common causes are pubertal change, obesity, drugs and hypogonadism; liver and renal disease are among the systemic associations. [[PMID 37592101]]
- Physiological gynaecomastia occurs in almost 25% of cases - newborns, adolescents and older men - and is benign and self-limited. [[PMID 27145756]]
- In adolescents, most cases are idiopathic; if history and examination are reassuring, no further evaluation is required. [[PMID 28613047]]

**Uncertainties**
- Which drug-induced cases regress after withdrawal and over what timeframe.
- When imaging or tissue sampling is warranted in persistent unilateral gynaecomastia.

**MCQ angles**
- Sorting the cause: physiological, drug-induced, hypogonadal and systemic disease.
- The reassuring adolescent: criteria for no further work-up.
- Drugs that induce gynaecomastia and the mechanism (androgen-estrogen balance).
- When 'lump in a man' stops being gynaecomastia and needs imaging.

**Case hooks**
- 16-year-old with bilateral breast tenderness and reassuring examination - investigate or reassure?
- 64-year-old on spironolactone and amiodarone with new breast tenderness - next step?

**Gaps for the reviewer.** Definitive management (tamoxifen/raloxifene, surgery, radiotherapy) and the investigation pathway (hormones, imaging, when to biopsy) are not covered by the retained abstracts; add an endocrine-society-aligned source before approval.

**Sources**

- PMID 28613047: Adolescent Gynecomastia (review, 2017)

## #759 haematological malignancy  (cluster `haematological malignancy`)

**Mentor intro.** Modern haematological malignancy care is defined as much by its complications as by the diseases: CAR T-cell therapy and allogeneic transplantation have transformed outcomes while introducing distinct infection, toxicity and graft-versus-host disease challenges that cross disease boundaries.

**Teaching points**
- Infectious complications after CAR T-cell therapy are a significant driver of morbidity and non-relapse mortality; ASTCT best-practice guidance addresses prevention in this rapidly advancing field. [[PMID 39084261]]
- GVHD remains a major contributor to mortality and morbidity after allogeneic stem-cell transplantation; EBMT guidance standardises prophylaxis and management approaches. [[PMID 32004485]]
- Haematological and HSCT patients face life-threatening toxoplasmosis, mainly reactivation of latent cysts in seropositive patients - lack of TMP-SMX prophylaxis is a key risk factor. [[PMID 38134949]]
- CAR T-cell toxicities, including cytokine release syndrome and unexpected organ damage reported in early cases, require structured recognition and management protocols. [[PMID 27207799]]

**Uncertainties**
- Optimal infection-prophylaxis duration and agent selection after CAR T across risk strata.
- How best to prevent and treat GVHD when evidence from large trials remains limited.

**MCQ angles**
- Cross-cutting complications: infection prophylaxis after cellular therapy.
- GVHD prevention strategies after allogeneic HSCT.
- Opportunistic infection recognition (toxoplasmosis) in the immunocompromised haematology patient.
- CAR T toxicities: recognition sequence and first-line management.

**Case hooks**
- Day 10 after CAR T for diffuse large B-cell lymphoma with fever and hypotension - CRS or infection?
- Seropositive allogeneic transplant recipient not on TMP-SMX with encephalitis - what's the missed diagnosis?

**Gaps for the reviewer.** Disease-specific management (lymphoma, leukaemia, myeloma) is out of scope for the retained abstracts given the umbrella topic; either narrow the topic or add disease-specific guideline sources before approval.

## #760 Haemodialysis Complications  (cluster `haemodialysis complications`)

**Mentor intro.** Haemodialysis complications cluster around vascular access - the patient's lifeline and its commonest failure point - alongside cardiovascular instability and the broader burden of kidney failure. Access planning is the most evidence-rich and most examinable corner of this topic.

**Teaching points**
- The KDOQI 2019 vascular access update is the comprehensive evidence-based reference for haemodialysis access, replacing the 2006 version. [[PMID 32778223]]
- Arteriovenous access (autogenous fistula or graft) requires an individualised strategy - the 'Patient Life-Plan' - that anticipates future access needs rather than reacting to failure. [[PMID 38497953]]
- KDIGO's 2024 CKD guideline updates the broader evaluation and management frame in which dialysis complications sit. [[PMID 38519239]]

**Uncertainties**
- Optimal timing of access creation versus progression to dialysis dependence.
- How to reduce the fistula-first default for patients where grafts or catheters serve better.

**MCQ angles**
- Access hierarchy: fistula versus graft versus catheter and their complication profiles.
- The failing access: surveillance, stenosis and when to intervene.
- Catheter-related bloodstream infection prevention in haemodialysis units.
- Intradialytic hypotension: mechanism and management.

**Case hooks**
- AV fistula with falling flows and rising venous pressures - next investigation?
- Tunnelled catheter patient with recurrent bacteraemia - how should access be re-planned?

**Gaps for the reviewer.** The retained abstracts do not cover intradialytic hypotension, dialysis amyloidosis, electrolyte/fluid shifts or long-term cardiovascular outcomes; add an HD-complications-specific review before approval.

**Sources**
