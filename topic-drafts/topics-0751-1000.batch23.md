## 971 — Intravenous inotropes: dobutamine, milrinone, levosimendan in acute heart failure

topicId: `3ea3f705-b554-4b57-9f71-917d61dd6f4f`

### Mentor intro
In acute decompensated heart failure with a low-output state — hypoperfusion, rising lactate, cool extremities despite adequate filling pressures — intravenous inotropes are the temporary scaffold while the cause is treated. Dobutamine, the beta-agonist workhorse, increases contractility and output at the price of tachyarrhythmia and myocardial oxygen demand; milrinone, a phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitor, adds vasodilation and lusitropy but accumulates in renal dysfunction and causes hypotension; levosimendan, the calcium-sensitising agent with active metabolites, offers catecholamine-independent inotropy suited to the beta-blocked patient. None improve hard outcomes — they are a bridge to recovery, device, or decision.

### Teaching points
- Inotropes in acute heart failure are indicated for the hypoperfused low-output state (end-organ dysfunction with low cardiac output), not for routine decongestion; they are a bridge to definitive therapy. [[PMID 26690127]]
- Dobutamine provides inotropy and mild vasodilation through beta-agonism, with tachyarrhythmia and increased oxygen demand as principal limitations. [[PMID 26690127]]
- Milrinone offers inotropy plus vasodilation and is useful in pulmonary hypertension contexts, but causes hypotension and requires renal dose adjustment; levosimendan provides calcium-sensitising, catecholamine-independent inotropy with utility in beta-blocked patients. [[PMID 26690127]]
- No inotrope has improved survival in acute heart failure; therapy is temporising, with arrhythmia, ischaemia, and hypotension as the costs that must be monitored. [[PMID 26690127]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether levosimendan improves outcomes over dobutamine in specific phenotypes (beta-blocked, septic-cardiomyopathy) is unproven.
- Optimal weaning sequences and inotrope-free intervals are protocolised rather than evidence-defined.

### MCQ angles
- Low-output-state recognition and inotrope indications.
- Agent selection by phenotype and renal function.
- The no-mortality-benefit principle.
- Monitoring: arrhythmia, ischaemia, hypotension.

### Case hooks
- A decompensated-HF patient is cold, confused, and lactate-rising on optimal diuretics; decide on inotrope initiation and agent selection.
- A beta-blocked low-output patient needs inotropy; argue for levosimendan over escalating dobutamine.

### Gaps
- THIN EVIDENCE BASE: only one source. The retained abstract lacks the comparative-trial literature (LIDO, SURVIVE, LEVO-CTS); verify agent-comparison data before approval. Cross-reference topic 947 for the shock-specific inotrope evidence.

## 972 — intrinsic acute kidney injury

topicId: `929507d7-090b-41d9-bef5-191088b89e01`

### Mentor intro
Intrinsic acute kidney injury is parenchymal failure: the kidney itself is injured, whether by ischaemia-reperfusion (the ischaemic ATN of shock and sepsis), nephrotoxins (aminoglycosides, contrast, cisplatin), pigment (rhabdomyolysis, haemolysis), or interstitial inflammation (drug AIN). It is distinguished from pre-renal azotaemia by its failure to respond to volume and by urinary sediment — muddy-brown casts the classic signature of ischaemic ATN. Management is largely supportive: haemodynamic optimisation without fluid overload, nephrotoxin withdrawal, dose adjustment, and the recognition that recovery, when it comes, takes weeks.

### Teaching points
- AKI is defined by rising creatinine or falling urine output over hours to days; categorising the cause as pre-renal, intrinsic, or post-renal structures the diagnostic work-up. [[PMID 33121629]]
- Intrinsic AKI subtypes include ischaemic/toxic acute tubular necrosis, pigment nephropathy, and interstitial nephritis; the urinary sediment (muddy-brown granular casts in ATN) and failure to respond to volume help distinguish intrinsic from pre-renal disease. [[PMID 33121629]] [[PMID 26804946]]
- Management is supportive: treat the cause, withdraw nephrotoxins, adjust drug doses, optimise haemodynamics while avoiding fluid overload, and institute RRT only for absolute indications. [[PMID 26804946]] [[PMID 33121629]]
- Recovery from intrinsic AKI is slow — tubular regeneration takes weeks — and incomplete recovery leaves CKD, the rationale for nephrotoxin vigilance and follow-up. [[PMID 26804946]]

### Uncertainties
- No pharmacological therapy (dopamine, fenoldopam, mannitol) has improved outcomes in intrinsic AKI.
- Biomarker-guided early differentiation of pre-renal from intrinsic AKI is promising but not practice-changing.

### MCQ angles
- Pre-renal, intrinsic, post-renal categorisation.
- Sediment and diagnostic clues by subtype.
- Supportive management and nephrotoxin withdrawal.
- Recovery timeline and CKD transition.

### Case hooks
- A septic patient has AKI unresponsive to 48 hours of fluid optimisation with muddy-brown casts; classify and manage.
- A crush-injury patient has dark urine and rising creatinine; outline the pigment-nephropathy pathway and prevention.

### Gaps
- The retained evidence is AKI-general; verify intrinsic-subtype specifics (pigment, AIN, contrast) against dedicated reviews — cross-reference topics 918 and 961 for interstitial causes.

## 973 — invasive aspergillosis antifungal therapy

topicId: `5c1b08a0-d2cb-4cc7-bb95-6a7215143a94`

### Mentor intro
Antifungal therapy for invasive aspergillosis is anchored by the ESCMID-ECMM-ERS guideline: voriconazole is first-line for most invasive disease, with isavuconazole an equal-alternative and liposomal amphotericin B the alternative when azoles fail or are contraindicated. The pharmacology makes or breaks outcomes: voriconazole's nonlinear kinetics, CYP2C19 genetic variability, and narrow therapeutic window demand therapeutic drug monitoring; posaconazole and itraconazole absorption is erratic without formulations optimised. Resistance surveillance — azole-resistant A. fumigatus — is now a first-order concern shaping empiric choices and requiring susceptibility data.

### Teaching points
- Voriconazole is first-line therapy for invasive aspergillosis per ESCMID-ECMM-ERS guidance, with isavuconazole an equal alternative and liposomal amphotericin B reserved for intolerance, failure, or resistance. [[PMID 29544767]] [[PMID 34016284]]
- Therapeutic drug monitoring is essential for voriconazole (nonlinear kinetics, CYP2C19 variability, neurotoxicity at high levels) and recommended for posaconazole/itraconazole; trough-guided dosing improves exposure-target attainment. [[PMID 28702763]] [[PMID 29544767]]
- Combination antifungal therapy (voriconazole plus an echinocandin) is considered in selected high-risk or refractory cases but lacks definitive outcome superiority. [[PMID 29544767]] [[PMID 34016284]]
- Azole resistance in A. fumigatus is an escalating threat; susceptibility testing and local-resistance patterns should inform empiric and salvage therapy. [[PMID 34016284]] [[PMID 29544767]]

### Uncertainties
- The optimal role and timing of combination antifungal therapy remains unresolved.
- Whether newer agents (rezafungin, olorofim) displace current first-line therapy awaits comparative outcome data.

### MCQ angles

- TDM indications and target troughs.
- Combination-therapy positioning.
- Azole resistance and susceptibility-guided therapy.

### Case hooks

### Gaps

## 974 — invasive candidiasis and candidemia antifungal therapy

topicId: `5cb3c77b-8add-4bcb-88f4-de8ab7072a23`

### Mentor intro
Candidemia is the commonest invasive fungal disease in hospitals, and its management has two non-negotiables: an echinocandin is first-line empiric therapy (fluconazole reserved for stable, susceptible isolates), and central-line removal plus fundoscopic examination are part of the treatment, not adjuncts to it. The IDSA 2016 update codifies therapy by syndrome — candidemia, candidaemia with neutropenia, and organ-specific disease each have distinct durations and agents. The challenges review frames the modern threats: non-albicans species with reduced azole susceptibility, echninocandin resistance emerging in C. glabrata, and the difficulty of diagnosing deep-seated foci without candidemia.

### Teaching points
- An echinocandin (caspofungin, micafungin, anidulafungin) is recommended first-line empiric therapy for candidemia; fluconazole is an option for stable patients with susceptible isolates, and step-down to oral fluconazole follows susceptibility. [[PMID 26679628]] [[PMID 26739610]]
- Source control is integral: central-venous-catheter removal where feasible, and ophthalmological examination to exclude endophthalmitis, are parts of standard candidemia management. [[PMID 26679628]] [[PMID 37220664]]
- Non-albicans species (C. glabrata, C. parapsilosis, C. tropicalis) now dominate many series with reduced azole susceptibility, driving species-level therapy selection and susceptibility testing. [[PMID 37220664]] [[PMID 26739610]]
- Treatment duration is typically 14 days from first negative blood culture for candidemia without metastatic foci; longer with endophthalmitis, osteoarticular, or other deep-seated disease. [[PMID 26679628]]

### Uncertainties
- The optimal role of rapid species-identification and resistance-marker testing in directing early therapy is evolving.
- Management of persistent candidemia despite therapy (source exclusion, resistance, immune reconstitution) is individualised.

### MCQ angles
- First-line agent selection and step-down rules.
- Source control: line removal and fundoscopy.
- Species-level susceptibility patterns.
- Duration by syndrome.

### Case hooks
- A candidaemic ICU patient has a central line and new visual floaters; sequence therapy, line removal, and ophthalmology.
- A stable patient grows fluconazole-susceptible C. albicans from blood; justify step-down to oral therapy and duration.

### Gaps

## 975 — Invasive fungal infection in immunocompromised: aspergillosis, candida, galactomannan, voriconazole

topicId: `9f7052a1-8c48-44c9-afc9-b4010138d564`

### Mentor intro
In the immunocompromised host, invasive fungal infection is a race between diagnostic certainty and disease progression — and the modern strategy treats early. Serum and bronchoalveolar galactomannan, beta-D-glucan, and high-resolution CT's halo sign provide pre-culture evidence; the host's net state of immunosuppression (neutropenia duration, corticosteroid dose, transplant type) sets the pre-test probability. The ICU adds a diagnostic wrinkle: invasive aspergillosis now occurs in non-neutropenic patients — COPD on steroids, cirrhosis, post-influenza — where the classic frameworks fit poorly. Therapy is empiric-to-preemptive, anchored on voriconazole with the echinocandins and the newer agents filling defined niches.

### Teaching points
- Host-immunosuppression assessment (neutropenia, corticosteroid dose, transplant, haematologic malignancy) drives fungal-risk stratification and the threshold for empiric/preemptive therapy. [[PMID 26165502]] [[PMID 28355466]]
- Non-culture diagnostics — galactomannan (serum and BAL), beta-D-glucan, and CT halo/infarct patterns — enable earlier diagnosis and preemptive therapy before culture or histology. [[PMID 26165502]] [[PMID 24994691]]
- Invasive aspergillosis increasingly affects non-neutropenic ICU patients (COPD on steroids, post-influenza, liver failure), challenging classical diagnostic frameworks built on neutropenia. [[PMID 28355466]]
- Voriconazole anchors therapy; echinocandins serve as first-line in selected candidiasis contexts and salvage in aspergillosis, with newer agents expanding the armamentarium. [[PMID 24994691]] [[PMID 26165502]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal thresholds for galactomannan and beta-D-glucan positivity vary by host and specimen.
- Whether preemptive (diagnostics-triggered) strategies outperform empiric antifungal therapy in outcomes is incompletely tested.

## 976 — Invasive Pulmonary Aspergillosis

topicId: `857fdab7-7441-4adc-8b0f-d6a0377964c5`

### Mentor intro
Invasive pulmonary aspergillosis is the clinical entity — fever, cough, pleuritic pain, and progressive infiltrates in an immunocompromised host — that the antifungal guidelines (topic 973) treat. Diagnosis is probabilistic, staged from possible to probable to proven on host factors, radiology, and mycology; the halo sign on early CT is the radiological signature, later giving way to the air-crescent of recovering lesions. Refractory disease — failure on adequate voriconazole — demands systematic reassessment: adherence and troughs first, resistance and imaging progression next, then salvage with a lipid amphotericin, isavuconazole, or combination approaches, with surgical resection considered for localised disease in fit patients.

### Teaching points
- IPA diagnosis is staged (possible/probable/proven) combining host factors, radiological features (halo sign, infarct-shaped consolidation), and mycological evidence (galactomannan, culture, BAL microscopy). [[PMID 38714471]] [[PMID 32034433]]
- Invasive aspergillosis increasingly involves non-fumigatus species with variable antifungal susceptibility; identification to species level and susceptibility testing inform management. [[PMID 38714471]]
- Refractory IPA management follows a sequence: confirm exposure (adherence, trough levels), assess resistance and progression, then salvage with alternative agents (liposomal amphotericin B, isavuconazole), combination therapy, or surgical resection for localised disease. [[PMID 38286175]]
- Severe respiratory infection in the immunocompromised demands systematic identification of the cause — invasive diagnostics (BAL with galactomannan, multiplex PCR) improve targeted therapy in hypoxaemic respiratory failure. [[PMID 32034433]]

### Uncertainties
- The contribution of surgical resection to outcomes in localised IPA is based on case series rather than trials.
- Optimal salvage-therapy sequencing for refractory disease is expert-guided.

## 977 — investigating suspected stable angina in women

topicId: `6b20bb5c-3328-4323-8263-7b099abed624`

### Mentor intro
Suspected stable angina in women runs on a different track: women present later, with atypical and microvascular-predominant disease, and carry a higher burden of angina with non-obstructive coronary arteries — ANOCA/INOCA affecting nearly half of women undergoing angiography for suspected ischemia. The 2021 AHA/ACC chest-pain guideline explicitly addresses sex-specific pathways: functional testing and CT-angiography selection perform differently by sex, and a negative anatomical test does not end the work-up when microvascular disease is plausible. The modern skill is resisting both errors: dismissing women's chest pain as non-cardiac, and stop investigating once the epicardial arteries look clean.

### Teaching points
- Women with suspected CAD present with atypical symptoms more often, develop more nonobstructive and microvascular disease, and are under-investigated relative to men — the 2021 AHA/ACC guideline codifies sex-specific diagnostic pathways. [[PMID 36921653]]
- ANOCA/INOCA — angina with non-obstructive coronary arteries — may affect nearly half of patients undergoing invasive angiography for suspected ischemia and encompasses coronary microvascular dysfunction and vasospasm. [[PMID 37704315]] [[PMID 36921653]]
- Diagnostic-test performance differs by sex; CTA subgroup analysis informs appropriate-use, and a clean epicardial angiogram should prompt functional/microvascular assessment (stress imaging, CFR/IMR where available) rather than reassurance alone. [[PMID 31189617]] [[PMID 37704315]]
- Investigating women requires the dual framework: exclude obstructive disease with appropriate testing, then actively pursue microvascular and vasospastic mechanisms when symptoms persist. [[PMID 37704315]] [[PMID 36921653]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal diagnostic thresholds and test-selection algorithms for women remain refined by ongoing sex-specific outcome data.
- Treatment of confirmed microvascular angina improves symptoms more consistently than outcomes.

## 978 — iron deficiency anaemia

topicId: `9e99370c-46b4-43cf-b749-9e9fd4c370ba`

### Mentor intro
Iron deficiency anaemia is a sign before it is a diagnosis: in men and postmenopausal women the mandate is finding the blood loss, which is gastrointestinal until proven otherwise. The BSG guideline structures the work-up — coeliac serology with the ferritin, faecal occult blood and endoscopic evaluation by risk, and small-bowel investigation when upper-and-lower endoscopy is negative — while ferritin thresholds (with transferrin saturation where inflammation blurs the picture) anchor the laboratory diagnosis. Treatment restores the deficit (oral first for most, intravenous when oral fails or urgency dictates — see topic 979) but never substitutes for finding the cause.

### Teaching points
- In adults without inflammation, ferritin below 45 ng/mL is diagnostic of iron deficiency; ferritin 46-99 ng/mL with transferrin saturation below 20% also confirms deficiency — inflammation raises ferritin and masks deficiency. [[PMID 41252836]]
- Men and postmenopausal women with IDA require gastrointestinal evaluation for occult blood loss; coeliac serology is part of the first-line work-up, and small-bowel investigation follows negative bidirectional endoscopy in ongoing-deficiency cases. [[PMID 34497146]] [[PMID 33362380]]
- Oral iron is first-line for most stable patients; intravenous iron is reserved for intolerance, malabsorption, ongoing losses exceeding oral absorption, or clinical urgency. [[PMID 34497146]] [[PMID 41252836]]
- Response is monitored by reticulocyte haemoglobin and haemoglobin rise at 2-4 weeks; failure to respond prompts re-evaluation of adherence, absorption, continued loss, and the original diagnosis. [[PMID 41252836]] [[PMID 34497146]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal small-bowel-imaging pathways (capsule versus MR-enterography versus device-assisted enteroscopy) follow local expertise.
- The threshold for IV-first strategies in non-urgent IDA is evolving (see topic 979).

### MCQ angles
- Ferritin/TSAT diagnostic thresholds and inflammation caveats.
- GI work-up sequence by sex and risk.
- Oral-versus-IV first-line selection.
- Response monitoring and non-response work-up.

### Case hooks
- A 65-year-old man has ferritin 30 ng/mL and microcytic anaemia; structure the cause-seeking work-up.
- An IDA patient's haemoglobin fails to rise after 4 weeks of oral iron; evaluate adherence, absorption, and continued loss.

### Gaps

## 979 — Iron deficiency anaemia: intravenous iron therapy

topicId: `9ff99ba7-404a-45b9-ba4e-f8524b0cfdb4`

### Mentor intro
Intravenous iron has moved from rescue to routine: modern formulations (ferric carboxymaltose, iron derisomaltose, ferumoxytol) deliver a gram of iron in 15-60 minutes with reaction rates that no longer justify inpatient observation. The indications are intolerance or non-response to oral iron, malabsorption, ongoing losses outpacing oral absorption, and urgency — but increasingly also convenience and guaranteed delivery. The distinctive modern safety signal is hypophosphatemia: FCM's FGF-23-mediated phosphate wasting is common, usually transient and subclinical, but symptomatic cases with osteomalacia risk warrant monitoring in high-risk and repeated-dose patients.

### Teaching points
- IV iron indications: oral intolerance or non-response, malabsorption (IBD, bariatric, coeliac), ongoing losses exceeding oral replacement, and clinical urgency; modern formulations allow rapid high-dose repletion in single or split doses. [[PMID 40603803]]
- Oral iron remains first-line for stable, tolerant patients; IV iron achieves faster and more complete repletion and is increasingly used first-line where speed, certainty, or tolerance favour it. [[PMID 40603803]]
- Ferric carboxymaltose is associated with hypophosphatemia through FGF-23-mediated renal phosphate wasting; systematic review quantifies the risk as frequent, usually transient, but occasionally symptomatic. [[PMID 39935027]] [[PMID 34534708]]
- Hypophosphatemia risk is higher with repeated doses, low baseline phosphate, and certain formulations; monitoring phosphate in at-risk patients and choosing lower-risk formulations (e.g. derisomaltose) are practical mitigations. [[PMID 34534708]] [[PMID 39935027]]

### Uncertainties
- Long-term clinical consequences of repeated FCM-associated hypophosphatemia (bone health) are incompletely defined.
- Optimal IV-first versus oral-first strategies by phenotype and health-system cost are evolving.

### MCQ angles
- IV-iron indications and modern formulations.
- Oral-versus-IV positioning.
- FCM hypophosphatemia mechanism and risk factors.
- Monitoring and formulation-selection mitigation.

### Case hooks
- An IBD patient with ongoing intestinal losses needs iron repletion; compare oral versus IV strategies and select a formulation.
- A patient receiving repeated FCM doses has fatigue and a low phosphate; recognise FCM-associated hypophosphatemia and adjust.

### Gaps
- The retained evidence is general-adult; condition-specific IV-iron evidence (heart failure, CKD) follows separate guidance — verify scope before approval.

### Sources

## 980 — Irradiated, Leukoreduced and Washed Blood Products

topicId: `7226395e-bc4e-4f0f-9577-0cb4b5d98ef0`

### Mentor intro
Blood-component modifications exist because transfusions can harm in specific hosts: irradiation prevents the rare and fatal transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease in recipients whose immune systems cannot clear donor lymphocytes — congenital immunodeficiency, haematologic malignancy, stem-cell transplant, directed donations from relatives, and HLA-matched platelets. Leukoreduction reduces febrile non-haemolytic reactions, CMV transmission risk, and HLA-alloimmunisation — near-universal in many systems. Washing removes plasma for the rare severe allergic or IgA-deficiency recipient. Each modification adds cost and delay, so the discipline is matching modification to indication, not applying them by default.

### Teaching points
- Irradiated cellular components prevent transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease (TA-GVHD), a rare but near-universally fatal complication in recipients who cannot reject donor lymphocytes. [[PMID 25931584]] [[PMID 37753040]]
- Irradiation indications include congenital cellular immunodeficiency, haematologic malignancy and its treatment, haematopoietic stem-cell transplantation, directed donations from blood relatives, and HLA-matched platelets. [[PMID 37753040]] [[PMID 27113002]]
- Leukoreduction reduces febrile non-haemolytic transfusion reactions, CMV transmission risk, and HLA-alloimmunisation; many jurisdictions apply it universally while others target by indication. [[PMID 27113002]]
- Washed components (removing plasma) are reserved for severe allergic transfusion reactions and IgA-deficiency with anti-IgA — the modification must match a defined indication because each adds cost and delay. [[PMID 27113002]] [[PMID 25931584]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether pathogen-reduction technology will consolidate or replace component-modification strategies is system-dependent.
- The precise threshold of immunosuppression warranting irradiation in non-classical indications follows expert consensus.
