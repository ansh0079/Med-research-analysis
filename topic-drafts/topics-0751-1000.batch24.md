## 981 — irritable bowel syndrome

topicId: `91ee83bd-9f84-44a5-9412-aedb0163cb79`

### Mentor intro
Irritable bowel syndrome affects roughly 4-10% of the population (estimates range up to 21% depending on criteria) and is defined by abdominal pain associated with altered bowel habits rather than being a diagnosis of exclusion. Modern management is positive and subtype-directed: classify as IBS-C, IBS-D, or mixed, then step through diet and lifestyle measures, fibre and probiotics, targeted pharmacotherapy by subtype, and gut-directed psychological therapy for refractory symptoms. This page anchors the general strategy; the paired diet/microbiome topic (982) details dietary intervention.

### Teaching points
- IBS is a positive clinical diagnosis based on symptom criteria (Rome IV): recurrent abdominal pain related to defecation with altered stool frequency or form; subtypes are IBS-C, IBS-D, and mixed. [[PMID 40789179]] [[PMID 25734736]]
- Probiotics may benefit some IBS patients, but heterogeneity of species, strains, and endpoints has prevented specific strain-level recommendations. [[PMID 37541528]]
- Dietary therapy - soluble fibre and fermentable-carbohydrate restriction - is a first-line treatment pillar alongside lifestyle advice, chosen by subtype. [[PMID 35419092]]
- IBS substantially reduces quality of life and work productivity; practical stepwise treatment recommendations exist for generalists and specialists. [[PMID 25734736]] [[PMID 40789179]]

### Uncertainties
- Which specific probiotic strains (if any) have reproducible efficacy in IBS subtypes remains unresolved.
- The durability of dietary restriction beyond short symptom-control periods is uncertain.

### MCQ angles
- Rome IV diagnostic criteria and subtype classification.
- Evidence limits of probiotics and why no specific strain is recommended.
- Stepwise management ladder by IBS subtype.
- Red flags that should divert the work-up away from an IBS label.

### Case hooks
- A 28-year-old woman has 6 months of bloating and pain relieved by defecation with alternating stool habits; structure the positive diagnosis and subtype.
- An IBS-D patient asks whether a probiotic will cure symptoms; frame an honest evidence answer and subtype-appropriate alternatives.

### Gaps
- Diet/fibers evidence retained is a narrative review; the dietary-interventions network meta-analysis (40258374) was assigned to topic 982 for scope differentiation.

## 982 — Irritable bowel syndrome diet and microbiome-directed therapy

topicId: `616e8a96-a634-4323-9b85-d14b2152cc86`

### Mentor intro
Diet is the most requested and most misunderstood lever in IBS. The low-FODMAP diet - restricting fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides, monosaccharides, and polyols - is now an internationally accepted strategy: MRI studies show short-chain fermentable carbohydrates expand small-bowel water and colonic gas, provoking symptoms in patients with visceral hypersensitivity. The diet works through a three-phase restriction-reintroduction-personalisation structure under dietetic supervision. Microbiome-directed therapy (probiotics, and emerging prebiotic/psychobiotic approaches) rests on the reframing of IBS as a disturbance of the microbiota-gut-brain axis, though long-term, large randomised evidence remains thin.

### Teaching points
- MRI studies demonstrate that short-chain fermentable carbohydrates increase small-intestinal water and colonic gas, producing symptoms in patients with visceral hypersensitivity - the mechanistic basis of the low-FODMAP diet. [[PMID 28592442]]
- The low-FODMAP diet is an internationally accepted dietary management strategy for IBS with demonstrated symptom efficacy in randomised trials. [[PMID 38337655]] [[PMID 28244669]]
- Implementation follows three phases: short restriction, structured reintroduction to identify triggers, then personalisation for long-term maintenance - ideally under dietetic guidance. [[PMID 28244669]]
- IBS is increasingly conceptualised as a disturbance of the microbiota-gut-brain axis, providing the rationale for microbiome-directed (probiotic and psychobiotic) therapies. [[PMID 35125827]]
- A network meta-analysis compares the relative efficacy of available dietary interventions for IBS, informing diet selection when multiple options are proposed. [[PMID 40258374]]

### Uncertainties
- Long-term, large randomised controlled trials of the low-FODMAP diet are still lacking, and effects on microbiome composition versus symptoms need disentangling.
- Which microbiome-directed interventions (which strains, which targets) confer durable benefit is unresolved.

### MCQ angles
- FODMAP acronym and the fermentable carbohydrates involved.
- Three-phase low-FODMAP implementation and reintroduction logic.
- Mechanistic evidence (luminal distension, visceral hypersensitivity) behind dietary therapy.
- Microbiota-gut-brain axis as rationale for microbiome-directed therapy.

### Case hooks
- An IBS-C patient asks for dietary options before medication; outline the dietitian-supervised low-FODMAP pathway and alternatives.
- A patient restricted FODMAPs for a year and never reintroduced; correct the phase structure and micronutrient risks.

### Gaps
- Evidence base for long-term dietary and microbiome-directed therapy remains limited; statement-level citations come from reviews and one network meta-analysis rather than large durable-outcome trials.

## 983 — ischaemic heart disease

topicId: `a17809cc-5f60-4bfa-837b-cb36aceb9a02`

### Mentor intro
Ischaemic heart disease spans stable chronic coronary disease through acute occlusive events. The 2023 AHA/ACC chronic coronary disease guideline consolidated a decade of evidence since the 2012 stable ischaemic heart disease guideline: guideline-directed medical therapy for secondary prevention, symptom-directed antianginal therapy, and selective revascularisation. At the acute extreme, STEMI is complete coronary occlusion and a time-critical emergency. Antiplatelet choice for long-term secondary prevention is an active question - an individual patient-level meta-analysis now directly compares clopidogrel with aspirin monotherapy in established coronary disease.

### Teaching points
- The 2023 AHA/ACC chronic coronary disease guideline updates and consolidates evidence for managing stable ischaemic heart disease, replacing the 2012 stable IHD guideline framework. [[PMID 37471501]]
- STEMI represents complete coronary artery occlusion leading to myocardial ischaemia and necrosis; it remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality and demands rapid reperfusion. [[PMID 37904413]]
- An individual patient-level meta-analysis compares clopidogrel versus aspirin monotherapy for secondary prevention in established coronary disease, most of whom had prior PCI or ACS. [[PMID 40902613]]
- Chronic coronary disease management rests on secondary-prevention risk-factor control alongside symptom-directed antianginal therapy, with revascularisation reserved for prognostic or refractory-symptom indications. [[PMID 37471501]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal long-term antiplatelet agent and intensity after PCI or ACS is still being refined (clopidogrel vs aspirin head-to-head evidence is young).
- Which stable patients benefit prognostically from revascularisation beyond left-main/proximal-LAD anatomy.

### MCQ angles

- Antiplatelet selection for secondary prevention after PCI/ACS.
- Indications for revascularisation in stable disease.

### Case hooks

### Gaps

## 985 — Ischaemic optic neuropathy: arteritic (GCA) vs non-arteritic (NAION) differentiation

topicId: `ce12718d-6010-4956-9acb-e486373a0067`

### Mentor intro
Anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy is a leading cause of sudden, painless monocular vision loss after age 50, and the first clinical task is separating its two forms because everything downstream differs. The arteritic form, usually driven by giant cell arteritis, is a medical and ophthalmological emergency: corticosteroids must start immediately to protect the fellow eye, and GCA may present with minimal systemic features. The non-arteritic form (NAION) - associated with vascular risk factors, often a small cup-to-disc ratio - has no proven acute treatment, so management is risk-factor control. Posterior forms (PION) complicate the picture and require the same urgent discrimination.

### Teaching points
- Two forms of AION carry clearly different clinical, diagnostic, and therapeutic implications: arteritic (usually GCA, an emergency) versus non-arteritic; posterior forms (PION) also exist and complicate diagnosis. [[PMID 31952875]] [[PMID 42710617]]
- Suspected arteritic AION demands immediate corticosteroid therapy because the fellow eye is threatened; GCA may present with few or no systemic features, so inflammatory markers and temporal artery assessment support - not replace - clinical judgement. [[PMID 33892603]] [[PMID 42710617]]
- AAION is usually due to giant cell arteritis; NAION, by contrast, is associated with vascular risk factors and small optic discs, and has no proven acute therapy. [[PMID 31952875]] [[PMID 33892603]]
- Ischemic optic neuropathies are among the leading causes of severe visual loss over age 50 and are clinically, aetiologically, and therapeutically distinct entities - anatomical (anterior vs posterior) and aetiological (arteritic vs non-arteritic) classification both matter. [[PMID 31952875]]

### Uncertainties
- No acute therapy has proven efficacy for NAION; the role of aspirin remains debated.
- Optimal biopsy timing and steroid-taper strategy after visual-stabilisation in GCA-associated AAION.

### MCQ angles
- Differentiating arteritic from non-arteritic AION at the bedside (disc appearance, age, systemic features).
- Immediate management steps when GCA is suspected, and protecting the fellow eye.
- Anatomical classification: AION versus PION.
- What can and cannot be offered for NAION.

### Case hooks
- A 72-year-old reports sudden painless visual loss with jaw claudication; sequence steroids, inflammatory markers, and biopsy without delaying treatment.
- A 55-year-old hypertensive has painless morning visual loss with a crowded disc; explain NAION prognosis and risk-factor management.

### Gaps
- Evidence base is narrative reviews and a position paper; no recent randomised treatment evidence for either form is retrievable under the pipeline filter.

### Sources
- PMID 31952875 — Acute ischemic optic nerve disease: Pathophysiology, clinical features and management.
- PMID 33892603 — Arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy: An update.
- PMID 42710617 — Joint position paper on anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (AION). Vascular Risk Group and Systemic Autoimmune Disease Group.

## 986 — ischaemic stroke

topicId: `414e27f8-c92c-4204-bab6-003ad2b0fe8d`

### Mentor intro
Acute ischaemic stroke - vascular occlusion causing cerebral infarction - is the fifth most common cause of death in the United States, with incidence projected to rise. Its management has been recast by reperfusion: intravenous thrombolysis, now shifting from alteplase to tenecteplase, plus mechanical thrombectomy for large-vessel occlusion have expanded the treatment window to 24 hours in selected patients. The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline replaces the 2018 guideline and its 2019 update with a comprehensive prehospital-to-discharge framework. Everything in the pathway keys off one datum: time since last-known-well.

### Teaching points
- The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline for early management of acute ischaemic stroke replaces the 2018 guideline and 2019 update, providing comprehensive evidence-based recommendations from prehospital care onward. [[PMID 41582814]]
- Timely reperfusion is the cornerstone of stroke care and requires early symptom recognition by the public and first responders, triage to an appropriate stroke centre, and efficient assessment. [[PMID 32507747]]
- Intravenous thrombolysis (first alteplase, now increasingly tenecteplase) remains a cornerstone of acute management; in large-vessel occlusion, endovascular thrombectomy added to IV thrombolysis has expanded the therapeutic window to 24 hours. [[PMID 39496213]]
- Treatment decisions require establishing a high probability of ischaemic stroke and confirming the time since last-known-well, which anchors eligibility for both thrombolysis and thrombectomy. [[PMID 39496213]]

### Uncertainties
- Comparative effectiveness of tenecteplase versus alteplase is being refined by ongoing trials.
- Patient-selection criteria for thrombectomy in the extended (up to 24-hour) window continue to evolve.

### MCQ angles

- The decisive role of last-known-well time in eligibility decisions.
- Prehospital recognition and stroke-centre triage.

### Case hooks

### Gaps

## 987 — Ivabradine in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction

topicId: `a38355d5-2072-4124-bd75-ccbaf166604a`

### Mentor intro
Ivabradine is the selective funny-current (If) blocker that reduces heart rate without touching contractility or vascular tone - a pharmacological niche in HFrEF. It was tested in the landmark SHIFT trial in stable chronic heart failure, low ejection fraction, sinus rhythm, on contemporary background therapy. Its licensed niche is precise: sinus rhythm, resting heart rate at or above threshold, despite maximally tolerated beta-blockade - or when beta-blockers cannot be used at all. Meta-analyses now quantify its clinical and haemodynamic effects on top of, and apart from, beta-blockade.

### Teaching points
- Ivabradine selectively blocks funny-current (If) channels in sinoatrial node cells, producing pure heart-rate reduction with no direct effect on contractility or vessels. [[PMID 27752846]]
- Ivabradine is indicated for HFrEF patients in sinus rhythm with heart rate >=70 bpm (licensing varies by region) on a maximum tolerated beta-blocker dose, or when beta-blockers are contraindicated. [[PMID 38729854]] [[PMID 27752846]]
- Ivabradine was tested in a large outcome trial (SHIFT) in stable chronic heart failure with low ejection fraction and sinus rhythm on contemporary background therapy. [[PMID 27752846]]
- A systematic review and meta-analysis examined ivabradine's additional effect, apart from beta-blockers, on cardiovascular death, all-cause mortality, and hospitalisation in HFrEF. [[PMID 30173307]]

### Uncertainties
- Whether ivabradine's benefit is fully independent of beta-blockade, and its net effect when added to contemporary quadruple therapy.
- The balance of haemodynamic versus hard clinical benefit in real-world populations outside trial enrolment criteria.

## 988 — JAK inhibitors in RA: tofacitinib, baricitinib, upadacitinib: efficacy and cardiovascular safety

topicId: `5827fc33-2ace-49b5-8941-9ef314a6bed3`

### Mentor intro
The JAK inhibitors transformed RA treatment with oral, rapid, targeted efficacy - and then the class acquired a safety shadow. After the regulatory wake-up call from the tofacitinib cardiovascular outcomes programme, prescribing is now risk-stratified: older patients with cardiovascular risk factors need particular counselling before a JAK inhibitor, and TNF inhibitors remain the usual comparator benchmark. This page focuses on RA-specific evidence: meta-analyses of randomised trials quantifying cardiovascular event risk, network meta-analyses comparing class safety, and the upadacitinib-specific cardiovascular data.

### Teaching points
- A systematic review of phase 2-4 randomised trials compares major adverse cardiovascular events and all-cause death for JAK inhibitors or tocilizumab versus TNF inhibitors in RA - the head-to-head safety evidence regulators now expect. [[PMID 39383617]]
- A network meta-analysis meticulously assesses and compares the cardiovascular safety of JAK inhibitors in RA, supporting class-level risk comparison across agents. [[PMID 37614310]]
- For upadacitinib specifically, a systematic review and meta-analysis of cardiovascular adverse events found current evidence does not provide a definitive conclusion - counsel with calibrated uncertainty. [[PMID 39513305]]
- Meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials found the overall impact of JAK inhibitors on cardiovascular event risk in RA requires subgroup interpretation, particularly in older patients with cardiovascular risk factors. [[PMID 31088790]]

### Uncertainties
- Absolute cardiovascular risk of individual JAK inhibitors in older RA patients with baseline risk factors.
- Whether safety differences exist between JAK1-selective and pan-JAK agents at the class level.

### MCQ angles

- When to prefer a TNF inhibitor over a JAK inhibitor on safety grounds.

### Gaps

## 989 — JAK Inhibitors in Rheumatology

topicId: `f1ef4acc-bffd-4240-9409-29778761bc8f`

### Mentor intro
JAK-STAT signalling sits downstream of the cytokine receptors that drive rheumatoid arthritis, which is why JAK inhibitors work - and why their biology spans rheumatic disease more broadly. The 2023 EULAR RA recommendations position these agents within treat-to-target sequencing, after conventional synthetic DMARDs and usually after or instead of a TNF inhibitor, with risk-factor caveats inherited from the class's cardiovascular surveillance. Upadacitinib, the JAK1-selective exemplar, is approved across rheumatic, dermatologic, and gastrointestinal indications, giving rheumatologists a cross-specialty familiarity with its mechanism and trial programme.

### Teaching points
- The 2023 EULAR update on synthetic and biological DMARDs for RA provides the current recommendations on where JAK inhibitors sit in the treatment sequence, based on systematic literature research and international task-force consensus. [[PMID 41826212]]
- JAK-STAT signalling contributes to RA pathogenesis through cytokine-receptor pathways, providing the mechanistic rationale for JAK inhibition as targeted oral therapy. [[PMID 38240831]] [[PMID 39125897]]
- Upadacitinib is a selective JAK1 inhibitor approved by the FDA, EMA, and other agencies for chronic inflammatory diseases spanning rheumatic, dermatologic, and gastrointestinal conditions. [[PMID 37984057]]
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised trials characterises upadacitinib's efficacy and safety across immune-mediated inflammatory diseases, informing its benefit-risk profile. [[PMID 40666511]]

### Uncertainties
- Long-term class safety of JAK inhibitors relative to established biologic DMARDs beyond cardiovascular endpoints (malignancy, serious infection).
- Optimal positioning of JAK1-selective agents versus IL-6 pathway blockade after TNF-inhibitor failure.

### MCQ angles

## 990 — Juvenile idiopathic arthritis biologic strategy

topicId: `ff84d9e4-835e-4dd7-a0e4-554b9910c71b`

### Mentor intro
Juvenile idiopathic arthritis management aims for remission - control of articular and systemic inflammation before joint damage produces disability and growth restriction. Methotrexate remains the conventional anchor, but the biologic era has transformed the escalation ladder: TNF inhibitors (etanercept, adalimumab), abatacept, tocilizumab, and IL-1 blockade each answer a different phenotype. Comparative-effectiveness evidence - including health-technology evaluation of abatacept, adalimumab, etanercept, and tocilizumab - supports choosing among them, while the infection signal associated with biologics demands disciplined screening and vigilance in a population where the disease itself already raises bacterial-infection risk.

### Teaching points
- JIA management combines pharmacological, physical, and psychosocial therapies with the goal of inducing remission by controlling articular and systemic inflammation; biologics now anchor escalation beyond methotrexate. [[PMID 32116038]]
- Clinical-effectiveness and cost-effectiveness evidence directly compares abatacept, adalimumab, etanercept, and tocilizumab for JIA, informing biologic selection. [[PMID 27135404]]
- Biologic-associated infection is the principal safety concern in paediatric rheumatology; the bacterial-infection risk appears increased in JIA patients from the disease itself, compounding agent-related immunosuppression. [[PMID 26385753]]
- Biologic choice is phenotype-driven: TNF inhibitors for polyarticular disease, IL-1/IL-6 pathways for systemic phenotypes - matching mechanism to presentation. [[PMID 32116038]] [[PMID 27135404]]

### Uncertainties
- Optimal biologic sequencing after first-agent failure and how to choose the second biologic.
- Long-term safety and growth effects of early biologic exposure; cost-effectiveness thresholds vary by health system.

### MCQ angles
- Biologic classes and their pathways (TNF, CTLA4-Ig, IL-6R, IL-1).
- Phenotype-driven biologic selection in JIA subtypes.
- Pre-biologic infection screening and vaccination review.
- Treat-to-target and remission goals specific to paediatric populations.
